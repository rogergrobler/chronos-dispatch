South Africa · Banking · Technology Economics Analysis Chart of the day
Absa Group's interim results for the six months to 30 June contain a contradiction the bank states plainly rather than hides. Digitally active customers grew 14% — and the cost-to-income ratio edged up. Total IT spend, including staff, amortisation and depreciation, rose 7% to R8.8bn (the technology cost line is R8.78bn, $538.7m), which is roughly 28% of the group's R31.4bn ($1.9bn) operating expense base. On top of that, Absa impaired a further R200m ($12.3m) of software assets in the half, following a R2.4bn ($147.2m) write-down in 2025. The bank's own language on the impairment is unusually direct: "the Group impaired certain software assets for which the value in use is determined to be zero, mainly derived from head office," and the causes given are "changes in the Group's strategy, regulatory developments and the pace of technological change." The rest of the result is solid: total income R58.79bn ($3.61bn), up 4.1%; headline earnings per share up 7.9%; ROE 15%, from 14.8%; gross loans +5%, deposits and debt funding +8%; interim dividend 850c, up 8.3%. The physical estate has not gone anywhere either — 13.4m customers across 17 countries, 1,043 outlets, 6,212 ATMs, 37,030 employees.

Absa moved 14% more customers onto digital and its cost ratio went up — R8.78bn of technology, 28% of the cost base, and another R200m written to zero

TechCabal · Banking · Phathisani Moyo · Tue 18 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a decent set of interim numbers with a large IT bill attached. The one that matters is a large African bank publishing, in its own words, that a material part of what it capitalised as technology has a value in use of zero — and doing so twice in eighteen months. The digital-transformation case has always rested on an implied bargain: spend now on the stack, harvest later as the branch network shrinks. Absa has run that experiment at scale for long enough to report the interim scoreboard, and the honest version is that adoption rose, spend rose, write-downs recurred, and the ratio did not improve. The write-down reason is the part to underline — not fraud, not failure, but the pace of technological change, which is a bank saying its software aged faster than its amortisation schedule assumed.

The digital dividend has not arrived at the ratio — and the stack is being written off faster than it amortises
TechCabal — “Absa's digital push comes with a $540 million price tag”

The structural read is that this is the clearest public evidence yet that digital migration at an incumbent African bank is a cost substitution rather than a cost reduction, and that the substitution is currently running at a loss. The theory of the case was always that the marginal cost of serving a digital customer approaches zero, so as the mix shifts the ratio falls. What the theory omits is that the fixed cost does not shift with it. Absa still runs 1,043 outlets and 6,212 ATMs because the customers who use them have not left, and it has now added a software, cloud, cybersecurity, data and engineering-talent base on top. Two cost structures, one customer base, and a cost-to-income ratio that reflects exactly that. The subtler and more important number is the impairment. R2.4bn last year and R200m this half is not a rounding error; it is capitalised software whose value in use the auditors have agreed is zero, and the bank attributes it to the pace of technological change. Read that as an admission with a general application: in a period when the tooling is being rebuilt every eighteen months, capitalising and amortising software over a multi-year life assumes a stability that no longer exists. The write-down is not a failure of this project. It is the accounting catching up with a shortened useful life.

A bank that capitalises software over five years in a market that replaces its stack every two is not investing. It is pre-booking an impairment and calling it an asset.

The threads: to Issue 066's item 2 (AfricaNenda, and the 8.78% consumed in the settlement layer) — the arithmetic rhyme is a coincidence but the lesson is not, in that owning the rail across 17 countries does not exempt you from the cost of running it. To Issue 065's item 1 (Standard Bank wanting to own more of Africa's digital payment flows, holding 30% of SA cross-border value) — read alongside this, the ambition acquires a price tag; Standard Bank is proposing to own more of exactly the layer that has just cost its closest peer 28% of a cost base. To Issue 062's item on Absa Kenya automating 71% of processes after a $31m technology spend — that was the same strategy reported at subsidiary scale and framed as an efficiency win; this is the group-level consolidation of the same spend, and the group number tells a less flattering story than the country number did, which is itself a lesson in where to look. To Issue 066's item 3 (Spar's franchise model, and ownership you gave away) — the mirror case, where Absa owns everything and is discovering that ownership has a running cost. To the Chronos read on portfolio technology budgets — the transferable diligence question is not "what is the technology spend" but "what is the assumed useful life of capitalised software, and when was it last tested against the replacement cycle actually observed"; on Absa's own evidence the honest answer at most institutions is a number set years ago and never revisited. The sceptic's ledger: a cost-to-income ratio that "edged up" during a period of 4.1% income growth and 7% IT growth is close to arithmetic inevitability and does not by itself indict the strategy; ROE improved and HEPS rose 7.9%, so the shareholder outcome moved the right way regardless of the ratio; the R200m impairment is explicitly attributed to head office and to strategy changes, which may mean a discarded project rather than a systemic mispricing of software life; 28% of opex on technology is high for an African bank but not out of line with global peers running comparable multi-country estates; and a single half is far too short a window to declare that the digital dividend will not arrive — the branch estate can only shrink with a lag measured in years, and Absa has not yet begun to shrink it. The forward marker: whether the FY26 result shows outlet or headcount reduction alongside the technology spend. If the physical estate is still 1,043 outlets and 37,030 people at year end, the substitution has not started; it has only been paid for.

Nigeria · Payments · Channel Economics Analysis Chart of the day
Central Bank of Nigeria Q1 2026 statistics, released Friday, show a reversal in the cash-access market that took three years to build. ATM transaction value rose 64.6% year-on-year to ₦26.3 trillion ($19.4bn) on 438.6m transactions (volume up a comparatively modest 6.6%) — roughly $215m a day. Over the same quarter, PoS transaction volume fell 19.9% to 2.92 billion and value fell 16.4% to ₦59.3 trillion ($43.7bn). The context is that PoS agents became Nigeria's cash network by default: active ATMs had fallen from about 22,600 in 2021 to 16,714 by mid-2024 through underinvestment and empty cash trays, and the 2023 currency-redesign cash crunch pushed millions onto agents, with terminals rising from 2.32m at the crisis peak to 8.36m registered (5.90m active) by March 2025. Nigeria now has roughly one PoS terminal per 28 people against 13 ATMs per 100,000 adults. What changed is regulation on both sides at once. Against agents: October 2025 guidelines restricting each agent to a single terminal provider, a 70-metre operating radius, and a ₦1.2m ($884) daily cumulative cap. For ATMs: a February 2025 cap on withdrawal fees at ₦500, cash-availability requirements, a 24–48 hour failed-transaction resolution rule, and a March 2026 mandate that card issuers deploy at least one ATM per 7,500 cards over three years.

Nigeria's banks let the ATM network rot, agents took the cash business, and the central bank has now legislated the channel back — ATM value up 64.6%, PoS volume down 19.9%

TechCabal · Banking · Temitayo Jaiyeola · Mon 17 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a quarterly statistics release showing a channel shift. The one that matters is a clean natural experiment in how fast a regulator can reverse a distribution outcome that the market took three years to produce — and the answer is about four quarters. The agent network was not built by superior economics; it was built in the vacuum left by banks that stopped maintaining their machines, and it has been unwound not by banks competing better but by a central bank simultaneously capping what agents may do and mandating what banks must deploy. Note the shape of the numbers, which is the genuinely interesting part: ATM volume rose only 6.6% while ATM value rose 64.6%. That is not more people using ATMs. It is the same people withdrawing much larger amounts, once per trip, to avoid the fee.

Value up 64.6%, volume up 6.6% — not more trips to the machine, just much bigger ones
TechCabal — “Nigerians are returning to ATMs as PoS transactions fall 20%”

The structural read is about where a distribution moat actually comes from. For three years the standard account of Moniepoint, OPay and the agent networks was that they had built something banks could not: millions of neighbourhood touchpoints, deep last-mile density, a genuine physical moat in a country where the formal network had collapsed. The Q1 numbers suggest a less comfortable reading. The agent network's decisive advantage was never density in the abstract; it was density relative to a bank channel that had been allowed to rot. That is a moat built on somebody else's neglect, and neglect is reversible. When the CBN mandated one ATM per 7,500 cards, capped the withdrawal fee at ₦500 and forced 24-to-48-hour resolution of failed transactions, it did not out-compete the agents. It removed the conditions that created them. Meanwhile the caps running the other way — single provider, 70-metre radius, ₦1.2m daily ceiling — attack the specific operational flexibility that let the network scale so fast. The pincer is deliberate and it worked inside a year. The corollary for anyone underwriting agent-network economics anywhere on the continent: distinguish carefully between a moat you built and a gap you occupied, because only one of them survives a regulator deciding to close it.

The agents did not beat the banks. They occupied the space the banks abandoned, and a central bank has now ordered the banks to come back and reclaim it. A moat made of someone else's neglect drains the moment they start maintaining the ditch.

The threads: to Issue 066's item 3 (Spar's franchise model, ownership distributed and then unrecoverable) — the same structure at national scale, except Nigeria had a central bank able to legislate the reversal that Spar's board cannot; the lesson is that whether outsourced distribution is recoverable depends entirely on whether someone with authority wants it back. To Issue 066's item 5 (ICASA ruling that unused data belongs to whoever paid for it) — a second African regulator this week resetting the economics of a channel by decree rather than by competition, which is worth noting as a pattern: on this continent, in these sectors, the regulator is frequently the most consequential competitor in the market. To Issue 065's item 1 (Standard Bank's payment-flow ambitions) and item 3 (the debt share of July's funding) — both assume that the fintech distribution layer is where value accrues; this is the first hard data suggesting the incumbent channel can be restored by policy. To Issue 063's item on the CBN rewriting fintech growth rules — that was the announcement, this is the measured effect one and a half quarters later, and the effect is larger and faster than the announcement implied. To the Chronos read on African financial services and on Optasia's adjacency — the diligence question that transfers is whether a distribution advantage is structural or occupies a regulatory vacuum, and specifically whether the counterfactual channel is dormant or dead. The sceptic's ledger: one quarter is one quarter, and Q1 in Nigeria carries seasonal effects that a year-on-year comparison only partly removes; the ATM value surge with near-flat volume is substantially a fee-avoidance and inflation artefact rather than genuine channel migration, since ₦26.3tn in nominal naira against a period of significant currency depreciation overstates the real shift considerably; PoS terminals still handle far more transactions in absolute terms — 2.92 billion against 438.6 million — and do a great deal beyond cash withdrawal, so calling this a comeback flatters the ATM; the agent network retains a genuine proximity advantage that 16,714 machines cannot replicate for a population of 237 million; and the mandate of one ATM per 7,500 cards runs over three years, so most of the deployment it requires has not happened yet and cannot explain the Q1 numbers. The forward marker: whether PoS volume declines again in Q2 and Q3. One quarter is a data point; three consecutive is a channel shift, and it would put a visible dent in the revenue assumptions underpinning the agent-network valuations.

Nigeria · Defence Technology · Funding Briefing Thesis test
Terra Industries, founded in 2024 by Nathan Nwachuku and Maxwell Maduka, has raised a further $18m, closing what is now Africa's largest seed round at $52m and making it the seventh most-funded African startup of 2026. It is the third raise this year: $11.75m in January, $22m in February led by Lux Capital, and now this. New investors are Norleo Space Investments and angel Grant Gordon, alongside existing backers 8VC, Silent Ventures, Nova Global, Belief Capital and SV Angel. The company builds autonomous security systems — long- and mid-range drones, interceptor drones, sentry towers and unmanned ground vehicles — connected through ArtemisOS, its proprietary platform for real-time threat detection, autonomous mission planning and coordinated response. It says its systems already protect nationally critical assets worth approximately $11bn across several African countries. Manufacturing is deliberately on-continent: Pax-1, a 15,000 sq ft facility in Abuja, and Pax-2 in Ghana at 34,000 sq ft, due to open Q4 2026 and reach 50,000 units a year by 2028. There is a February MoU with Nigeria's DICON for a local-production joint venture. The new capital funds a first international office in London, more manufacturing capacity, deployment across the Global South, and engineering and business-development hiring, with expansion planned into the Gulf, South America and South Asia. Nwachuku's framing: "Critical infrastructure across the Global South is best protected by systems designed for these environments and built in the regions they protect... It also puts us in the rooms where global defense decisions are made."

The structural read is that the word "seed" has stopped carrying information. A company founded in 2024 that has raised three times in eight months, operates two factories on two continents, holds a defence-ministry joint-venture MoU and is opening a London office is not at seed stage in any operational sense; it is a Series B business whose funding rounds have been labelled by sequence rather than by maturity. The same logic that made Airtel rent a foreign constellation in Issue 066 works in reverse here, and both are correct for their own buyer.

Egypt · Banking · Privatisation Briefing Deal structure
Egypt is preparing to float part of Banque du Caire, one of its oldest and largest state-backed banks, on the Egyptian Exchange in November 2026 — and two development finance institutions are circling the anchor slot. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development and the International Finance Corporation are reported by local outlet EnterpriseAM to be eyeing a combined 10% stake, split roughly evenly, with EBRD taking up to 5%. The timing has slipped deliberately: the government was considering selling up to 49% as of April, and the banks running the deal are understood to have asked for more time to widen the investor pool, with the roadshow restarting in September or October ahead of a November listing. The transaction sits inside a broader programme — Egypt is targeting $3–4bn from IPOs and stake sales by the end of 2026, having sold 51% of Arab Investment Bank in 2021 (its first bank privatisation in over a decade), listed United Bank in October 2024, and put six state-owned companies onto the exchange in April 2026. The stated objectives are familiar: raise money, attract hard currency, deepen the local market, and reduce the state's role in institutions it has historically controlled.

Cairo recruits the EBRD and the IFC to underwrite belief in a state bank before the public is asked to price it

TechCabal Daily · Banking · Muktar Oladunmade, Yemi Kareem & Zia Yusuf · Tue 18 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is an emerging-market privatisation with a November date on it. The one that matters is the mechanics of how state assets get sold when the state is the credibility problem. Nobody needs the EBRD's or the IFC's money — a combined 10% is not the capital that makes this deal work. What they supply is diligence that a domestic retail buyer cannot perform and would not trust the seller to perform for them: two institutions with no political stake in Egypt's fiscal position, statutory governance conditions, and a public reputation that would be damaged by anchoring a bad book. The stake is small on purpose. It is a signal, priced as an investment, and it is the cheapest instrument available to a government whose own assurances about its own bank are worth nothing to the market.

TechCabal Daily — “EBRD and IFC want a slice of Egypt's Banque du Caire”

The structural read is that DFI anchoring is one of the more underrated instruments in emerging-market deal design, and it is worth understanding precisely because it generalises well beyond Cairo. The problem in any state-asset sale is asymmetric: the seller knows the loan book, the buyer does not, and the seller is a government that will still be regulating the bank the morning after the listing. Ordinary remedies — audited accounts, a prospectus, an underwriter's comfort letter — all rely on parties the state can influence. A DFI cannot be influenced in the same way, has its own credit committee, imposes governance and disclosure conditions as a condition of participation, and crucially stays on the register. That last part carries most of the signal. An underwriter takes a fee and leaves; the IFC holds the paper and therefore holds the same downside as the retail buyer who follows it in. The size being small is the point rather than a limitation, because the signal comes from the willingness to hold at all, not the quantum. Note too what the delay tells you: the deal was contemplated at up to 49% in April and is now a November listing with a restarted roadshow, which reads less as fiscal urgency and more as a seller who would rather widen the book than clear it cheaply. That is a discipline emerging-market privatisations frequently lack.

The EBRD and the IFC are not buying 10% of a bank. They are selling the other 90% on the government's behalf, and the fee is that they have to hold the risk of being wrong.

The threads: to Issue 066's item 5 (ICASA settling who owns unused data) — both are cases where the ownership question in a market is resolved by an institution that is neither buyer nor seller, and both suggest that in these jurisdictions the decisive actor is often the one with no position. To Issue 065's item 2 and Issue 066's thread on Egypt's e-finance (the $96m Tamweely acquisition, and the closed-loop financial ecosystem being assembled) — the same market from the other side: private consolidation buying the credit layer while the state divests the deposit franchise, and it is worth watching whether those two flows eventually meet on the same register. To Issue 059 and Issue 060 (Egyptian funds abandoning mark-ups for DPI, A15 at 10x) — Egypt's capital markets are producing genuine liquidity events at a rate the rest of the continent is not, and the EGX listing pipeline is a large part of why. To the Chronos read on exit routes — the transferable technique is that a small DFI participation can be engineered into a transaction specifically to de-risk the perception of a seller rather than the economics of the asset, which is a cheaper solution to a credibility problem than a discount is. The sceptic's ledger: this is a single unconfirmed report from one local outlet, EnterpriseAM, describing intentions rather than signed commitments — neither the EBRD nor the IFC has confirmed anything, and DFI credit committees decline deals routinely and late; a November listing that has already slipped once from an April framing may slip again, and Egyptian IPO timetables have a poor record; the drop from a contemplated 49% to a 10% DFI slice plus an unspecified public tranche means the actual free float is unknown, and a thin float would undermine the market-deepening rationale entirely; DFI anchoring also imports conditionality that can constrain a bank's commercial freedom in ways minority public holders may not want; and the broader $3–4bn privatisation target has been restated across several years without being met. The forward marker: whether the September or October roadshow actually happens on schedule, and whether either institution converts interest into a signed cornerstone commitment before the book opens. Interest reported by a local outlet in August is not an anchor investor in November.

Global · AI Infrastructure · Capital Markets Analysis Thesis test
Simon Taylor unpacks the structure behind Nvidia's agreement with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to build financing platforms mobilising over $500bn of third-party capital for AI infrastructure. The pitch, credited personally to Jensen Huang: compute is now an investable asset class, to be priced like real estate or toll roads rather than hardware on a depreciation schedule. Taylor's framing is that this makes Nvidia the world's largest fintech company — captive finance in the tradition of GM Financial and GE Capital, with one critical difference: the money is third-party, the six firms underwrite independently, and Nvidia's exposure is residual-value support of up to 25% on some projects, decided case by case. Goldman's David Solomon describes the goal as creating "a market for credit backed by NVIDIA compute" — underwriting the infrastructure rather than the customer, so that an AI lab with no credit rating can finance a data centre the way an airline finances a 787. The economics: a 1GW data centre costs $50–60bn, which almost nobody can fund from their own balance sheet, so leasing turns it into a monthly payment. Pricing infrastructure is arriving alongside — Kalshi publishes forward curves for GPU prices and CME has compute futures in development with Silicon Data. Taylor's discomfort is specific and worth quoting on: the SEC has just exempted certain data-centre bonds from asset-backed-securities rules, including post-2008 risk-retention requirements, on the logic that a data centre keeps operating and earning unlike a mortgage that amortises to zero — which also means pension funds and insurers can hold the paper. And roughly half the contracted backlog sits with AI labs that have never made a profit.

Nvidia arranges for six of the world's largest capital pools to own the AI factories — and the SEC has just agreed the bonds are not asset-backed securities

Fintech Brainfood · Simon Taylor · Sun 16 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a very large vendor-financing announcement. The one that matters is the single unresolved question Taylor isolates — is a GPU equipment or infrastructure? — because the entire $500bn structure rests on the answer and the deal terms contradict the pitch. Huang says infrastructure: underwrite the income stream, price it like a toll road. But Nvidia is offering residual-value support of up to 25%, and as Taylor observes, nobody insures the residual value of a toll road. They insure it on used cars. The seller is arguing infrastructure in public while behaving like an equipment vendor in the contract, and the difference between those two readings is the difference between a securitisation market and a leasing book with a manufacturer guarantee underneath it.

Fintech Brainfood — “Nvidia is the world's largest fintech company”

The structural read is that we are watching an asset class get manufactured in real time, and the sequence is instructive because it is the same sequence every time. First the asset needs a price, which is arriving via Kalshi's forward curves and CME's coming compute futures. Then it needs a legal characterisation, which the SEC has just supplied by declaring data-centre bonds outside the asset-backed rules. Then it needs distribution, which is what the six underwriters are for, and which the SEC's characterisation unlocks by making the paper eligible for pension and insurance balance sheets. Each step is individually defensible. Compute genuinely is fungible, transferable between customers, and kept productive by software updates in a way a single-purpose machine is not — the A100 shipped in 2020 and is still earning, which is real evidence for the infrastructure claim. But the aggregate is a structure whose safety depends on a residual value that no downturn has ever tested, distributed to holders selected precisely for their inability to absorb surprises, with the post-2008 rule designed to keep the issuer's skin in the game specifically disapplied. Taylor's own formulation is the fair one: securitisation done transparently is healthy, and the 2008 failure was not securitisation but ratings applied to risk nobody had priced. The worry here is not that the structure is fraudulent. It is that transparency is moving backwards at the exact moment the asset is being distributed most widely.

Jensen says it is a toll road. The contract says it is a used car with a manufacturer's residual guarantee. You do not need to resolve that argument to invest, but you do need to know which one the pension fund holding the paper thinks it bought.

The threads: to Issue 066's item 4 (Airtel renting Starlink capacity rather than building towers across the Congo) — identical logic at a different scale, and today it is the AI labs declining to own $50–60bn of factory; in both cases renting is the fast path to capability and in both cases somebody else ends up holding the residual, which is the question neither transaction answers. To Issue 066's item 1 (Oui Capital, and the difference between an option and a claim on scarce resource) — residual-value support up to 25% is exactly that kind of quiet claim, off Nvidia's income statement today and on it in a scenario nobody is modelling. To Issue 065 and Issue 064 on the compute build-out — the financing layer is now the story rather than the chips, which is usually the point in a cycle where the interesting risk stops being technological. To item 1 in today's edition (Absa impairing software because of "the pace of technological change") — the juxtaposition is almost too neat: a South African bank writing capitalised technology to zero because it aged faster than its schedule assumed, on the same morning that global capital markets agree to treat GPUs as long-lived infrastructure. Both cannot be right about useful life, and only one of them is being financed at $500bn. To the Chronos read on AI infrastructure exposure — the practical implication is that AI risk is now entering diversified fixed-income portfolios through a door marked "infrastructure", so any LP or balance sheet holding investment-grade infrastructure paper may be accumulating AI-demand exposure it has not underwritten as such. The sceptic's ledger: these are MOUs subject to final agreements, not funded facilities, and the gap between an announced $500bn platform and deployed capital has historically been very wide; the residual-value support is up to 25%, on some projects, decided case by case, which is a much weaker commitment than the headline implies and may be immaterial in aggregate; the A100 evidence genuinely does support the infrastructure characterisation and this note may be too quick to dismiss it; the SEC's reasoning — that an operating, earning asset differs categorically from an amortising mortgage — is not obviously wrong and is the same logic applied to pipelines and toll roads for decades; and "half the backlog with unprofitable labs" describes counterparties who are among the best-capitalised private companies in history, so the credit comparison to subprime borrowers is rhetorically satisfying and analytically loose. The forward marker: the two numbers Taylor names. What hourly rent a five-year-old GPU commands, which is the infrastructure test, and what residual value an insurer will actually sign on a used box, which is the equipment test. The second has never been run in a downturn, and until it is, this remains a thesis with $500bn of intent behind it.

Nearly 40% of the index is down on the year, and you would not know it from the index
Pershing Square Inc. — Q2 2026 Letter to Shareholders (PDF)

The structural read is that Ackman has written down, in a founding public document, the argument that the scarce good in asset management is not returns but patience that cannot be withdrawn. Every manager says they invest for the long term. Almost none can, because the capital underneath them has a redemption right, and a redemption right converts every drawdown into a potential forced sale at the worst moment. Permanent capital removes that conversion, and the second-order effects compound: no perpetual fundraise means partner attention goes to portfolio companies rather than to prospective LPs, and retained gains mean fee-paying AUM grows with performance rather than with distribution effort. The 82.4% margin is what that looks like in the accounts. It is worth being clear about what has been traded away for it, because the letter is honest about this in a way that is easy to skim past: the investors gave up the right to redeem and got exchange liquidity instead, which means they can always sell but not always at NAV. In a stress the discount to NAV is where the redemption pressure goes. That is not a free lunch; it is a relocation of the liquidity risk from the manager's balance sheet to the shareholder's exit price, and the manager has been explicit that this relocation is the whole competitive advantage. Then there is the other half of the letter, which is arguably more useful to us than the first: fewer than one in ten S&P constituents contributed meaningfully to a 10% index gain, and roughly 40% of them fell. Anyone benchmarking a portfolio against the index this year is benchmarking against two sectors wearing a broad-market costume.

Permanent capital does not remove the liquidity risk. It moves it from the manager, who would have had to sell, to the shareholder, who now has to accept a discount. Ackman is unusually clear that this relocation is the entire product.

From the Workshop

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Stop perfecting the prompt — write a rough one, then correct the output

The most common way to waste time with these models is to sit staring at an empty box trying to compose the perfect instruction, as though you were drafting a brief to counsel and would not get another go. You get as many goes as you like, and the second one is always better informed than the first, because by then you have something concrete to react to. People are far better at criticising than at specifying — you may not be able to describe the memo you want, but you will know within ten seconds of reading a draft that the tone is too formal, the second section should be first, and it has completely missed the point about the debt covenant. That reaction is the specification, and it is much easier to produce than the prompt you were trying to write. So write the lazy version, read what comes back, and then say what is wrong with it in plain language. Three rounds of that beats twenty minutes of prompt engineering nearly every time, and it costs you nothing to start badly.

A worked example

Round one, deliberately lazy: “Draft a note to the IC on the Absa results and what they say about technology spend at African banks.” Then read it and correct in plain speech: “Too long and too neutral. Cut it in half. Lead with the software impairment, not the revenue — the R200m this half on top of R2.4bn last year is the actual story and you have buried it in paragraph four. Drop the background on Absa, the IC knows who they are. Add a paragraph asking what the assumed useful life of capitalised software is across our own portfolio, because that is the question this raises for us. And stop hedging every sentence — say what you think and let me disagree with it.” That took forty seconds to type and specifies far more than any opening prompt you would have written cold.
