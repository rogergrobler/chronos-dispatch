Howard Marks: "The Seduction of Certainty" — May 2026 memo

Howard Marks · Oaktree Memos · Wed 20 May 2026

Marks's new memo argues that the current market environment — heavily distorted by AI capex, geopolitical disruption, and persistent rate-curve dislocation — rewards investors who can hold a calibrated uncertainty in their thesis. The temptation is to resolve ambiguity by adopting a clean directional view; the discipline is staying with the ambiguity and sizing positions accordingly. The memo references the 1998-2000 cycle as the historical analogue.

Read at oaktreecapital.com

The 1998-2000 reference is the part to read carefully. Marks's argument is that the investors who held a calibrated view of internet-as-real-but-overpriced did better than either the true believers or the dismissive sceptics. The structural feature: they sized positions consistent with a probability distribution rather than a point estimate.

Applied to 2026: AI deployment is real and compounding (the dispatch's recurring thread); the public-market pricing is partly justified and partly distorted; the venture-stage pricing is more clearly distorted. The investor who sizes positions assuming "AI is real" gets the upside if it is and gets crushed if pricing reverts. The investor who sizes assuming "AI is overpriced" misses if the compounding continues. Marks's prescription is to hold both possibilities and size accordingly.

"The seduction of certainty is strongest precisely when the data justifies caution about which certainty to adopt." — Howard Marks, paraphrased

The longitudinal thread: Issue 007's Ferguson piece on institutional fragility maps directly here. Both pieces describe the same operator discipline from different angles — slow-thinking when the environment rewards speed, calibrated uncertainty when the environment rewards conviction.

Andrew Ng: open-source AI is beating frontier on specific enterprise tasks — depth over raw capability

Andrew Ng · The Batch · DeepLearning.AI · Wed 20 May 2026

Ng's Wednesday Batch this week tracks a pattern he calls "depth over capability". On specific enterprise tasks where fine-tuning to proprietary data matters — medical-imaging segmentation, legal-clause extraction, fraud-pattern detection — properly fine-tuned open-source models (Llama 4, Mistral Large 3) are now outperforming GPT-class frontier models. The implication: the moat is shifting from "access to the best model" to "depth of domain-specific fine-tuning data".

Read at deeplearning.ai

The argument deepens the operating-model thread from Issues 001 through 007. The conventional read of "AI moat" was: best model + best workflow + best data. Ng's new framing collapses two of those — the model layer is converging, the workflow layer is becoming templated (per Stanford in Issue 006), so the durable moat is the data layer specifically.

For our AI-applied portcos: Vertice MedTech's clinical-imaging dataset is the moat. OfferZen's developer-behaviour data is the moat. TymeBank's GoalSave-driven savings data is the moat. The model can be open-source and the workflow can be templated — what can't be replicated is the proprietary fine-tuning corpus.

"The moat is shifting from access to the best model toward the depth of domain-specific fine-tuning data." — Andrew Ng, paraphrased

The longitudinal arc closes here. Issue 001 raised the productivity-gap puzzle. Issues 003-005 saw the labs respond. Issue 006-007 made the deployment patterns concrete. Issue 008 names the durable moat: proprietary data depth. The picture is now eight items wide and structurally consistent.

Anthropic Economic Index supplement: African deployments outperform US at equivalent maturity

Anthropic · Wed 20 May 2026

Anthropic's supplementary release breaks the Q1 Economic Index data by geography. US deployments at the same stage-of-maturity show 18% YoY productivity gain (per Issue 007); UK is at 21%; SA at 28%; Nigeria and Kenya at 35%. The pattern: where the pre-AI baseline is more manual, the AI deployment lift is proportionally larger. The implication is that emerging-market AI-applied businesses may compound faster than the US comparables suggest.

Anthropic Economic Index

The geographic breakdown is the structural insight. The conventional read of EM AI deployment was "lag behind US" — implying lower returns. The Anthropic data flips it: lower baseline means more headroom, which means proportionally larger productivity gains per deployment dollar.

This is consistent with Issue 007's AI-in-insurance chart (SA at 30%, Nigeria at 15%) — the African market isn't behind on deployment, it's behind on the curve with more room above. The economic returns to early African AI deployment may exceed the returns to incremental US deployment.

"Lower pre-AI baseline equals larger productivity lift per deployment. EM AI economics may exceed US." — observation

For Chronos: this is the underwriting framework for the AI-applied African portfolio. Vertice MedTech, OfferZen, and any new AI-applied portco we consider can be underwritten with deployment-lift assumptions higher than the US benchmark — not lower. The thesis-test is whether the Anthropic data holds across vertical applications, not just chat workflows.

First Round's piece on what makes seed-stage CEOs scale through Series B — and what makes them break — runs from a 50-CEO interview cohort. First Round Review: three observable CEO behaviours at $5m ARR that predict scale

First Round Review · This week

A First Round Review research piece based on interviews with 50 seed-to-Series-B CEOs argues that three observable behaviours at the $5m ARR mark predict whether a CEO scales through to $25m+ ARR or stalls. The three: (i) hires their first VP of Engineering before product-market-fit is fully proven; (ii) ruthlessly says no to adjacent revenue opportunities for 18 months; (iii) maintains an explicit metric for "decision velocity" inside the leadership team.

Read at firstround.com

The first criterion is the counterintuitive one. The conventional wisdom is "don't hire executives until you need them" — but First Round's data says the CEOs who scale are the ones who hire ahead of need, not behind it. The mechanism: by the time you need a VP of Engineering, your product velocity has already started to decay, and recovering it costs more than the prevention.

The third criterion — explicit decision-velocity metrics — is the operator-craft theme that's run through several of our dispatches. The observable form of "good operating model" is fast, consistent decisions; the observable failure mode is meetings stretching into committees stretching into delays.

"Hire the VP of Engineering before you think you need them. By the time you need them, you've already missed the window." — First Round, paraphrased

The thesis-test: across the AI-applied early-stage portfolio (Insure.ag, OfferZen at its scaling moments, the Crossfin operating units that are scaling), how does the management team rate against these three observable behaviours? The data set is small enough to be subjective but the framework is usable for the next round of board check-ins.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Force structure — "give me this as a 2x2 with examples in each quadrant"

When Claude returns a long-form analysis that's hard to act on, force it into a structure. The 2x2 prompt is the simplest version — pick two axes that matter, ask Claude to populate each quadrant with concrete examples from the analysis. The structure forces Claude to commit to a position rather than hedging. Other useful structures: ranked list with reasons, traffic-light table (green/amber/red), three-by-three matrix with action implications.

Try this after any long-form analysis

"Turn this analysis into a 2x2 matrix. Axis 1: high vs. low confidence. Axis 2: high vs. low strategic impact. Place each conclusion from the analysis into a quadrant, with one specific example in each. Don't add commentary outside the matrix."
