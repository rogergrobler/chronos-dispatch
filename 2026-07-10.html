Global · AI · Economics Analysis Thesis test
Benedict Evans published "Ways to think about token pricing" on Thursday — a structural essay on where AI pricing settles once the current supply crunch clears. The load-bearing facts: over a trillion dollars of data-centre capex is in the pipe; inference reportedly runs at 40–50% gross margin before training costs that currently exceed revenue; the crunch has been driven by product-market fit in essentially one use case, software development. His chosen comparison is mobile data: traffic rose by orders of magnitude into a trillion-dollar-revenue industry with $200bn of annual capex — and the stocks went nowhere, because the value was captured further up the stack. Even TSMC, with a de facto monopoly on the semiconductor frontier, earned $53bn last year — less than half of Apple alone. His conclusion: every dynamic currently visible points to frontier models becoming commodity infrastructure, and every path to durable pricing power requires something to change that is not yet visible.

Ben Evans: every visible dynamic points to frontier models becoming commodity plumbing

Benedict Evans · Essay · Thu 9 Jul 2026

In plain English: the labs can name their price today only because supply is short, and Evans asks the four questions that decide what happens after — how many use cases actually need the expensive frontier, whether the frontier keeps moving, whether frontier competition stays fierce, and how much value the model itself captures versus the software wrapped around it. His answer to the last is the one that matters here: on current evidence the model is the database, and every SaaS company is a database wrapper. The essay refuses the metaphor game — analogies to fiber, mobile or fabs have no predictive value — but the empirical record says something can change the world and still make its builders no money.

Benedict Evans — “Ways to think about token pricing”

This is the cleanest statement yet of the argument underneath the Chronos thesis. If the value of AI accrues up the stack — to the companies that own customers, distribution, proprietary data and workflow — then the investable layer is the tool user, not the toolmaker. That is the same conclusion the Chronicle's threads have been converging on from the other direction: the Remote Labor Index item (Issue 040) showed the capability arriving; Flutterwave collecting stablecoin issuers as interchangeable tenants (Issue 040) showed distribution taxing infrastructure; and the “foundation models are a commodity” remark Evans made earlier in the year has now been worked into a full argument.

“Cellular data traffic has risen by several orders of magnitude… an enormous industry, with annual revenue of a trillion dollars — but the stocks have gone nowhere, and all the value was captured by other people further up the stack.”

Two disciplines worth stealing. First, his refusal to model bottom-up: a token-price spreadsheet in 2026 is the broadband forecast of 1998 — pretty, plausibly right for a year, useless for market structure. Second, his inversion of the burden of proof: commoditisation is the default trajectory, and it is pricing power that requires a special event — network effects that haven't appeared, divergence that hasn't happened, or regulation (he names China's floated open-source curbs and US export politics as the two live wildcards). For the portfolio, the practical read is that model costs in every portco P&L should be treated as a deflating input, not a strategic dependency — and that the durable moats remain exactly where they were before AI: customers, data, licences, distribution. The forward question is his own: what would have to change for the labs to escape the mobile-operator ending — and would we see it coming in time to reprice?

West Africa · Macro · Chart Analysis Longitudinal
The ECOWAS Bank for Investment and Development released its regional report, "Distant Turbulences, Familiar Shocks", in Lomé on Tuesday. The headline numbers are the best since Covid: growth of 4.8% in 2025, the regional fiscal deficit narrowed from 3.7% to 2.6% of GDP, public debt down sharply from 55.3% to 49.3% of GDP, and the current account swung from 0.3% to a 1.8% surplus. Then the other half: working poverty increased in every one of the 15 countries the bank covers, and none of the 15 converted macro stability into productive job creation over the past 22 months. EBID calls the recovery fragile and insufficiently inclusive, projects 4.7% growth in 2026 and 4.9% in 2027, and expects the deficit to widen back towards 3.5% as governments subsidise energy through the region's third global shock in six years — Covid, Ukraine, now the Middle East's oil and fertiliser disruption.

West Africa's best macro numbers since Covid — and worker poverty rose in all 15 countries anyway

Ecofin Agency (Fiacre E. Kakpo) · Macroeconomics · Thu 9 Jul 2026

In plain English: the sovereign balance sheets healed and the households didn't notice. Every stabilisation lever — revenue mobilisation, spending control, debt reduction — worked, and the one thing stabilisation is supposed to buy, jobs, arrived in zero of fifteen countries. That is not a rounding error; it is a 22-month controlled experiment in whether macro orthodoxy alone converts to living standards. EBID's own prescription is regional self-reliance in energy, fertiliser and food — the institutional echo of the Africa-funds-itself thread this page has tracked for a month.

Every sovereign dial green, both household dials red — the recovery nobody downstairs received
Ecofin Agency — “West Africa's Growth Rebound Fails to Lift Workers Out of Poverty”

Read this against the two capital-flows items it completes. Issue 039's lead was US endowment stress forcing an early African fund handover — external capital getting scarcer. Issue 040's Ecobank diaspora desk was the retail version of Africa funding itself. EBID now supplies the macro floor under both: the region has done the orthodox repair work — deficits, debt, external balance — and the reward, on the ground, has not arrived. That matters for the investment lens because “improving macro” is the standard top-down case for the region, and this report is the region's own development bank saying the transmission mechanism from stability to income is broken.

“None of the 15 West African countries covered by the report succeeded in turning that progress into productive job creation over the past 22 months.”

The structural read: growth without jobs in a region this young is not a neutral miss — it is the demand-side question for every consumer-facing thesis, and it sharpens the Remote Labor Index thread (Issue 040) uncomfortably. If AI is absorbing exactly the digital-services work that was supposed to be West Africa's employment ladder, then the formal-jobs route to household income narrows at both ends at once — macro stability fails to create them, and automation removes the export variety. The businesses that win in that world are the ones that raise household productivity directly rather than waiting for employment to arrive: the Djamo item two cards down is, in effect, a bet on that customer. The forward question: EBID projects the deficit widening again as energy subsidies return — if the orthodox gains start unwinding before the jobs ever arrived, what exactly was the stabilisation for?

South Africa · AI · Frontier Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Refiant, an AI-optimisation startup founded by South Africans Dr Viroshan Naicker, Siddharth Gutta and Mathew Haswell, launched Protea on Wednesday — a suite of long-context language models topping out at a 10-million-token context window, among the largest publicly available anywhere. That is roughly 7.5 million words or 15,000 pages held in a single prompt. The models come in 1M, 5M and 10M versions, free to use with no waitlist. The company claims progress on the “lost in the middle” problem — long-context models losing accuracy on information buried deep in the input — and says an internal prototype already runs a 100-million-token window. The kicker is the balance sheet: Refiant raised a $5m seed led by VoLo Earth Ventures just three months ago. The frontier labs sell long context as a premium feature; a seed-stage team is giving it away.

A South African lab gives away a 10-million-token context window — on a $5m seed

TechCabal · AI · Thu 9 Jul 2026

In plain English: context length — how much a model can hold in view at once — is one of the dimensions on which the big labs differentiate and price. Refiant's bet is that it can be attacked with optimisation cleverness rather than compute scale, from Johannesburg, for free. The claims await independent benchmarking, and long-context accuracy is precisely where marketing and reality diverge most — but if even the 1M-token tier holds up, an entire deal-room, a year of board packs, or a full litigation record fits in one prompt without retrieval plumbing. Note the wider signal regardless of benchmark outcome: the frontier's premium features are being commoditised from below, at seed-stage prices.

TechCabal — “Refiant AI launches 10 million-token large language model”

Run this item and the Ben Evans lead as a pair. His argument — commoditisation is the default and pricing power needs a special event — usually gets discussed as a fight among trillion-dollar labs. Refiant is what the argument looks like from below: a $5m seed company attacking a premium feature of the frontier, in the same week the frontier's own capabilities became formally unmeasurable (next card). Whether Protea's accuracy claims survive independent testing almost matters less than the existence proof: the cost of entering a dimension of the frontier has fallen to seed-round scale. Every such entry is deflation in the token market Evans describes.

“A 10-million-token window holds roughly 15,000 pages — several years of corporate communications — in a single prompt.”

The local thread is the counterweight this page has needed. The recent South African tech narrative in these editions has been brain drain from Joburg (Issue 037's layoffs chart, the TechCentral exodus piece) and the opinionista's “winner-takes-all AI economy, and SA wasn't invited”. Here is a South African-founded team shipping at the global frontier's edge — and choosing distribution-first economics (free, no waitlist) straight from the African fintech playbook: acquire usage, monetise later. For the portfolio, the practical test costs nothing: the cross-examination workflow from Thursday's Workshop tip is exactly what a 10M-token window claims to do natively — feed it a full data room and see whether it actually finds the contradictions on page 9,000, or gets lost in the middle like everyone else. The forward question: when long context is free, which retrieval, vector-database and document-pipeline businesses — a well-funded layer of the current AI stack — quietly lose their reason to exist?

Côte d'Ivoire · Fintech · Deals Briefing Longitudinal
Djamo, the Ivorian fintech founded in 2020 by Hassan Bourgi and Régis Bamba, is raising a $40m Series C to deepen its position across Francophone West Africa — which would rank among the largest rounds ever for the region's homegrown fintechs. The base: more than two million customers on bank-backed cards, current accounts, credit and remittances, in a market where Côte d'Ivoire's financial inclusion has climbed from 41% (2017) to 58% (2024) and 44% of adults still hold no formal account. Dealroom puts the valuation between $100m and $500m, likely towards the top. Its April 2025 $17m round was already an Ivorian record. The capital is earmarked for Senegal and the eight-market WAEMU zone, products for young consumers, SMEs and the diaspora — and Djamo is discussing a stablecoin sandbox with the regional central bank for cross-border payments.

Djamo raises to become what Francophone West Africa graduates to after mobile money

TechCabal Daily (Opeyemi Kareem) · Fintech · Thu 9 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: Djamo is not fighting Wave for the wallet — it is positioning as the account customers move to once they need credit, savings and international payments, which is where the margin lives and where the EBID item's stagnant household incomes make product-market fit hardest and most valuable. And the stablecoin sandbox conversation with the BCEAO extends the thread of Issues 037–040: every serious African rail, Anglophone or Francophone, is now negotiating its dollar-settlement layer with its regulator before the informal market decides for them.

South Africa · Fintech · Expansion Briefing Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Float, the Johannesburg-founded card-linked instalment platform, has expanded into the UK — skipping the standard pan-African playbook entirely. The product converts purchases on existing Visa and Mastercard credit cards into interest- and fee-free instalments of up to 24 months, issuing no new credit: it works inside the bank's existing facility, with the merchant paying the fee. In South Africa it has signed 2,200+ merchants (Samsung, iStore, The North Face) and raised more than R280m ($17.1m) in equity and debt including Standard Bank. Founder Alex Forsyth-Thompson's UK logic: 55 million credit cards in circulation, over £70bn of balances incurring interest, roughly £250bn of unused limits — “these shoppers don't need more credit; they need more time.” The entry is backed by the UK Government's Global Entrepreneur Programme.

Float skips the African expansion race and takes SA's instalment rails to the UK

TechCabal (Phathisani Moyo) · Fintech · Thu 9 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the reverse-flow story worth watching — a product built under SA's crowded, capital-constrained, compliance-heavy conditions arriving in a developed market as the leaner competitor, the same direction of travel as Kenya's WapiPay entering Canada last month. And the positioning detail is the clever part: by riding existing bank credit lines rather than issuing loans, Float sidesteps both Klarna's regulatory exposure and the capital costs of a lending book — a structurally lighter BNPL that only works in markets where the credit card already won.

Global · AI · Governance Briefing Thesis test
The Batch (issue 358) reports that multiple independent evaluators — including Artificial Analysis and Vals AI — could not fully measure Claude Fable 5, the publicly available version of Anthropic's newest frontier model. Anthropic's classifiers screen every prompt before it reaches the model, filtering questions touching cybersecurity, biology and chemistry, or AI model engineering; flagged prompts are refused outright or silently answered by the less capable Claude Opus 4.8. A new 30-day data-retention condition led some evaluators to withhold proprietary test sets altogether. Each testing house now has to choose between a “pure” capability score the public can never experience and a “practical” score contaminated by refusals and fallback routing — and different houses chose differently, so the published numbers no longer measure the same thing.

The yardsticks no longer fit: independent testers cannot fully measure the newest frontier model

The Batch, issue 358 (DeepLearning.AI) · AI Governance · Wed 8 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the market Ben Evans analyses in today's lead runs on benchmark scores the way credit markets run on ratings — and this is the moment the ratings agencies lost access to the books, with capability claims now resting partly on the vendor's word. And the thread is longitudinal: the US government's shutdown of a Claude model (Issue 036) and JPMorgan restricting Anthropic in Hong Kong (June) were access politics between institutions — this is the same access politics arriving at the measurement layer itself, which is where every buyer, regulator and investor gets their information.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Stop re-typing your best prompts — build the firm's playbook one template at a time

Four tools have run in this series: the 2×2, the pre-mortem, the sceptic, and Thursday's document cross-examination. The fifth is not a new prompt — it is what you do with the ones that worked. A prompt that produced a genuinely useful result is intellectual property, and most people throw it away by leaving it in a scrolled-off chat. The fix takes thirty seconds: when a run lands, copy the prompt into a note, strip the deal-specific details, and replace them with placeholders in square brackets. Ten of those and you have something no consultant sells: a prompt playbook calibrated to how your firm actually thinks. The compounding effect is the point — the sceptic prompt you refined in March attacks your July deal with all of March's learning already priced in.

A worked example

Take Thursday's cross-examination prompt and template it: “You are cross-examining these documents, not summarising them: [LIST DOCUMENTS]. Find every place where two documents disagree — numbers that don't reconcile, claims in one undermined by another, language that shifts between versions. Quote both sides exactly, rank the contradictions by how much they matter if the less flattering version is true, and end with the three questions for [MANAGEMENT / COUNTERPARTY] these make unavoidable. UK English, no preamble.” Store it wherever you'll actually look — a Notion page, a note, a saved Project — and add one line under it: what it found last time, and what you changed. That single line of provenance is what turns a prompt into a playbook.
