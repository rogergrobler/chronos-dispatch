Fintech & Operating Model · Fintech Brainfood
The single sharpest framing of the AI transition I have seen this year. Some companies are restructuring themselves around AI — quietly, deliberately. Everyone else is waiting to be done to. Directly answers your Issue 005 ask: this is the practical deployment thread, with portfolio implications.

Simon Taylor: "Some companies are rebuilding themselves around AI. Everyone else is waiting to be done to."

Simon Taylor · Fintech Brainfood · Sun 17 May 2026

Taylor's piece argues that the AI transition in 2026 is bifurcating into two camps. The first — a small group, naming Klarna, Revolut, Wise, and parts of JPMorgan and DBS — are rebuilding internal operating models around AI before being forced to. The second — most large incumbents — are running pilots and waiting for a vendor, lab, or competitor to define the new model for them. Taylor's prediction: by Q4 2026 the gap between the two cohorts will be visible in unit economics.

Read at fintechbrainfood.com

Taylor names the proactive cohort by what they're doing structurally: they have rebuilt the workflow around an agent first, and then asked who needs to remain in the loop and at what gate. The reactive cohort has bolted AI onto existing workflows as a feature — chatbot here, copilot there — without rebuilding the operating model around it.

The Q4 2026 prediction is the testable claim. If the proactive cohort has materially better unit economics by Christmas (cost-to-serve, time-to-decision, customer ARPU), the gap is structural. If they don't, AI is closer to a feature than a transformation in financial services.

"Either you rebuild yourself around AI, or someone rebuilds your business around theirs." — Simon Taylor

The longitudinal thread: Issue 001 (90% of firms see no AI gain), Issue 003 (OpenAI deployment company), Issue 004 (Andrew Ng on agentic workflows), Issue 005 (three case studies in production). Today's piece is the explicit prediction those threads have been pointing at — and the framing you can take into a portco board: which side of the line are we on?

Practical Deployment · Stanford
A Stanford team studied 51 successful enterprise AI deployments and identified the structural pattern that distinguishes them from failures. The single most useful "help us help our portcos" reference document of 2026. Worth circulating to every portco CEO.

Stanford: "The Enterprise AI Playbook — Lessons from 51 Successful Deployments"

Pereira, Graylin, Brynjolfsson · Stanford Digital Economy Lab · Mar 2026

A Stanford working paper analysing 51 production AI deployments across financial services, healthcare, manufacturing, retail, and government. The authors identify a six-element pattern that every successful deployment shares — and that failed pilots lack. Among the elements: (1) workflow rebuild rather than feature addition, (2) explicit ownership at the executive level rather than CTO delegation, (3) measurable economic objective set at outset rather than "AI exploration".

Read the paper (PDF)

The six-element pattern, summarised: (1) workflow rebuild not feature addition; (2) executive-level ownership; (3) measurable economic objective from day one; (4) production-grade data infrastructure before model selection; (5) explicit human-checkpoint design in the agent loop; (6) commitment to a 6-12 month integration runway rather than 4-8 week pilots.

The failure pattern is symmetric — most stalled enterprise AI initiatives lack four or more of the six. The Stanford team's argument is that this is not coincidence: the pattern is causal.

"Failed enterprise AI initiatives don't fail at the model. They fail at the operating model around the model." — Pereira et al.

For Chronos portcos: the paper is short enough to circulate (38 pages) and dense enough to be actionable. The six-element checklist is the kind of test that every portfolio company CEO should be able to walk through honestly. The Brynjolfsson signature on the paper gives it institutional weight.

EM Charts · Private Credit
Private credit AUM trajectory — visualised. The trade is no longer a thesis, it is a fact pattern. The chart reframes the Lima fundraise conversation: LPs aren't deciding whether to allocate to private credit, they are deciding which corner of it.

Private credit AUM: from $0.9T to $4T in a decade — charted

Wellington Management · Ares · Moody's · 2026 outlook
$4T is no longer the bull case. It's the consensus path.

Combined data from Wellington, Ares, and Moody's converges on a private credit AUM trajectory from $0.9T (2020) to $1.6T (2024), through $2T this year, to a consensus path toward $4T by 2030. The driver shifted in 2024: it is no longer just US direct lending — asset-backed finance, EMEA, and APAC are now the marginal contributors to growth.

Wellington outlook

The interesting feature of the chart is not the slope — it's the change in composition. Pre-2024, the AUM growth was almost entirely US direct lending. 2024–2026 saw asset-backed finance and infrastructure debt take share. 2026–2030 will see EMEA and APAC take share — meaning the consensus growth path now depends on private credit succeeding outside the US.

For Lima, this matters two ways. First, the LP base globally has converged on private credit as a strategic allocation, which de-risks the fundraise from a category-availability standpoint. Second, the marginal incremental LP dollar is now looking at non-US strategies — exactly the corner Lima occupies.

"The $4T trajectory only holds if private credit succeeds outside the US. Lima is exactly that bet." — observation

The longitudinal thread: Issue 001's Apollo Daily Spark chart was the first time private credit overtook US high-yield in our coverage. Issue 005's stablecoin piece pointed to settlement-infrastructure as the next layer. The current chart is the structural backdrop those two pieces sit against.

AI Frontier · Import AI
A 20-year-old computer virus that selectively tampers with precision-calculation software is the kind of capability that quietly threatens any AI-applied operator dependent on numerical correctness — including underwriting, valuation, and treasury. The "positive alignment" framing in the same issue is a more useful policy lens than the binary safety debate.

Import AI 457: An AI stuxnet, and the case for "positive alignment"

Jack Clark · Import AI 457 · Mon 18 May 2026

Clark covers two threads in this week's Import AI. First, the discovery of an old (20+ year) Windows driver — fast16.sys — that selectively targets high-precision calculation software, patching code in memory to tamper with results. The reframing: this is the kind of capability that becomes valuable in an AI-deployment world where numerical correctness is the whole product. Second, a paper from Oxford, DeepMind and others on "positive alignment" — moving AI safety from "don't be harmful" to "actively support human flourishing".

Read at importai.substack.com

The fast16.sys discovery is the more interesting half. A piece of malware quietly running on regulated-industry desktops that selectively tampers with high-precision floating-point math is exactly the kind of attack surface that doesn't get tested in the standard cybersecurity audit, because the consequences look like model drift or data quality issues rather than tampering.

For any portfolio company deploying AI for numerical decisioning (Vertice MedTech for imaging analysis, Crossfin for transaction monitoring, TymeBank for credit scoring), the implication is that the security model needs to extend to the precision of the calculation, not just the data going in and the model running.

"A 20-year-old driver that quietly tampers with numerical precision. The attack surface we don't audit." — observation on fast16.sys

The "positive alignment" framing is also worth tracking. The standard alignment debate has been about preventing harm. The Oxford/DeepMind paper argues the more useful frame is whether AI actively supports the institutional aims its operator cares about. That's a more practical alignment problem — and one that maps onto how portcos should think about deploying AI inside their own value chain.

Africa H1 2026 tech funding hits $887M — fewer deals, bigger rounds

TechCabal Insights · 7 May 2026

TechCabal Insights reports $887M raised by African tech companies in the first four months of 2026, on track to cross the $1B mark for H1. The deal-count is down 22% YoY but average ticket size is up 41% — the funding profile is consolidating around fewer, larger rounds. Fintech infrastructure (Stitch is on this list), payments, and B2B SaaS are the dominant categories.

Read at techcabal.com

The fewer-but-larger pattern is what category maturation looks like. The 2018-2022 African tech boom was characterised by spray-and-pray seed rounds; the 2026 picture is meaningful Series A-D into a narrower set of proven categories. Fintech infrastructure, payments, and B2B SaaS — three categories that look exactly like what Chronos has been positioned around.

The slump in deal count is worth flagging, though. A 22% drop suggests that local angel and seed-stage investors are pulling back even as the international Series A money is concentrating. That's a familiar mid-cycle pattern in EM venture, but it tightens the early-stage funnel for any portco still ramping.

"Fewer deals, bigger cheques. That's what category maturation looks like in EM venture." — observation

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Draft the reply, then edit — 3x faster than from blank page

Most partners stare at a blank reply box and write from scratch. Stop. Paste the inbound email (or letter, or message) to Claude with one line of intent — "decline politely", "say yes but push timing to next week", "ask for one more data point". Claude drafts. You edit. Two minutes instead of seven. The editing makes it sound like you; the speed comes from not starting cold.

Try this on the next inbound

"Here's an inbound email asking for a 30-minute call to pitch their fund. Draft a polite decline that acknowledges the work and says we're not deploying into the strategy in 2026. Two paragraphs. Sound warm, not corporate."
