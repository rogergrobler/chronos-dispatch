Global · Stablecoins · Market Structure Analysis Longitudinal
On 30 June a new independent venture, Open Standard, launched Open USD (OUSD) — a dollar stablecoin backed by more than 140 companies. From traditional finance: Visa, Mastercard, BlackRock, Standard Chartered, US Bank, American Express, BBVA, BNY and Stripe. From tech: Google, Samsung, IBM, Shopify, Mercado Libre. From crypto: Coinbase, Ripple, Solana, Fireblocks, Crypto.com. The design has three legs: businesses mint and redeem at zero cost with no volume caps; reserve earnings are distributed back to partners minus a small management fee; governance sits with an independent board of the partners. Founding CEO is Zach Abrams, the former Coinbase product lead. Stripe’s president of technology says OUSD “will be the default stablecoin for businesses running on Stripe.” Live later in 2026.

Visa, Mastercard, Stripe and BlackRock agree to share a dollar — 140 companies launch Open USD and give away the float

FinTech Futures (Tyler Pathe) · Stablecoins · Thu 2 Jul 2026

In plain English: today a stablecoin issuer’s business is the float. You give Circle $10m, it gives you 10m USDC, it invests your $10m in US Treasuries and keeps the interest — billions a year, which is why Circle is worth what it is. Open USD inverts that. A business holding $10m of OUSD gets the Treasury yield handed back to it; the consortium keeps only a management fee. It is the same move that zero-commission trading made against brokers: give away the visible revenue line to own the flow. Whoever controls distribution — Stripe’s checkout, Visa’s network, Shopify’s merchants — no longer needs to own the token to win from it.

FinTech Futures — “Over 140 banking and tech giants team up to launch Open USD stablecoin”

Friday’s DR Congo item — which Roger flagged as a real threat to local banking systems — showed dollar stablecoins re-plumbing African wallets from the street up. Open USD is the same trade executed from the boardroom down, and the two halves matter more together than apart. When the rail was USDC, an African payments business had a choice of whether to settle on a crypto company’s coin. When the rail is a consortium containing Visa, Mastercard, Stripe and BlackRock, with zero minting fees and the float handed back, the choice starts making itself. The commodity is no longer the token; it is the settlement layer itself, priced at cost by the biggest names in payments.

“Open USD will be the default stablecoin for businesses running on Stripe.” — Will Gaybrick, Stripe

Three reads for Chronos. First, the issuer-economics era of stablecoins is closing: Circle and Tether’s margin — keep the reserve interest, pay nothing on the coin — has just been repriced to roughly zero by a coalition that can afford it, exactly as exchange rebates and free brokerage repriced earlier monopolies. Any thesis that valued a standalone issuer on float income needs rework. Second, for the African fintechs in our line of sight, the “which rail do you settle on” question from Friday now has a likely default answer, and it is one owned by a foreign consortium — the monetary-sovereignty pressure on African central banks intensifies, but so does the practical case for any cross-border operator to adopt the shared rail rather than build one. Third, the quiet winner profile repeats a pattern this newsletter keeps finding: the value migrates to whoever owns the customer relationship the rail serves — the checkout, the wallet, the merchant network — not to whoever owns the pipe. The forward question: when the float is given away and the token is free, what is left to charge for — and the answer, almost certainly, is credit, FX and data, which is where the next fight starts.

Africa · Capital Flows · Macro Analysis Thesis test
Ecofin’s Idriss Linge maps a squeeze: lenders financing AI data centres charge 8–15%, and private infrastructure funds returned 12.8% in 2025 (Goldman Sachs) — returns few sovereign borrowers or African VCs can compete with. Equity funding for African startups fell 37% year-on-year in early 2026 (Launch Base Africa), with US investors like QED, Quona and Left Lane absent from 2026 deals. Against that, the Africa Finance Corporation counts more than $2 trillion in African pension, insurance and sovereign-fund assets — more than the ~$1.7 trillion in total external financing the continent received across 2014–24 — much of it parked in short-term government paper. The blockers are regulatory: Ghana caps offshore pension investment at 5%; Botswana forced $1.7bn back onshore; a Kenyan pension fund cannot easily buy an Ivorian toll road. Working counterexamples exist: Nigeria’s InfraCredit has mobilised ~$152m of local-currency infrastructure debt from 21 pension funds; Africa50 has built an $8bn portfolio in eight years.

The AI build-out is eating the world’s savings — and Africa’s answer is the $2 trillion already sitting at home

Ecofin Agency (Idriss Linge) · Finance · Mon 6 Jul 2026

In plain English: capital is finite in the short run, and a data centre in Ohio now pays a lender 12% where a 20-year Kenyan bond or a Lagos Series A cannot. So the money goes to Ohio. Larry Fink said in May that much of the trillions for America’s AI infrastructure will likely come out of ordinary retirement accounts — and the mirror image is that Africa’s own retirement accounts, $2 trillion strong, sit in short-term government paper funding nothing. The squeeze has not fully bitten yet (Sub-Saharan Eurobonds raised ~$6bn in early 2026, the DRC’s debut was four times oversubscribed), but the direction of travel is set: global savings toward AI, African development left to fund itself.

Ecofin Agency — “AI Infrastructure Is Draining the World’s Capital. Africa Has to Fund Itself.”

This is the macro engine behind three threads we have been carrying separately. Thursday’s funding data showed African capital flowing only toward collateral; Friday’s DR Congo item showed African savings migrating into US Treasuries via stablecoins; today’s Rubinstein piece has Saylor himself blaming the AI trade for draining the Bitcoin bid. They are one phenomenon: a global repricing of where the marginal dollar goes, with double-digit AI infrastructure yield as the new hurdle rate everything else must clear. African venture, African sovereigns and crypto are all on the wrong side of the same reallocation.

“Africa’s domestic, non-bank capital now tops $2 trillion — more than the roughly $1.7 trillion in external financing the continent received in total between 2014 and 2024.”

For Chronos, three calibrations. First, the fundraising climate for African growth equity is structurally, not cyclically, tighter: the US investors who left did not leave because Africa got worse but because AI got better-paying, and that comparison does not mean-revert on Africa’s schedule. Underwriting should assume the external-capital tide stays out. Second, the $2 trillion at home is the swing factor, and the article’s worked examples say the unlock is credible intermediation, not new money: InfraCredit got 21 pension funds comfortable with infrastructure risk by wrapping it in a guarantee — the “own the trusted pipe” play again, this time between African institutional savings and African assets. Whoever builds that conduit at scale becomes the most important financial institution on the continent, and it is a fair question whether it looks more like a fintech or a monoline insurer. Third, the same drain explains today’s AI-geography item: the capital that will not fund a Kenyan bond will also not fund a Kenyan gigawatt, which is why Microsoft’s $1bn Nairobi campus stalled while Johannesburg — with bankable power — takes the compute. The forward question: which African pension regulator moves first to let the $2 trillion out of government paper — and which operators are positioned on the receiving end when it moves.

Global · Financial Engineering · Best Thinkers Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Marc Rubinstein’s Friday Net Interest dissects Strategy (formerly MicroStrategy) as a case study in financial engineering. Bitcoin is down nearly 30% this year and has lost roughly half its value since peaking at $126,000 last October. Strategy owns 847,363 Bitcoin — 4% of total supply, close to 6% of active circulation — and this year alone bought 174,863 coins for $13.7bn, which JPMorgan estimates is roughly 70% of all net digital-asset inflows year to date. The buying is funded by “Stretch” (STRC), a Nasdaq-listed preferred security launched last July that pays a variable dividend management adjusts with the intention of holding the price at $100 par. Strategy has issued $10.5bn of Stretch, alongside $5bn of other preferred stock. Saylor blames the Gulf War, tighter money and capital “pulled into the AI trade” for crypto’s weakness. (Full piece is paid; the mechanics above are from the open section.)

One company is 70% of the Bitcoin bid — Rubinstein on the machine Saylor built to keep buying

Net Interest (Marc Rubinstein) · Financial Engineering · Fri 3 Jul 2026

In plain English: Strategy sells an instrument that promises a dividend it can adjust at will, priced to stay at $100. Investors hand over cash; the cash buys Bitcoin; the Bitcoin justifies the story that sells more Stretch. It works while investors trust the dividend. But if Bitcoin keeps falling, the dividend needed to hold Stretch at par rises — which raises the cost of the leverage propping up the very asset securing it. When one buyer is 70% of a market’s net inflows, the market’s price is substantially that buyer’s balance sheet — and the balance sheet is circular.

Net Interest — “Stretch Marks: A Case Study in Financial Engineering”

Rubinstein does not need to say “Ponzi-adjacent”; he lets the mechanics say it. The structure rhymes with every reflexive machine finance has built before — the CDO that bought its own tranches, the ETF that was the market for its underlying — and the tell is always the same: the instrument’s stability depends on a discretionary promise (here, a dividend management adjusts) rather than on the asset. What makes this edition of the pattern notable is scale and disclosure: it is happening on Nasdaq, in public filings, with JPMorgan measuring the circularity in real time.

“This year alone, the company bought 174,863 Bitcoin… accounting for roughly 70% of estimated net digital-asset flows year to date.”

Two threads and a discipline. Thread one: Saylor’s own excuse list — capital pulled into the AI trade — is today’s capital-drain item speaking through a different mouth; the AI hurdle rate is repricing everything from Kenyan Eurobonds to Bitcoin, and it is rare to catch the same force named by a Lagos-focused news agency and a levered Bitcoin bull in the same week. Thread two: it sharpens the stablecoin items — the boring, fully-reserved, yield-passing dollar token (Open USD) is institutionalising at the precise moment the levered, reflexive end of crypto is unwinding; the asset class is splitting into plumbing and casino, and the plumbing is winning. The discipline: for any business we underwrite that holds or touches crypto assets — including the African exchanges in today’s briefing that now lend against Bitcoin at 50% loan-to-value — the Strategy episode is the stress scenario in miniature: collateral whose marginal price is set by one levered buyer is worth less than its chart says, and margin models built on its volatility history will be wrong in the tail.

Africa · AI Infrastructure · Hyperscalers Briefing
Ecofin maps where Africa’s AI economy actually landed. Accra: Google announced Africa’s first Applied AI Lab on 1 July (applications close 31 August), capping seven years and $37m of cumulative Ghana investment since opening its first African AI research centre there in 2019. Nairobi: Microsoft’s $1bn geothermal data-centre campus with G42 has stalled on power — the project wanted 1,000MW in a country with 3,192MW installed and 2,444MW peak demand; President Ruto said authorities would need to “switch off half the country”. Lagos: Microsoft closed its 120-engineer Lagos development centre in 2024 amid 31.7% inflation and naira weakness — hyperscalers now tap Nigerian talent remotely. Johannesburg is the quiet winner: Google, Microsoft and AWS concentrate their largest cloud assets in South Africa, with Google projecting $90.6bn in economic output from its SA cloud region by 2030. Morocco plans ~2GW of data centres — four times the ~500MW deployed across the continent’s five leading markets combined.

Africa’s AI map settles: Accra gets the research, Joburg gets the compute, Lagos gets bypassed

Ecofin Agency (Fiacre E. Kakpo) · Digital · Mon 6 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: there will be no single African AI capital — the continent has specialised into research (Accra), talent (Lagos, accessed remotely), governance (Kigali), and compute (Johannesburg, the only grid hyperscalers will underwrite). The through-line to today’s capital-drain analysis is electricity: the same discipline that priced Alcoa into Hillside and Microsoft out of Olkaria makes bankable power the real industrial policy of the AI age — and South Africa, for all its faults, is the one African market that has it at scale.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Run the pre-mortem — tell the model the deal already died, and make it write the obituary

Friday’s 2×2 landed (“frameworks like that very welcome”), so here is its darker sibling. Before committing to a decision, do not ask the model whether it is a good idea — it will hedge. Instead, move the clock forward and declare failure as fact: “It is 2029. This investment has failed badly. Write the post-mortem.” The tense does the work. Asked for risks, a model produces a generic list; told the failure already happened, it must construct a specific causal chain — which assumption broke first, what the early warning was, who saw it and stayed quiet. Psychologists call this prospective hindsight; it reliably surfaces failure modes that risk lists miss, because it forces a story rather than a category.

A worked example

On a live opportunity, prompt: “It is July 2029. Chronos invested in this company in 2026 and has just written the position down to zero. Write the two-page internal post-mortem: the single root cause, the three earliest warning signs we ignored, the moment intervention was still possible, and the one question we should have asked in diligence but did not. Be specific to this business, not generic. UK English, no preamble.” Then run it twice more with different root causes forced (“the failure was regulatory”, “the failure was the founder”) and compare which obituary reads most plausible — that is where the diligence hours should go. Finish by asking for the pre-mortem’s mirror: what did the 2029 write-up of the deal we didn’t do say it cost us to pass?
