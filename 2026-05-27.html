Africa & EM · Kenya Finance Bill 2026
Kenya's Finance Bill 2026, tabled in parliament on 25 May, introduces a 15% capital gains tax on indirect transfers — gains made by non-resident investors selling shares offshore where the value derives from Kenyan assets. The Mauritius / Cayman / Delaware exit route, used by virtually every African-focused fund vehicle for the last decade, just got named. Egypt and Nigeria have already moved; Kenya is now the third African jurisdiction to close this gap in twelve months.

Kenya proposes 15% capital gains tax on offshore VC and PE exits

Adonijah Ndege · TechCabal · Mon 25 May 2026
Indirect-transfer CGT on non-resident sales of shares deriving value from local assets

Kenya's Finance Bill 2026, tabled by Treasury secretary John Mbadi on Sunday, would allow the KRA to tax gains made by non-resident investors selling shares abroad if those shares derive value from Kenyan assets — closing the offshore-holding-company exit route used by foreign VC and PE. ICPAK has warned the wording extends beyond traditional asset sales to capital raises, group restructurings, and internal reorganisations at holding-company level. Bill goes to second reading in June.

TechCabal — Adonijah Ndege

The structural read is straightforward: the offshore-holding-company structure has been the standard exit vehicle for African PE for at least a decade. Mauritius for Anglo-Africa, Luxembourg for Francophone Africa, Cayman for some US-LP-driven structures. The arbitrage was that capital gains accrued at the holding-company level were taxed at zero, while the underlying African operating asset's gain was, in principle, never recognised inside the host jurisdiction. Kenya's Bill, like Nigeria's earlier this year, names the structure and taxes the indirect transfer at 15%.

The chart shows the broader picture. South Africa and Egypt have had CGT in this band for years. Kenya and Nigeria — the two largest VC-active markets — are pulling their effective rates onto the same plane in 2026. Mauritius remains the safe harbour for now, but the Bill's wording on "value-derived-from" is broad enough that an aggressive KRA can challenge most pass-through structures regardless of intermediate-vehicle location.

"The provision may create Kenyan CGT exposure for offshore investor exits, capital raising transactions, group restructurings and internal reorganisations undertaken at holding company level." — ICPAK submission to parliament

For Chronos and the Lima fundraise specifically, this lands on the structuring conversation we're already inside. The Werksmans opinion is built around a Mauritius vehicle; the analogous Kenyan precedent doesn't yet apply (we don't hold Kenyan operating assets directly), but the direction-of-travel matters for LP underwriting of African PE generally. The LPs we're talking to in Singapore and the GCC will see this within days and ask the question. The right framing is: this confirms African jurisdictions are professionalising their tax base, which long-term improves PE returns by reducing repricing risk, but short-term raises the cost of structuring.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 002's TymeBank FT placement, Issue 004's Rank Capital, Issue 008's $1.5T mobile-money chart, Issue 009's $887M H1 VC chart all reflected the maturation of the African private-capital ecosystem. Today's piece adds the regulatory-and-tax dimension. The category is being formalised top-down and bottom-up simultaneously.

Africa & EM · African Fintech M&A
dLocal's $23.7m AZA Finance asset deal closed on 27 February 2026; the disclosure dropped in dLocal's Q1 SEC filing this week. The deal teaches you what an African payments licence and customer book is actually worth in 2026 — and how a $150m round-priced asset becomes a $23.7m debt-cancellation when regulatory delays stretch out. Customer relationships valued at $14.2m; the Cameroonian payment licence at $120k. Read carefully if you're underwriting any African payments asset.

dLocal's AZA Finance acquisition closes as $23.7m asset deal — settled entirely through debt cancellation

Emmanuel Nwosu · TechCabal · Tue 26 May 2026

dLocal's Q1 2026 SEC filing reveals the final structure of its long-running AZA Finance acquisition. Originally announced in June 2025 at a reported $150m valuation, the deal was restructured around three assets — Mint Code Solutions (Cameroon payments entity, $120k), NeWurth IP ($2.05m), and AZA's African customer relationships ($14.2m). Total consideration: $23.7m, settled entirely by cancelling $22.29m principal and $1.96m interest on two loans dLocal had previously extended to AZA. No cash changed hands. The largest line item, customer relationships, dominates — 60% of the purchase price.

TechCabal — Emmanuel Nwosu

The numbers reveal what 2026 African payments M&A actually pays for. A regulated payment licence in Cameroon: $120,000. The IP behind the AZA Finance brand and tech: $2.05m. The customer book: $14.2m. The rest ($6.6m) is goodwill — expected synergies from regional integration. The dominant value driver is the customer relationships, which is consistent with the broader pattern: in fragmented African payments, scaling distribution is the hard problem, not the licence.

The deal-restructure mechanics matter too. dLocal originally extended two short-term credit facilities (December 2024 at 7%, June 2025 at 15%) with a conversion option attached. The jump from 7% to 15% on the second facility tracks dLocal's revised view of AZA's risk profile during the acquisition negotiation. When a third party filed a complaint that derailed the original deal, dLocal simply exercised the conversion option in January 2026 and took the assets at face value of the outstanding debt. The seller received nothing in cash. This is the kind of structuring that works when one party has a real walk-away option.

"It would definitely have taken us a lot longer to build all of that without having gotten this acquisition across the finishing line." — Pedro Arnt, dLocal CEO, Q1 2026 earnings call

For Chronos portfolio context: Crossfin's payment-rail customer book and Optasia's distribution into African telcos are the assets that, in a comparable transaction, would carry similar customer-relationship multiples. The dLocal precedent informs the framing for any conversation about strategic value of those books — and also for any conversation about how regulatory delays compress headline valuations into ground-truth value.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 009's $887M H1 VC chart showed funding flowing to fewer, larger deals. Today's piece shows what those larger deals look like at the realisation end — and how often the realisation deviates from the announcement. The category is maturing, but the price discovery is rough.

AI Frontier · Benedict Evans
Ben Evans's essay on Sunday makes the sharpest case yet that the entire AI-job-exposure modelling industry is fooling itself. Three back-tests — accountants under spreadsheet automation, journalists under internet decoupling, taxi drivers under smartphone GPS — show why the static O*NET-style job-mapping approach misses the actual mechanisms by which technology rewrites work. Worth reading before any conversation about portfolio company AI-deployment strategy.

Benedict Evans: predicting AI job exposure is an exercise in predicting the unpredictable

Benedict Evans · ben-evans.com essay · Sun 24 May 2026

Evans argues that the AI-job-exposure modelling industry — a wave of papers and viral charts crunching O*NET data against LLM capabilities — is structurally wrong. Three mechanisms break the models: (1) Jevons paradox means automation can grow demand, not shrink it (accountants grew through a century of automation); (2) businesses can be disrupted underneath jobs that themselves don't change (the internet broke journalism by removing classified-ad subsidies, not by changing what journalists do); and (3) entirely new categories appear unpredictably (smartphone GPS didn't show up in 2005 taxi-exposure models — but Uber did). The essay invokes the Aaron Levie variant of Gell-Mann Amnesia: you correctly intuit your own field is too subtle for current AI, then assume everyone else's field is simpler than it is.

Ben Evans — Predicting AI job exposure

Evans's three back-tests are the strongest part. The CPA chart — fifty years of relentless financial automation while the number of accountants kept going up — is the kind of unintuitive empirical anchor that should make anyone humble about predicting AI exposure. The newspapers / A&R-scouts decoupling story is even sharper: the job didn't change, the business model did. Most of the disruption was invisible from inside the job description.

For our portcos and our underwriting, two practical implications. First, when we evaluate an investment thesis that depends on "AI replaces X cost centre", we should systematically ask the three Evans tests: does it pass the CPA test (does cheaper analysis mean less analysis or more)? Does it pass the newspaper test (is the business that pays for this job actually affected, regardless of whether the job itself is)? Does it pass the Uber test (could a re-arrangement of adjacent technology reshape the category entirely)?

"It really does depend. As Yogi Berra said, 'it's tough to make predictions, especially about the future.'" — Benedict Evans, 24 May 2026

Second, the Aaron Levie Gell-Mann Amnesia point is the most useful internal disciplinary lens. Every time a partner looks at a Claude-generated McKinsey-style slide deck and thinks "consulting is dead," it's worth remembering that a McKinsey partner looking at the same artifact would see how shallow the slide is, just as we see how shallow an AI-generated PE deal memo is. The boundary of AI competence in any field is invisible from outside that field. This is why generalist take-rate predictions are unreliable, and why the most useful AI-deployment work happens inside operating teams, not in advisor decks.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 005's agentic-workflows case studies showed AI landing in production. Issue 008's Andrew Ng piece argued open-source models catch frontier on practical tasks. Today's Evans piece adds the counter-discipline: even with all that production traction, you cannot model who wins which job. The honest framing is "watch the operating data; trust the modelling much less."

Africa & EM · Telecom Infra
IHS Towers' board endorsed MTN's $8.50/share, $2.2bn take-private offer last Tuesday (filed 19 May, board recommendation 25 May). Combined with the Wendel and MTN voting blocks already locked, the deal has more than 40% of voting rights pre-committed against a two-thirds threshold. NYSE delisting is the practical certainty. This compresses the African digital-infrastructure ownership map further and lands shortly after Safaricom and Airtel both moved to take their own infrastructure in-house.

IHS Towers board backs MTN's $2.2bn take-private — Wendel and MTN blocs already 40% locked

Frank Eleanya · TechCabal · Mon 25 May 2026

IHS Holding's Schedule 13E-3 SEC filing on 19 May confirmed an EGM in London later this year on MTN's offer of $8.50/share cash. The board recommendation followed on Monday. MTN's existing 21.1% stake and Wendel's 19.6% are pre-committed via the February 2026 voting support agreement, putting more than 40% of votes in favour against a 67% threshold. If approved, IHS delists from NYSE and becomes a wholly-owned MTN subsidiary — ending its five-year run as a public infrastructure company since the 2021 IPO. The framing is consistent with Airtel's 2024 launch of Airtel Africa Fibre and Safaricom's August 2025 move to take direct control of its tower power systems: African telcos are reclaiming the infrastructure layer.

TechCabal — Frank Eleanya

The structural read on this is the same one that's been compounding through the year. The independent African tower-operator model was a 2010s thesis built on the assumption that telcos would permanently outsource passive infrastructure to specialist consolidators because the capital intensity was high and the operational complexity was specialised. Both halves of that assumption have weakened. Solar-and-battery economics have compressed the operational complexity (Safaricom now runs 1,500+ solar-powered sites directly). And telco share prices in 2025-26 have suggested investors value vertical integration of digital infrastructure more than independence.

For the portfolio, the read into rain is direct. rain owns its 5G infrastructure and runs a vertically-integrated model. The market is moving toward that model — independent tower operators are getting absorbed back into the telco stack. The strategic position rain is in (own the rails, own the customer, own the spectrum) is increasingly the consensus model rather than the contrarian one.

"The board unanimously authorised and approved the execution, delivery and performance of the merger agreement." — IHS Holding SEC filing, 19 May 2026

For the wider Chronos thesis on African digital infrastructure: this consolidation reduces the number of independent investible targets in the tower-operator category, but increases the strategic value of any operator that owns the integrated stack. The opportunity set is narrowing and concentrating at the same time.

Africa & EM · Compliance Tech
Smartcomply, a Nigerian compliance-tech startup, launched its Adhere AML platform in the UK on Tuesday. The reverse-direction expansion (Africa-built infrastructure servicing London EMIs and neobanks on African payment corridors) is a category the partners haven't seen surfaced before. The platform monitors $1bn/month in African transaction volume and reports 70% reduction in manual compliance workload and 40% drop in false fraud alerts.

Smartcomply takes its AI-powered AML platform to London — Africa-built compliance going north

Opeyemi Kareem · TechCabal · Tue 26 May 2026

Lagos-based Smartcomply launched its Adhere AML/KYC platform in the UK on Tuesday, targeting Electronic Money Institutions, remittance firms, neobanks, and cross-border payment fintechs operating on African corridors (Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, SA, Rwanda). The pitch: existing UK compliance providers (ComplyAdvantage, Sumsub, Smile Identity) built their models on European and North American financial behaviour and adapted to Africa secondarily; Smartcomply built for African data first. Direct integration with Nigerian BVN/NIN databases, mobile-money behavioural models, regional partner network for East and Francophone Africa identity datasets. Adhere now monitors over $1bn in monthly African transaction volume; customers report a 70% reduction in manual compliance workload and 40% drop in false fraud alerts. Aligned with CBN's March 2026 baseline standards for automated AML.

TechCabal — Opeyemi Kareem

The interesting move here is the direction of capability transfer. The standard African fintech narrative since 2020 has been imported-product-distributed-locally: Stripe, Adyen, Visa, Sumsub bringing their stacks to Africa and adapting at the edges. Smartcomply's reverse-direction expansion — Africa-built compliance shipping to London because that's where the African-corridor counterparties sit — inverts the assumption that capability flows one way. It also addresses a specific pain point: UK and EU institutions de-risking African corridors entirely because their compliance tooling can't price African transaction behaviour, so they pull back rather than absorb the false-positive cost.

For the Chronos AI/fintech thesis, this is a clean data-moat story. The AML model quality is downstream of the training data quality, and Smartcomply's training data is structurally different from a London-built model's because it includes mobile-money flow patterns, BVN/NIN-anchored identity graphs, and West African mule-network typologies. The model edge isn't algorithmic; it's a data-asset edge. That's the right kind of moat for an AI-deployment story to have.

"Compliance technology designed in New York or London cannot read Nigerian Bank Verification Numbers, cannot understand mobile money flows in Kenya, and cannot make sense of West African mule networks." — Anita Ajalla, Smartcomply CTO

The category architecture for African digital finance is being built piece-by-piece — and increasingly, the pieces are being built in Africa for export.

Operator Craft · Portfolio Signal
Salvador Anglada, Optasia Group CEO, published an essay in Gadget yesterday arguing the African fintech "land grab" era is over and that the next chapter will be written by operators whose systems don't fail the people who can least afford it. Surfaced not as advocacy but as a signal — what the CEO of one of our portcos is publicly saying about the sector's evolution, and how that frames the operating posture inside Optasia.

Salvador Anglada (Optasia): "trust is infrastructure" — the African fintech land-grab era is over

Salvador Anglada, Optasia Group CEO · Gadget · Tue 26 May 2026

Anglada's Gadget essay argues that 2024's funding correction has decisively ended the African fintech "land grab" era. Capital is back (2025 up almost 50% to $1.64bn) but on different terms — proven models, structural partnerships, debt and asset-backed facilities, rejection of platform-wars territorialism. Three substantive arguments: (1) trust is infrastructure that compounds in small increments, lost in an instant, and the SARB's data on banked South Africans withdrawing all funds on payday reveals where the system has failed it; (2) binary credit AI models exclude the very users fintech is meant to serve, and the answer is dynamic-affordability behavioural models; (3) the standalone-platform era is over — the new model is comparative-advantage partnerships between telcos, banks, retailers and specialist infrastructure providers.

Gadget — Salvador Anglada

Two things are useful here. First, what Anglada chooses to argue publicly tells you what Optasia's operating doctrine is: dynamic-affordability over binary credit, partnerships over land-grab, trust as a compounding-not-headline asset. The doctrine is consistent with the unit-economics story Optasia has told inside the FirstRand stake-increase and the management commentary on telco partnerships. The public framing is consistent with the operating model — there's no mismatch.

Second, the structural argument matters independently. The "trust is infrastructure" framing is a more disciplined version of the standard inclusion narrative. It treats trust as a measurable, compoundable substrate that can be built or destroyed by specific operational decisions (transparent pricing, ethical credit explainability, dispute resolution, system uptime). That's actionable in a way that "inclusion" usually isn't — and it's the same substrate Marks and Klarman have been talking about for capital markets generally, applied to consumer financial infrastructure.

"African fintech's next chapter will not be written by the fastest movers. It will be written by the operators whose systems don't fail the people who can least afford it." — Salvador Anglada, 26 May 2026

For Chronos and the wider portfolio: the read across is that the partners running our operating companies are converging on a "discipline beats velocity" model. The boards should be checking that the operating doctrine being executed inside each portco matches what the CEO is willing to put their name to publicly. Where the public framing and the operating reality diverge is usually where the most useful board questions live.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Use Claude as a sceptic, not a cheerleader — explicitly

The default failure mode of every LLM is agreement. If you paste a draft IC paper, a deal thesis, or a board memo into Claude with no other instruction, you'll get back something that sounds like a polite consultant nodding. That's not what the model is best at. The most useful prompt pattern is to explicitly cast Claude as a sceptic — "find the three weakest assumptions in this paper", "argue the bear case", "find the contradiction between paragraph two and paragraph five" — and then read what comes back as the start of a debate, not the end of one. The model is good at scepticism if you ask for it; it just won't volunteer.

Three sceptic prompts to keep in the back pocket

"(1) 'You are an experienced PE partner sceptical of every African fintech thesis. Read this memo and tell me the three assumptions most likely to break, ranked by the size of the break.' (2) 'A regulator from country X is reading this. What's the single sentence that would make them open an investigation?' (3) 'I have three weeks to find the one fact that kills this deal. Where would you look first, and what's the second-most-likely place if the first is clean?'"
