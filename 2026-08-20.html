Nigeria · South Africa · Fintech M&A Analysis Deal structure
Standard Bank Group — South Africa's largest lender, market capitalisation R534bn ($33bn) — is in talks to take a stake in Chinese-backed OPay ahead of the fintech's planned US initial public offering, at which it is seeking a $4bn valuation. The reporting is Bloomberg's, carried by TechCabal on Wednesday. OPay is working with Citigroup, Deutsche Bank and JPMorgan to list in New York later this year. The company is the Nigeria-focused operator behind the green handheld point-of-sale terminals that became the country's default cash network; it is backed by SoftBank and Sequoia and has scaled to over 50 million users. Standard Bank comes to the table from a position of strength rather than distress: it reported record H1 2026 headline earnings of R26.1bn ($1.59bn). The precedent TechCabal reaches for is the FirstRand and Optasia partnership of October 2025 — the same trade, structured as a commercial arrangement rather than equity. What a pre-IPO stake buys is specific: entry at a private mark before a public one is set, and a standing relationship with the distribution layer, rather than a competitive posture toward it.

Standard Bank decides it is cheaper to buy into the terminal network than to build one — a pre-IPO stake in OPay's 50m users ahead of a $4bn New York listing

TechCabal · TC Daily · Emmanuel Nwosu · Wed 19 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a large African bank taking a pre-IPO position in a large African fintech. The one that matters is an incumbent conceding, in the only language that is not cheap talk, that the distribution layer cannot be rebuilt at a competitive price. Standard Bank has the balance sheet, the licences and the engineering budget to build a Nigerian agent network. It is choosing not to. And the timing is the tell — buying before the listing means paying a negotiated price rather than a marked one, which is what you do when you believe the public mark will be higher and when the strategic value to you exceeds the financial value to a passive holder.

TechCabal Daily — “Standard Bank wants a piece of fintech company OPay”

The structural read is that Africa's banks have stopped trying to win the distribution argument and started trying to buy their way out of it. The competitive story of the last decade was that fintechs would disintermediate banks; the story of this one is that banks are taking equity in the disintermediators, and doing it early enough to matter. The economics are not subtle. OPay's terminals became Nigeria's cash network because banks let their own machines fail — that was yesterday's item 2, and it is the direct antecedent of today's. Having lost the channel, an incumbent has three options: rebuild it at enormous cost against an entrenched incumbent, partner with the winner commercially, or own a piece of the winner. FirstRand chose the second with Optasia. Standard Bank is reaching for the third, which is the more expensive option and the more durable one, because a commercial partnership can be renegotiated at renewal and a shareholding cannot be taken away. Note also what a pre-IPO stake implies about Standard Bank's read on the listing: you buy before the market prices something when you think the market will price it higher, and you accept illiquidity to do so. That is a directional statement about African fintech valuations from a party with unusually good information about African fintech cash flows.

An incumbent that buys equity in the channel rather than rebuilding it has made a public admission about the replacement cost of distribution. The price of the stake is the price of that admission.

The threads: to Issue 067's item 1 (Absa's R8.78bn technology bill, 28% of its cost base, and another R200m of software written to zero) — the two cards are the same decision taken opposite ways within a week, and the Absa result is the strongest available evidence for why Standard Bank might prefer to buy. To Issue 067's item 2 (Nigeria's agent network and the CBN's reversal of it) — the asset Standard Bank is buying into is the one whose regulatory position deteriorated four quarters ago, with agents now capped at a single terminal provider, a 70-metre radius and a ₦1.2m daily limit; buying the channel just as the regulator finishes constraining it is either good timing on a repriced asset or bad timing on a structurally impaired one, and the difference is the whole trade. To Issue 065's item 1 (Standard Bank stating it wants to own more of Africa's digital payment flows, holding roughly 30% of South African cross-border value) — that stated ambition now has a transaction attached to it, and the direction of travel is inorganic. To the FirstRand–Optasia arrangement TechCabal names as precedent — the read-across for our own view of that structure is that the commercial-partnership form is being tested against the equity form in real time, by two comparable institutions, and we will have a clean comparison of the two within eighteen months. The sceptic's ledger: this is reported talks, not a signed deal, and pre-IPO conversations fail routinely; a minority stake in a company that will shortly be majority-controlled by public shareholders buys influence, not control, and Standard Bank has no obvious path to consolidating OPay; the $4bn target valuation is the company's ask, not a cleared price, and a US listing by a Chinese-backed Nigerian fintech faces a political and disclosure environment that could move that number substantially; OPay's 50m users is a registration count, not an activity count, and the CBN's agent restrictions bear directly on how many of them transact; and a bank taking a strategic equity position in a listed-to-be fintech is a trade that has been run before in other markets with mixed results, most of them dependent on whether the bank could actually integrate anything. The forward marker: whether the stake, if it closes, comes with commercial terms attached — distribution, settlement, deposit-taking — or is purely financial. A purely financial stake in a company you could have partnered with is an expensive way to express an opinion.

South Africa · Energy · Industrial Policy Analysis Chart of the day
Electricity minister Kgosientsho Ramokgopa, with Eskom chairman Mteto Nyati and chief executive Dan Marokane, introduced a new smelter pricing model on Tuesday 18 August. The framing was historical and blunt: South Africa once ran 66 smelters; 11 remain operational — 17% utilisation. Under the new tariff the government projects that number rises to 49 by the end of 2027, or 74% utilisation, creating and preserving 11,448 direct and 121,392 indirect jobs. The funding mechanism is the striking part: no new money is requested. The R10bn outstanding from the final tranche of Eskom's debt-relief programme is redirected to the intervention, on the argument that the resulting tax base leaves the fiscus about R5.5bn better off. Government further expects R20bn of raw mineral expenditure, R76bn in export earnings, and R17.9bn of additional revenue to Eskom from smelters that are, as Daily Maverick notes, intense 24/7 consumers. The context is deindustrialisation: manufacturing's share of GDP has fallen from around 22% in the late 1990s to roughly 12% today. Ramokgopa's position is that this is not a subsidy — "we're making them more competitive" against China — and Nyati's that it is "not charity, it is strategic stewardship". The dissenting voice is Transalloys, the last operating manganese smelter, which halted production on 1 July with 600 jobs at risk after being excluded from the ferrochrome arrangement. Chief executive Konstantin Sadovnik welcomed the focus but warned: "Policy direction alone, however, does not address the immediate crisis."

Eskom stops being the constraint and starts being the instrument — R10bn of unspent debt relief redirected to restart 38 dead smelters, on an arithmetic that says the state ends up ahead

Daily Maverick · Business Maverick · Lindsey Schutters · Wed 19 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is an industrial electricity tariff with a job number attached. The one that matters is a state utility being explicitly repurposed from a rationing mechanism into an allocation mechanism, and the allocation being justified on a net-fiscal argument rather than an industrial-policy one. That is the interesting move. Ramokgopa is not arguing that smelters deserve support; he is arguing that supporting them costs the Treasury nothing, because the tax and the Eskom revenue exceed the foregone debt relief. If the arithmetic holds it is a genuinely elegant piece of policy design. If it does not, the country has quietly converted a debt-reduction commitment into an operating subsidy and told itself otherwise.

Every number to the right of “today” is a projection — and the only firm figure on the page is the R10bn that stops reducing debt
Daily Maverick — “Eskom's three tenors sing a song of reindustrialisation under new pricing model”

The structural read is that this is a cross-subsidy that has been re-labelled as a financing arbitrage, and the re-labelling is doing real work. Consider what actually changes. Smelters get electricity below the price they would otherwise pay. Somebody funds that gap. The government's answer is that nobody funds it — the smelters' own consumption generates R17.9bn of Eskom revenue that would not otherwise exist, and their tax generates R5.5bn that would not otherwise exist, so the R10bn of foregone debt reduction is recovered with change. That claim rests entirely on counterfactual demand: it is true only if those smelters would genuinely not have operated at the standard tariff. For the 38 that are currently cold, that is close to demonstrable — they are cold. For the 11 still running, it is an assumption, and if any of them would have kept running anyway then that portion of the tariff is pure transfer with no offsetting revenue. The Transalloys detail is the one to sit with, because it exposes the mechanism's real defect. The last manganese smelter in the country stopped in July because it was outside the ferrochrome arrangement. A pricing model that operates deal-by-deal, sector-by-sector, produces exactly this: firms whose survival depends on which negotiation they were included in. That is not a tariff. It is a queue, and queues get gamed.

A tariff negotiated sector by sector is not a price. It is an allocation, and every firm outside the room learns that the thing to invest in next is not a furnace but a lobbyist.

The threads: to Issue 067's item 5 (Nvidia arranging for six capital pools to hold $500bn of AI factories) — the mirrored decision on who carries an asset that its user cannot fund, with Nvidia pushing the balance sheet out to third parties and the South African state pulling it in; both are answers to the same question and the state's version has no residual-value support and no six independent underwriters. To Issue 067's item 2 (the CBN reassigning Nigeria's cash channel by decree) — the same instrument, regulation as market design, applied to electricity rather than payments, and on a comparable timescale. To Issue 066's item on Spar and SAA losing their captains while becalmed — the through-line is South African industrial assets whose problems are structural and whose fixes keep arriving as governance events; this one at least arrives as a price. To the Chronos read on portfolio energy exposure — the transferable question for any South African industrial holding is not "what is our tariff" but "were we in the room", because on the evidence of Transalloys the tariff you get is a function of the negotiation you were included in, and that is a relationship risk rather than a commodity risk. The sceptic's ledger: 11,448 direct and 121,392 indirect jobs is a suspiciously precise pair of numbers for a projection that depends on global manganese and chrome prices nobody in the room controls; the fiscal arithmetic nets a certain R10bn against uncertain future receipts and would not survive the discount rate a private investor would apply; restarting a cold smelter is capital-intensive and slow, so 49 by end-2027 assumes both the capital and the commodity cycle cooperate; the "not a subsidy, competitiveness" framing is the standard formulation for a subsidy and should be read as such regardless of whether the policy is sound; and Eskom's improved financial trajectory, which is what freed the R10bn, is itself recent and not yet proven across a full tariff cycle. The forward marker: whether Transalloys receives a tariff, and on what terms. It is the cleanest test available of whether this is a rule or a series of favours.

South Africa · Telecoms · Regulation Briefing Thesis test
South Africa's mobile operators — MTN, Vodacom, Telkom, rain, Cell C and Liquid Intelligent Technologies — are required to stop charging customers for data used to reach eligible content from public benefit organisations by 15 January 2027. The obligation is not new law; it is a licence condition. It dates to the 2022 spectrum auction, in which ICASA sold additional radio frequency capacity and collected more than R14.4bn, attaching social obligations to the award. Zero-rating public-benefit content was one of them. The problem is delivery. The DG Murray Trust warns that operators are running out of time and that implementation is lagging: on its count, only 15 organisations have been zero-rated across the major operators. Qualification is not automatic — an organisation providing education, healthcare, employment support or comparable social services must apply to ICASA and have its content assessed before it qualifies. The underlying case is affordability rather than coverage: ICASA reported that more than 89% of rural populations had 3G and 4G/LTE coverage in every province in 2025, while South African data prices remain higher than in 27 other African countries. Zero-rating does not make the content available offline; it moves the cost of the data from the user to the operator or a sponsor.

Six operators have five months to make thousands of websites free — a 2022 spectrum condition matures into a cost line, and only 15 organisations have cleared the process

TechCabal · TC Daily · Yemi Kareem & Zia Yusuf · Wed 19 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a consumer-friendly deadline that most operators will meet in some fashion. The one that matters is the delayed-cost structure of spectrum — an auction that reads as a clean R14.4bn transaction in 2022 turns out to have been a transaction plus a set of obligations whose cost lands four and five years later, in a different rate environment, on a different management team. Read the 15 organisations figure carefully before assuming operator obstruction: qualification runs through an ICASA assessment process, so a bottleneck at the regulator and a bottleneck at the operator produce identical-looking numbers, and the distinction determines who is actually exposed to the deadline.

TechCabal Daily — “South Africa's mobile operators have five months to make websites free”

The structural read is that spectrum is sold as an asset and settled as a liability, and the industry's accounting for it consistently understates the second half. In 2022 the sector paid R14.4bn and booked capacity. What it also acquired, and could not easily quantify, was a stream of open-ended social obligations denominated not in rands but in future compliance — obligations whose eventual cost depends on how many organisations apply, how broad ICASA's definition of public benefit proves to be, and how much traffic the qualifying content ultimately attracts. That last variable is the one with no ceiling. An operator can model the cost of zero-rating fifteen sites. It cannot model the cost of zero-rating "thousands", because zero-rated content is content whose consumption is no longer disciplined by price, and demand for a free good is not a straightforward extrapolation of demand for a priced one. The affordability data is what makes this more than an administrative story: coverage above 89% in rural areas against pricing worse than 27 other African markets says plainly that South Africa's connectivity problem migrated from infrastructure to tariff some time ago, and that the regulator has concluded the tariff will not fix itself.

Spectrum obligations are priced at auction by people who will not be in the job when they fall due. The bill arrives five years later, denominated in traffic nobody modelled.

The threads: to Issue 067's yesterday-thread on ICASA holding the data-rollover rule from January 2027 — the same regulator, the same effective date, the same direction of travel, which together amount to a deliberate compression of operator revenue per gigabyte from two sides at once: unused data must be returned to the customer, and a growing category of used data must not be charged for at all. Any model of South African mobile ARPU that treats these as separate rulings is missing the pattern. To Issue 066's item on Airtel renting the sky over Congo rather than building towers — the counterpoint on where telecom capital should sit, and a reminder that infrastructure-light operators carry these obligations differently from infrastructure-heavy ones. To the Chronos portfolio — rain is named in the operator list, and the transferable question is one of relative burden rather than absolute cost: a data-only operator with no voice revenue to cross-subsidise absorbs a zero-rating obligation differently from an MTN or a Vodacom, and the deadline is common to all six regardless of that asymmetry. Worth understanding what has actually been zero-rated, what is in the ICASA queue, and where the traffic assumption sits, well before January. The sceptic's ledger: fifteen organisations is a DGMT count and an advocacy organisation has an interest in the number looking low; the qualification process is genuinely a two-sided bottleneck and the reporting does not separate ICASA's throughput from operator implementation; zero-rating a public-benefit website is not obviously expensive in aggregate, since the traffic is small relative to video and social, and the headline "thousands of websites" may be a very modest data volume; the obligation was known and priced in 2022, so this is a scheduled cost rather than a new one; and five months is a workable runway for a technical implementation that is, at bottom, a routing rule. The forward marker: whether ICASA publishes its assessment backlog. Without that number the fifteen tells us nothing about who is behind.

South Africa · Tax · Systems Architecture Briefing Thesis test
The South African Revenue Service has published a proposal for a digital VAT model that would change how businesses issue invoices, transmit VAT information and retain records. Three components: e-Invoicing, converting the invoice itself into a structured digital record; e-Reporting, transmitting that record to SARS through an online portal; and an Interoperability Framework, the translation layer allowing accounting software, invoicing systems and SARS's systems to communicate. The effect is that SARS receives transaction data at or near the moment of sale, rather than waiting for a periodic return and reconciling afterwards. The numbers that motivate it: VAT is a principal revenue source, with SARS collecting roughly R500bn ($30bn) in VAT in the 2025/26 financial year against total net revenue that exceeded R2 trillion — and research points to a 40.6% reporting gap, making under-reporting, fraud and leakage an expensive problem. The stated trade for business is less reconciliation and lower manual compliance over time. This remains a proposal: businesses, accounting software vendors and other stakeholders have until 16 October to comment, including on what safeguards implementation requires.

SARS proposes to move inside the invoice — near-real-time VAT reporting against a 40.6% gap, with the comment window closing 16 October

TechCabal · TC Daily · Yemi Kareem & Zia Yusuf · Wed 19 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a tax-administration consultation with a comment deadline. The one that matters is the state acquiring a near-live feed of private commercial activity as a by-product of collecting a tax — and the by-product being considerably more valuable than the tax. A revenue service that sees invoices as they are issued is no longer only a collector; it holds one of the better real-time economic datasets in the country, ahead of Stats SA, ahead of the Reserve Bank, ahead of the firms generating the data. The 40.6% reporting gap is the justification and it is a serious one. The design question the comment period exists to answer is what else the feed may be used for, and by whom.

TechCabal Daily — “South Africa's taxman proposes new value-added tax rules”

The structural read is that this is the tax-authority instance of a pattern we have now watched in payments, cloud and identity across three continents: the state moving from auditing a system after the fact to sitting inside it in real time. E-invoicing is not a South African invention — it has been rolled out across Latin America and much of Europe, generally with meaningful compliance gains and generally with a difficult first two years for small businesses. The mechanics are worth being precise about, because "SARS gets your invoices" understates the change. Under a periodic return, the taxpayer prepares a position and the authority tests it; the taxpayer controls the narrative and the burden of disproof sits with the auditor. Under structured e-invoicing, the authority holds the primary record and the taxpayer explains deviations from it; the burden inverts. That inversion is the entire compliance gain, and it is also the entire reason the comment period matters. For any business with complex intercompany invoicing, credit notes, or timing differences between economic and accounting events, the question is not whether SARS may see the data but whether the schema can represent the transaction faithfully. Badly specified schemas do not merely inconvenience; they make correct transactions look like errors, at scale, automatically.

Real-time reporting does not change what the taxman may ask. It changes who has to explain — and it changes it for every transaction rather than the few that get sampled.

The threads: to Issue 067's item 2 (Nigeria's central bank rewriting the cash channel by regulation) and to today's items 2 and 3 — four regulators in two countries, each reassigning a cost or a burden of proof by administrative instrument rather than legislation, on timescales of one to four quarters. To Issue 065's item on Nigeria collecting $19.9bn in taxes as digital systems expand, and to the $94m-a-day figure that ran alongside it — the same thesis, that African revenue authorities are among the continent's most effective adopters of transactional technology, and that the fiscal consequences arrive faster than the political debate about them. To Issue 064's Nigerian creator-economy tax item — the pattern of tax bases being broadened by instrumentation rather than by rate. To the Chronos portfolio — the transferable question is a systems one rather than a tax one: which of our operating companies could emit a structured, schema-valid invoice for every transaction today, and which would need a finance-system project to do it? On the evidence of comparable rollouts elsewhere, the companies that suffer are not the non-compliant ones but the ones whose billing logic lives in spreadsheets and exception handling. Sixteen weeks to comment, and a rollout that will run years after that. The sceptic's ledger: this is a proposal, and South African tax proposals have a long history of consultation followed by substantial redesign or quiet shelving; the 40.6% reporting gap comes from a bottom-up academic estimate rather than a SARS measurement and such estimates vary widely by method; e-invoicing regimes elsewhere have taken five to ten years to reach full coverage, so no near-term revenue effect should be assumed; SARS's own systems capability is the binding constraint and has been the binding constraint on several previous modernisation efforts; and the compliance-cost reduction promised to business is real in steady state but arrives only after an implementation cost that falls hardest on the smallest firms. The forward marker: whether the October consultation produces a published schema. A framework without a schema is a direction of travel; a schema is a project plan.

Kenya · Venture Capital · Talent Flow Analysis Thesis test
TechCabal has traced the founders of ten Kenyan startups that collapsed, entered administration or abandoned their core business over the past five years, using regulatory and corporate filings, LinkedIn, social posts and speaking engagements. The ten raised more than $500m combined: Copia $123m, Gro Intelligence more than $117m, KOKO Networks over $100m, with Sendy, MarketForce and Lipa Later raising tens of millions each. Sendy, founded 2015 by Mesh Alloys, Evanson Biwott, Don Okoth and Malaika Judd, raised at least $26.5m disclosed — Toyota Tsusho, Atlantica Ventures, Enza Capital, Sunu Capital — and was valued at over $80m before a down round collapsed when a key investor withdrew; it announced closure in August 2023. Where they went: Alloys chairs Boya, sits at Enza Capital as entrepreneur-in-residence, and founded tabb in December 2024, which connects banks, suppliers and businesses so suppliers are paid immediately while buyers settle later. Biwott co-founded AfroQuality, now in Kenya, Rwanda and Ghana. Okoth founded RTM Africa within months, then Wavu in aquaculture, joined Antler as EIR, founded Revazi in 2025, and rejoined Alloys as a director of tabb in January 2026. Judd moved to Ireland and is managing director of the NDRC, Ireland's national accelerator. iProcure, founded 2013, raised $17.2m over ten rounds including a $10.2m Series B in 2022 from Novastar, British International Investment and Safaricom's Spark Fund, and entered administration on 26 April 2024 with KPMG appointed; more than two years later it remains under administration. Its founders went to enterprise software, agritech advisory, and — in the case of CTO Patrick Wanjohi — to iPOS, a new company built on the point-of-sale technology the administrators sold in November 2024, now operating across Kenya, Uganda and Tanzania.

$500m of Kenyan venture capital written off, and almost every founder is building again inside two years — including one whose technology outlived the company through the administrator

TechCabal · People · Adonijah Ndege · Wed 19 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a where-are-they-now feature about failed startups. The one that matters is the first properly documented look at what an African ecosystem actually retains when the capital is lost — and the answer is: nearly all the operators, most of them within eighteen months, several of them building in the same sector with the same co-founders. That is the asset that does not appear on any LP report. Note the second-order detail, which is the sharper one: iProcure's point-of-sale technology was reacquired from the administrators and relaunched as a separate company now trading in three countries. The equity was destroyed. The asset was not.

TechCabal — “These Kenyan startups raised $500 million before shutting down. Where are the founders now?”

The structural read is that African venture capital's returns problem and African venture capital's capability problem are different problems, and this piece separates them for the first time with names attached. The capital outcome on these ten companies is unambiguous and bad: over $500m in, effectively nothing out. The capability outcome is close to the opposite. Sendy's four founders produced, within three years of closure, an instant-supplier-payment network built on bank balance sheets, a three-country retail and distribution platform, an aquaculture business, a circular-fashion company and the managing director of Ireland's national accelerator. Two of the four are working together again. That is not a wasted cohort; it is an expensively trained one, and the training was funded by investors who captured none of it. This is the standard mechanism by which ecosystems compound, and it is why Silicon Valley's failure rate was never the argument against Silicon Valley. But note the asymmetry that makes the African version harder: the second companies are, on this evidence, systematically smaller, more capital-efficient and more B2B than the first — tabb intermediates bank credit rather than lending, iPOS sells software rather than distributing fertiliser. Founders who have been through a capital-intensive failure build capital-light businesses next. That is rational learning at the individual level and it is a structural constraint on fund-returning outcomes at the portfolio level, because capital-light businesses absorb less capital and return smaller absolute dollars.

Five hundred million dollars bought no returns and an entire generation of operators. The investors paid for the education; the next set of cap tables gets the graduate.

The threads: to Issue 067's item 3 (Terra closing Africa's largest seed round at $52m) and to Issue 066's item 1 (Oui Capital's Olu Oyinsan arguing that no African seed fund should exceed $50m, with five of his six write-offs being the small optionality bets) — put all three cards together and the disagreement resolves into something more useful than either side of it: the argument is not about fund size but about which failures are recoverable. Oyinsan's small bets failed and returned nothing at all. These ten large failures returned no capital but a dense operator network and, in one case, a functioning product sold on by an administrator. To Issue 065's item on the mechanics of buying your own company — iPOS is precisely that transaction executed through insolvency, and the fact that it took an administrator to unlock a working asset from a failed corporate wrapper is a comment on how much value sits trapped in African cap tables. To the Chronos read on diligence — the transferable practice is that a founder's prior failure is now a traceable, sourceable reference in this market, with filings, administrators and co-founders all available to be asked; and the more informative question is not "did it fail" but "what did they build in the eighteen months after, and did anyone follow them there". Two of Sendy's four founders chose to work together again after losing an $80m valuation. That is a stronger signal about both of them than any reference call. Two lines of read: the surface story is a routine interim dividend and its withholding tax. The one that matters is what the shareholder register discloses about who actually owns Nigeria's largest listed company — and the answer is that 97.23% of the owners hold 0.66% of the shares, while one Mauritian holding company holds 73.39%. A local listing with 346,000 local shareholders is doing real work as a legitimising fact and almost none as an ownership fact. The government's 17× multiple over all retail combined is not a comment on the tax rate, which is unremarkable at 10%. It is a comment on the free float.

A local listing with 346,000 local shareholders — and a free float that rounds to nothing beside the parent
TechCabal — “Nigeria to earn $41 million from MTN's $406 million dividend payout”

The structural read is that this is a very clear picture of how a frontier state monetises a foreign-owned national champion, and of the limits of local listing as a mechanism for local ownership. Take the government's position first, because it is the enviable one. Nigeria receives ₦54.59bn from this distribution with no capital at risk, no governance obligation, no exposure to the naira revenue line and no dilution when MTN next raises. It also received ₦384.05bn in taxes from the same company over the period. The state is, in cash terms, MTN Nigeria's largest counterparty after its own parent — and unlike the parent, it is senior to the dividend. That is a materially better position than equity, and it explains why the listing requirement matters less to the state than the tax hooks do. Now take the register. A local listing is normally justified on three grounds: it deepens the domestic capital market, it broadens ownership, and it aligns a foreign operator with local stakeholders. On this evidence it delivers the first, gestures at the second, and does very little for the third. 336,608 Nigerians hold 0.66% of MTN Nigeria. The float is thin enough that price formation is dominated by a small number of institutional blocks, and the economic interest of the local shareholder base — ₦3.22bn net — is a rounding error against a payout of over half a trillion naira. The turnaround underneath all of this is real and should not be lost: revenue up 54.93% in FY25, up 25.9% again in H1 2026, profit after tax up 70.6%, and a company that restored positive retained earnings and equity before resuming distributions. This is a genuinely repaired balance sheet paying out of genuine profit.

The state took ₦54.59bn from a company it does not own, is senior to every shareholder in getting it, and carries none of the currency risk. Equity is not always the best seat at the table.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Make it argue against itself — the sceptic test

These models are agreeable by construction, and agreeableness is exactly the wrong property in the moment you most want help: when you have arrived at a view and want to know whether it survives contact. Ask a model to assess your thesis and it will find the merit in it, because that is what it has been shaped to do. So do not ask it to assess. Give it a role and an instruction to win. "You are the sceptic on our investment committee. You think this deal is a mistake. Give me your four strongest objections, in the order you would raise them, and do not soften any of them." The output changes character entirely — not because the model has learned anything new, but because you have stopped asking a question that has a polite answer. The discipline this enforces is more valuable than the objections themselves: if you cannot answer the four, you did not have a thesis, you had a preference. Run it before the memo is written rather than after, when changing your mind is still cheap.

A worked example

Weak, because it invites agreement: “What do you think of the argument that Eskom's smelter tariff is good policy?” Strong, because it cannot be answered politely: “You are a Treasury official who opposes the smelter tariff and has to write the minute explaining why. You believe the fiscal arithmetic is wrong. Set out your four strongest objections in the order you would raise them — be specific about which projections you dispute and what you would need to see to change your mind. Do not hedge, and do not give me the balanced view; I have already had it.” Then, having read the four, ask the same model to answer them as the minister. You now have both sides of a meeting you did not have to convene.
