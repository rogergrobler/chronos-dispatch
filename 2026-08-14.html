Pan-Africa · Ecommerce · Turnaround Analysis Chart of the day
Jumia, the New York-listed African e-commerce company (Nigeria, Egypt, Kenya, Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire and more), has secured $50m in fresh equity led by the International Finance Corporation (IFC) — the World Bank's private-sector arm — with participation from AXIAN Telecom, a top shareholder that took an 8% stake in May 2025. The raise lands on a sharply improving Q2 2026: net loss narrowed to $11.7m from $16.6m a year earlier; orders up 28%, active customers up 24%, gross profit up 28%, and adjusted EBITDA loss down 36% to $8.7m. Nigeria was the standout — GMV up 36%, orders up 34% — and sales from Chinese and Turkish international sellers grew 96%, cementing Jumia's pivot to a marketplace for affordable imports. Cash was only $48.3m at end-June, so the cheque buys runway; CEO Francis Dufay now targets breakeven in Q4 2026 and profitability in 2027.

After a decade of cash-burn, Jumia takes $50m from the World Bank's IFC — and says profitability is now quarters, not years, away

Nigeria · Digital Policy · Structural Briefing Structural design
Under the NIMC Act 2026 (which replaces the 2007 Act), the National Identity Management Commission is now Nigeria's Root Certification Authority for the national Public Key Infrastructure — the “trust anchor” that issues root digital certificates and underpins every digital signature tied to the National Identification Number (NIN), across banks, agencies and businesses. NIMC has ~136m enrolments. The snag: the long-gestating Digital Economy and E-Governance Bill (awaiting the Senate) empowers a different regulator, NITDA, to accredit certification authorities — creating overlapping mandates over who controls the country's digital-trust infrastructure. Policy experts warn the clash could stall the flagship bill and force banks and tech firms to comply with duplicate rules. Notably, Kenya, South Africa and Egypt all vest this oversight in specialised tech regulators, not the national identity agency — Nigeria's model is markedly more centralised.

Nigeria makes its identity agency the cryptographic root of the whole digital economy — then trips over its own digital-economy bill

TechCabal · Enterprise & Policy · Frank Eleanya · Thu 13 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a signatures technicality. The one that matters is the state installing itself as the trust anchor beneath every authenticated transaction in the economy — the same perimeter-drawing instinct as yesterday's SARB, now at the identity layer, where whoever controls the root certificate ultimately vouches for who everyone is. It threads straight from Issue 063's payments and cyber-cafe items: the digitising African state keeps moving from supervising infrastructure to being it. The tension is a self-inflicted one — two laws, two agencies, one mandate, and a drafting clash that could delay the very bill it was meant to enable.

TechCabal — “Nigeria's identity law raises questions over who regulates digital signatures”

The structural read is that identity has quietly become the deepest layer of a modern state's digital infrastructure — deeper even than payments — because everything else authenticates against it. A Root Certification Authority is the apex of a country's chain of trust: it rarely touches a citizen directly, but every digital signature, secure transaction and verified credential ultimately traces its authority back to the root's cryptographic keys. By writing NIMC into that role, Nigeria has done something more consequential than issue ID cards — it has made a single agency the guarantor of digital truth for banks, ministries and private companies alike. That is enormous leverage, and it is why the governance question is not a footnote: a trust anchor with overlapping, contested oversight is a trust anchor with a crack in it. The comparison the piece draws is telling — Kenya, South Africa and Egypt all put this power with specialised technology regulators precisely to keep identity-issuance and trust-certification in separate hands; Nigeria has fused them.

Own the payment rail, and you see the money move. Own the root certificate, and you decide who is who. Nigeria just put both instincts one ministry apart — and forgot to make them talk.

The threads: to Issue 063's item 3 (SARB takes the payment rail) — the matched instinct, one layer down the stack: first the state asserts control of the rail value moves on, then of the identity infrastructure that authorises it; jurisdiction over both is now treated as sovereign, not administrative. To Issue 063's item 4 (Kenya's cyber-cafe attribution rule) and today's item 3 (AI-fabricated identity) — the state is racing to make identity verifiable and legible at exactly the moment AI is making it cheap to forge, so the value of a trustworthy national root only rises. To Issue 062's item 4 (Nigeria's digital tax net) — the same build-out of state legibility infrastructure, from taxing the payment trail to anchoring the identity layer beneath it. To the Chronos read on regulatory and structural risk — when the trust anchor's governance is contested, so is the compliance calculus for every fintech, bank and platform that must derive its certificates from it. The sceptic's ledger: overlapping NIMC/NITDA mandates are a recipe for exactly the multi-agency friction that has stalled Nigerian digital legislation before, and could delay the E-Governance Bill by another cycle; concentrating both identity issuance and trust certification in one agency removes a natural check and raises real surveillance and single-point-of-failure concerns under a “One Person, One Identity” frame; and a Root CA is only as good as its operational security, which is a high bar for a young public institution. The forward marker: whether lawmakers harmonise the NIMC Act and the Digital Economy Bill cleanly, or whether the clash sends the flagship bill back for another round of scrutiny.

Pan-Africa · Artificial Intelligence · Cybersecurity Analysis AI deployment
Interpol's African Cyberthreat Assessment 2026 puts hard numbers on an asymmetry: AI was involved in 55% of cybercrime cases observed by surveyed African countries in 2025, and deepfake incidents rose sevenfold between Q2 and Q4 2024 — used to fabricate identities, clone voices and impersonate executives. The institutions chasing them lag badly: only 8% of intelligence analysts have advanced AI expertise, 92% of agencies cite a lack of technical skill as their main obstacle, 94% lack sufficient digital-forensics tools, and just 22% of forensics units understand AI-driven threats. The stakes are continental: Africa ran >$1.1trn in digital transactions across 1.1bn mobile subscriptions in 2025; African-origin material on dark-web forums rose 62% YoY; a single Uganda deepfake scam cost >$2m; and 72% of surveyed countries report organised scam centres. Cybercrime-as-a-service has “democratised access to advanced attack tools.”

Interpol's AI-crime gap: attackers weaponise it in 55% of cases — only 8% of the analysts chasing them can use it at all

TechCabal · Delve into AI · Adonijah Ndege · Thu 13 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a cybercrime report. The one that matters is the cleanest illustration yet of AI's offence-defence asymmetry — a criminal needs only an open-source model, stolen data and somewhere to wire funds, while the institutions opposing them need procurement, training, court-admissible evidence and cross-border cooperation. It is the direct counterpoint to yesterday's Kenya cyber-cafe item: the identity the state is straining to capture is exactly what AI-armed fraudsters now fabricate at machine speed. And it is acutely Africa-specific, because the continent's leap into mobile-money finance is precisely the attack surface.

The red bars are the attackers' advantage; the green bars are the defenders' capacity — the gap is the whole story
TechCabal — “Africa's cybercriminals are adopting AI faster than the institutions chasing them”

The structural read is that AI lowers the cost of offence faster than the cost of defence, and the gap is institutional as much as technological. A fraudster can adopt a frontier model with no new infrastructure — an API key, some stolen credentials, a place to launder — whereas a police force adopting the same tool must procure it, train investigators, and ensure the evidence survives a courtroom, all while coordinating across banks, telcos and foreign jurisdictions that move at the speed of a mutual-legal-assistance treaty. Interpol's own phrase captures it: criminals now operate “at machine speed,” while enforcement is bound by “legal processes, bureaucratic delays, and a lack of technical capacity.” The uncomfortable irony the piece lands on is that the cybercriminal is the one African actor who needs none of the things the continent's AI debate obsesses over — no sovereign data law, no national AI policy, no local talent pool. They need an open model and a target. And Africa is an unusually rich target precisely because its financial inclusion story ran through mobile phones: $1.1trn of digital transactions is $1.1trn of attack surface.

The criminal needs an open-source model and a stolen identity. The state needs a data-protection law, a forensics budget, a court order and a foreign counterpart who picks up the phone. That asymmetry is the whole market.

The threads: to Issue 063's item 4 (Kenya's cyber-cafe attribution rule) and today's item 2 (Nigeria's identity root) — the state is building the machinery to make every person and transaction legible at exactly the moment AI makes synthetic identity cheap, so the two are locked in a race the defenders are currently losing on speed. To Issue 063's item 2 (institutional quality) — this is that thesis in its darkest form: the binding constraint is not technology but institutional capacity, forensics budgets, and cross-border cooperation, which is why well-governed states will pull ahead on cyber-resilience just as they do on returns. To the Chronos read on portfolio risk — every fintech, bank and mobile-money operator in the book sits on the exposed side of this gap, and the ones that integrate real-time, AI-assisted fraud detection will separate from those that wait for the court order. The sceptic's ledger: Interpol's figures are self-reported by agencies with an incentive to plead under-resourcing, and “AI-involved” is a definitionally elastic category that can inflate the 55%; defensive AI is also improving fast, and banks' own fraud models are frequently ahead of the police cited here; and “criminals move faster than regulators” is a perennial that has been true of every technology without ending finance. The forward marker: whether African banks and telcos actually wire real-time fraud alerts into law-enforcement systems — the single integration Interpol flags as missing — or whether the trails stay scattered across institutions that cannot act in time.

Kenya · Banking · Earnings Analysis Hyper-local
KCB Group, Kenya's largest bank by assets, reported H1 2026 pretax profit up more than 20% to KES 49.3bn ($381.87m) from KES 40.8bn a year earlier. Tellingly, subsidiaries outside Kenya contributed 27.7% of pretax profit — the group operates in the DRC, Tanzania, Rwanda, South Sudan, Uganda and Burundi. Net interest income rose to KES 74.0bn (~$572.5m) from KES 69.1bn, while non-funded (fee and transaction) income climbed 15% to KES 34.1bn. The result lands with the Central Bank of Kenya holding its policy rate at 8.75% and private-sector credit growth returning to double digits — a supportive backdrop after a lean lending cycle.

Kenya's biggest bank grows profit a fifth — and now earns more than a quarter of it outside Kenya

The structural read is that Africa's most durable banking franchises are being built through regional diversification, not domestic dominance — and KCB is a clean example. A fifth more profit is a good year. A quarter of it earned in five other countries is a franchise. The first is a number; the second is a moat.

South Africa · Trade · Geopolitics Analysis Tests a thesis
India and the five-member Southern African Customs Union (SACU) — South Africa, Botswana, Namibia, Lesotho and Eswatini — have signed Terms of Reference to launch Preferential Trade Agreement (PTA) negotiations, with talks due to start within a month and a target of concluding within a year. It is a goods deal (not a full FTA covering services or investment), spanning eight chapters including market access, rules of origin, customs, trade remedies and dispute settlement. India is seeking lower tariffs for autos and auto-parts, pharmaceuticals, machinery, chemicals and textiles, plus reliable access to SACU's critical minerals — platinum-group metals, manganese and copper. The timing is pointed: South Africa is simultaneously weighing whether to raise car-import duties from India and China from 25% to 50%. Five earlier rounds (2002–2010) collapsed over product lists and mutual sensitivities.

India and Southern Africa reopen a trade pact after 16 years — critical minerals for market access, as SA eyes doubling car-import duties

Business Tech Africa · Greg Stewart · Thu 13 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a set of negotiating Terms of Reference. The one that matters is the same de-risking-by-plumbing logic as yesterday's BRICS item, one layer up the stack — a critical-minerals-for-manufacturing bargain that would route Southern African platinum, manganese and copper toward India while India's carmakers eye an African market that may soon wall them out at 50%. It threads from Issue 063's BRICS-rails piece: the Global South steadily building preferential channels around the incumbent trade order, this time in goods and metals rather than payments. It also tests a thesis Chronos watches — and the honest read is that a ToR is a long way from a deal that already failed once.

Business Tech Africa — “India seeking a new SACU trade agreement” (13 Aug 2026 briefing)

The structural read is that the interesting trade story of the decade is not a grand new currency bloc but the patient, unglamorous work of two emerging regions cutting each other preferential access to the things each actually needs — India wants secure supply of the platinum-group metals, manganese and copper that its manufacturing and energy-transition ambitions run on; Southern Africa wants a foothold in one of the world's fastest-growing consumer markets for its minerals and agricultural goods. That is a genuinely complementary bargain, which is why it keeps getting attempted. The twist that makes it newsworthy now is the auto tension: South Africa is contemplating doubling car-import duties from India and China to 50% to protect its own assembly base, so the very sector India most wants liberalised is the one Pretoria is preparing to fence off. That is the negotiation in microcosm — minerals flow one way, but market access for finished goods is exactly where industrial-policy self-interest bites, and it is precisely what sank the 2002–2010 rounds.

The Global South isn't building one big rail around the dollar — it's cutting a hundred small preferential deals around the incumbent order. Minerals for market access is the template; the auto tariff is where it snags.

The threads: to Issue 063's item 6 (BRICS payment rails) — the matched instinct in a different medium: where BRICS proposed routing settlement around the dollar, this routes trade preference around the WTO-default order, both expressions of a Global South assembling its own plumbing. To Issue 062's data-sovereignty and item on jurisdiction — the same recognition that control over strategic inputs (there, cloud and data; here, critical minerals) is now a geopolitical lever, not a procurement detail. To the Chronos read on structural shifts and optionality — a maturing India–SACU corridor would reshape the demand and pricing outlook for Southern African PGMs and base metals, and the auto-tariff decision is a live signal on how protective South African industrial policy is turning. The sceptic's ledger: a Terms of Reference is the thinnest possible commitment — the same partners ran five rounds from 2002 to 2010 and signed nothing — and “concluded within a year” is the kind of trade timeline that almost never holds; a goods-only PTA excludes services and investment, capping how transformative it can be; and the simultaneous move to double car duties suggests the two sides are already at cross-purposes on the sector that matters most to India. The forward marker: whether the first negotiating round actually convenes within the promised month — and whether South Africa's auto-duty decision lands before or after it, because the sequencing will tell you how serious either side is.

Ghana · Commodities · Macro Briefing Hyper-local
Ghana has widened its lead as Africa's largest gold producer, with a record 6 million ounces of output for the 2025 period, lifting export earnings to about $20.2bn and gold's share of merchandise exports to 63.1%. The surge is driven by stronger small-scale (artisanal) production and elevated global prices — gold trading around $4,400/oz. The windfall is real fiscal firepower for a country still working through debt restructuring, but it also deepens a structural dependence: nearly two-thirds of everything Ghana sells abroad is now a single, price-volatile metal.

Business Tech Africa · Greg Stewart · Thu 13 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a bumper mining stat. The one that matters is concentration risk dressed as a boom — $20.2bn of gold at $4,400 prices is genuine fiscal oxygen for a recovering economy, but a single commodity now carries 63% of exports, tying Ghana's currency, its debt trajectory and even its tech-funding climate to one volatile line on a screen. It threads to Issue 063's governance-versus-size map: resource windfalls rarely convert into durable, diversified growth, and the resource giants keep under-performing their scale. Business Tech Africa — “Ghana widens lead as Africa's largest gold producer” (13 Aug 2026 briefing)

The structural read is that a commodity windfall is a test of institutions, not a reward for them. Ghana is producing more gold than any African country ever has, at prices near record highs, and $20.2bn of export earnings is real money for a state that has spent the past two years in debt restructuring — it buys reserves, stabilises the cedi, and eases the fiscal squeeze that shapes everything from interest rates to the risk appetite of local investors. But 63.1% of merchandise exports in one metal is the definition of a concentrated, fragile export base: when two-thirds of your foreign earnings ride a single price you do not set, your macro stability is only ever as durable as the next leg of the gold market. The detail that the surge is led by small-scale production adds a further wrinkle — artisanal mining (galamsey) is a formalisation and environmental headache as much as an economic boon, and windfalls that flow through informal channels are the hardest to translate into durable public investment.

Six million ounces is a triumph. Sixty-three per cent of exports in one metal is a vulnerability. The windfall is real — the question is whether it funds diversification or just the next cycle of dependence.

The threads: to Issue 063's item 2 (governance beats size) — this is the cautionary half of that map: resource giants tend to under-earn their scale precisely because commodity concentration crowds out the diversified, institution-building growth that compounds, and Ghana is now more concentrated, not less. To today's item 5 (India–SACU critical minerals) — a useful contrast in commodity strategy: Southern Africa is negotiating structured, preferential access for its metals, while Ghana is simply riding the spot price; the first builds an off-take relationship, the second builds exposure. To the Chronos read on macro fragility and country risk — a West African economy tying its stability to a single volatile export is a standing reminder to stress-test any Ghana-linked exposure against a gold-price reversal, not just today's boom. The sceptic's ledger: a good part of the “record” is a price illusion — ounces up, but dollar earnings flattered by a historically high gold price that will mean-revert; the small-scale production driving volumes carries real environmental, governance and formalisation costs the headline omits; and export concentration this high means the cedi and the fiscal recovery are one commodity shock away from reversing. The forward marker: whether Ghana channels the windfall into reserves, debt reduction and diversification — or spends the up-cycle and re-enters the next downturn just as exposed.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Before you commit to a view, make Claude argue the opposite — use it as a red-team, not a cheerleader

The default failure mode of any assistant — human or AI — is agreement. Ask Claude to “review my thesis” and it will mostly polish it. The higher-value move is to explicitly cast it as the opposition: give it your position and instruct it to build the strongest case against you, as if a rival bidder or a sceptical IC member were briefed to kill the deal. The point is not to be talked out of a decision but to surface the objections before someone across the table does — the assumption your model quietly depends on, the disconfirming data point you skimmed past, the second-order effect you did not price. A view that survives a genuine red-team is one you can defend; a view that collapses under it was going to collapse anyway, and better here than in the room. The discipline is to demand the case you'd least like to hear, then engage with it honestly rather than reaching for the rebuttal.

A worked example

Instead of “What do you think of my case for backing this company?”, try: “You are the most sceptical member of our investment committee, and your job is to stop this deal. Here is my thesis. Give me the three strongest reasons it fails, the single assumption most likely to be wrong, and the one piece of evidence that — if it existed — would change my mind. Do not soften it.” Then do the same trick before any negotiation: “Argue the counterparty's side better than they will.”
