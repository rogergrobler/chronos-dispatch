Nigeria · Fintech · FX Infrastructure Tests a thesis
Stabyl, a liquidity exchange for financial institutions, has emerged from stealth with a $2.7m pre-seed round led by Konga, the Nigerian e-commerce group. It is neither a consumer app nor a cross-border payments product: it sits one layer below both, at the point where banks, payment service providers and large institutions source foreign exchange before any payment can move. Today that sourcing is fragmented — treasury teams call multiple banks and liquidity providers, compare rates, and often watch the market move before approvals clear. Stabyl replaces those bilateral negotiations with a central limit order book where FX buyers and sellers post and match orders automatically, settling across both traditional banking rails (via KongaPay for naira) and stablecoin rails (USDT/USDC, wallets via DFNS). Net FX inflow into Nigeria was $6.92bn in February 2026 alone. Stabyl charges a low take rate per transaction rather than earning a spread, and starts on the NGN/USD corridor.

Stabyl emerges from stealth with $2.7m to pool Africa’s fragmented FX liquidity

TechCabal · Startups · Opeyemi Kareem · Fri 26 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is infrastructure, not another app. Stabyl is deliberately positioned beneath the visible fintech layer; its customers are Onafriq, Yellow Card and Fincra, which it frames as clients, not competitors — “we’re creating more dough to make a bigger pie.” Two — the model is exchange economics. A take rate kept intentionally low to maximise volume, not a spread captured on each trade; the bet is that the deepest pool wins by being the cheapest place to transact. Three — the timing is regulatory. Stabyl launches into a Nigeria that lifted its crypto ban in 2023 and built a formal VASP framework, and its founders chose to build “hand-in-hand with regulators” rather than arrive late to a settled market.

TechCabal — “Stabyl emerges from stealth with $2.7 million for Africa’s FX infrastructure”

Friday closed on the surface of the market — the NGX league table, the app that won the most trades. Today opens directly underneath it. Before any of those trades can settle in dollars, someone has to source the dollars, and in Nigeria that has been a phone-call business: fragmented, slow, and priced against you by the time you commit. Stabyl’s wager is that the durable position is not the consumer relationship but the liquidity pool beneath it — the central order book everyone else has to clear through.

“Stablecoins are great, but not on their own — you still need to convert back to local currency. Stabyl connects the stablecoin rail to the fiat rail.”

The infrastructure through which FX liquidity moves is fragmented, with payment providers, banks and institutions relying on multiple relationships to source it; Stabyl’s goal is to connect those participants on one platform.

For Chronos this tests a thesis the week keeps returning to: in African money movement, value is migrating down the stack toward whoever owns the deepest, cheapest pool of liquidity and compliance. Three things are worth holding. First, the structure is a genuine network-effects business — an exchange whose depth improves with every participant, which is exactly the kind of moat that compounds and exactly the kind that is brutal to build from a standing start with $2.7m. Second, the Konga relationship is both the proof and the risk: Konga is lead investor, naira settlement partner and first customer, which de-risks the launch but leaves the open question of whether truly independent institutions will route flow through a platform anchored by one commercial group. Third, the honest caution is liquidity cold-start — an order book is worthless until it is deep, and the period before depth arrives is where most exchanges die. The thread to hold forward is the same one we set on the Yellow Card and Daya rails: in payments the value accrues to whoever owns the compliant pipe and the deepest pool. Stabyl is a clean, early bet on that being the FX layer — worth watching for the first signs of non-Konga volume, which is the only real proof the pie is getting bigger.

Africa · Telecoms · Infrastructure Chart
Starlink now operates in 27 African countries — adding Côte d’Ivoire on 17 June — and delivers faster download speeds than most fixed broadband providers, per Ookla’s Q1 2026 Speedtest Intelligence (released 15 June). It reached an estimated half a million African users by end-2025 (of ~10m globally). In Nigeria it is already the second-largest ISP at 91,991 subscribers (NCC, Q4 2025); Kenya recorded 19,470 (Sep 2025) and Rwanda 4,489 (Q2 2025). The predicted showdown with mobile operators has not happened: equipment costs of $200–$700 cap mass adoption, and satellite latency and capacity cannot match fibre-backed networks. Instead MTN, Airtel, Orange and Vodafone are partnering with satellite providers (Starlink, Kuiper, Eutelsat OneWeb) for rural coverage and Direct-to-Device. Africa is ~18% of the world’s population but holds less than 1% of global fibre.

Africa’s telcos stop fighting Starlink and fold it into the infrastructure stack

TechCabal · Telecoms · Frank Eleanya · Fri 26 Jun 2026

The structural read under a connectivity headline. The story that was supposed to be disruption — satellite versus tower — has resolved into integration: operators now treat low-earth orbit as one more layer of a stack that still runs mostly on fibre, towers, subsea cable and data centres. MTN’s Ralph Mupita put it plainly at Capital Markets Day on 11 June: “we have to embrace LEO satellites; they are not going away… a person connected at home will increasingly be using a combination of these technologies.” The deeper point for an investor is that Starlink has not commoditised the telcos — it has reinforced why owning integrated, end-to-end infrastructure (and selling it to enterprises, governments and hyperscalers) is the prize, with satellite as a complement rather than a substitute.

Satellite fills the gap fibre never reached · but the stack the telcos own still carries the traffic
TechCabal — “Why Africa’s telcos are embracing Starlink instead of fighting it”

Friday’s telecoms thread had MTN retiring 3G spectrum to fund the networks that actually pay. Today extends the same discipline outward: rather than defend turf against Starlink, the operators are absorbing it into an integrated stack. The instinct that reads “new entrant” as “threat” turns out to be wrong here for an economic reason — satellite and terrestrial are built for different jobs, and the upfront cost of a Starlink kit keeps it a complement to, not a replacement for, mass-market mobile.

“Bandwidth delivered through satellite cannot be compared with bandwidth delivered through fibre. Satellite is for where fibre and microwave cannot reach.”

Rather than reducing the need for terrestrial networks, the rise of satellite connectivity is reinforcing the importance of fibre, towers, spectrum and data centres — with LEO as one more layer of the digital infrastructure stack.

For Chronos there are two reads. First, the competitive lesson generalises well beyond telecoms: a dramatic new entrant does not always commoditise the incumbent — sometimes it raises the value of owning the integrated whole, because the entrant solves only one slice (rural reach) and depends on the incumbent’s backbone for everything else. MTN’s response — triple the fibre footprint, double subsea capacity, expand data centres, reintegrate towers via the proposed IHS deal, and bolt satellite on top — is a bet that the prize is the end-to-end platform sold to enterprises, governments and hyperscalers, not any single layer. Second, the caution is that “integrated infrastructure” is capital-hungry and long-duration, and the same story could read very differently if Direct-to-Device matures faster than expected and starts eating mainstream connectivity rather than just filling gaps. The thread to hold forward is the one that runs under the whole edition: in connectivity, as in FX and payments, the durable position is owning the layer everything else has to run through — and watching for the moment a “complement” quietly becomes a substitute.

Nigeria · Fintech · Cross-Border Longitudinal
Blaaiz launched in 2023 as a consumer cross-border payments product — first a Telegram bot, then a standalone app on the Canada–Nigeria corridor. Founder Ifelade Ayodele, an ex-Accenture consultant, knew his first ~500 users by name. Studying ten competing remittance apps, he found they all hit the same wall: the bottleneck was the underlying infrastructure, not the app. By late 2024 Blaaiz repositioned from a consumer app into a payments-infrastructure company that banks, fintechs and payment companies integrate to offer cross-border payments. It became profitable in 2025 and now holds an IMTO licence in Nigeria, MSB licence and PSP registration in Canada, and is pursuing money-transmitter licences across US states. Its competitors shifted from LemFi, Nala and Grey (consumer) to Fincra, Thunes and Onafriq (infrastructure). The old retail app — ~50,000 users — now survives as an internal testing ground. Nigeria received $20.93bn in remittances in 2024.

Blaaiz’s thousand-day pivot: from a Telegram bot to the rail other fintechs rent

TechCabal · Day 1-1000 · Opeyemi Kareem · Sat 27 Jun 2026

A founder narrative that doubles as a thesis case study. The arc is the one this edition keeps tracing: a company that began by owning the customer ends up owning the rail beneath the customer — deliberately. Ayodele’s tell is precise: he was “dealing with the symptoms” (a smoother app) until he realised the disease was infrastructural, and that “the rails the customers wanted, we couldn’t provide in the way they wanted them… it was not a Blaaiz-specific problem.” The pivot cost him the dopamine of watching daily downloads and bought him a harder, slower business of compliance, banking partnerships and licences — but a more defensible one. The retail app didn’t die; it became the lab where features are validated before shipping through the APIs.

TechCabal — “‘We had no clear path about how this was going to turn out’: Day 1-1000 of Blaaiz”

This is the most direct longitudinal thread of the week. Friday’s Tala item showed a mature lender concluding that the future is embedded rather than direct; Friday’s Monzo item showed a distributor renting a licensed rail. Blaaiz is the company on the other side of both trades — the one that decided, earlier and more completely, to become the rail. It is the same migration down the stack as Stabyl and the same logic the African fintech rails were built on: own the infrastructure everyone else depends on, and let the apps fight over the customer on top.

“It became obvious that the rails the customers wanted, we couldn’t provide them in the way they wanted — and it was not a Blaaiz-specific problem.”

A thousand days after setting out to build a better remittance app, Ayodele and Oni ended up building the infrastructure beneath it — profitable, licensed across jurisdictions, with the old app repurposed as a test bench.

For Chronos the value here is less the single company than the pattern it confirms, because it sharpens a diligence question we can apply across the book. When we look at any consumer fintech, the test is whether its real, defensible asset is the customer relationship or the infrastructure underneath — and whether management has the discipline to walk away from vanity metrics (downloads, MAUs) toward the harder infrastructure economics (integrations, licences, profitability) when the data points that way. Blaaiz is a clean example of that discipline: it gave up the thing that felt like progress for the thing that compounds. The honest caution is that the infrastructure lane it has entered is crowded with better-capitalised players — Fincra, Thunes, Onafriq — and being the rail is only valuable if enough volume rides it; an undifferentiated rail is just a low-margin utility. The thread to hold forward, across Blaaiz, Stabyl and Tala, is the same: name who would pay to embed you, and why they couldn’t easily build it themselves. Where that answer is thin, “we’re infrastructure now” is positioning, not a moat.

Nigeria · Agritech · Data Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Bango is a community-driven platform, launched November 2025, where buyers share what they paid for food commodities, where, and from whom — addressing the fact that Nigeria’s commodity markets have no fixed prices and little visibility across regions. The idea began when co-founder Ademuyiwa Taofeek discovered that tomatoes sourced from Jos and trucked to Lagos cost less, all-in, than buying them locally — despite the “logistics” explanation for higher Lagos prices. Buyers submit commodity, price, quantity, market, location and a photo, creating a feedback-loop database future buyers rely on. The founders then learned that information alone didn’t guarantee access, so they built Shopr (commerce, Abuja-only, ~40–50 orders since June) with shared delivery and demand aggregation, and are preparing Bango Market Day for collective bulk buying. ~2,500 users, word-of-mouth growth. Nigeria’s food & drink market is projected at ~$98.97bn by 2033; food was 56.65% of household spend (NBS, 2019).

Bango turns what shoppers paid into a public price record — then learns information isn’t access

TechCabal · Startups · Opeyemi Kareem · Sat 27 Jun 2026

A small startup carrying an outsized idea: build proprietary data by making buyers the source, then convert the data into commerce. The wedge is information — most grocery platforms start by selling products; Bango starts by telling you where the product is cheapest, and only then builds the means to get it to you. That sequencing is the interesting part. The founders discovered the limit of pure transparency (“a buyer could find a lower price… but that did not mean they could access it”) and responded by adding a thin commerce-and-logistics layer (Shopr) and a demand-aggregation model (Market Day) on top of the price record. The self-correcting trick: because buyers, not sellers, submit prices, traders gain little from gaming the data — underprice and you’re flooded, overprice and buyers walk.

TechCabal — “Bango thinks food prices shouldn’t depend on which trader you ask”

This is the item partners are least likely to have seen, and it earns its place on the data thesis rather than the food one. Bango is, underneath the consumer story, a proprietary-data company with a feedback loop: every purchase logged makes the next buyer’s decision better, which attracts more buyers, which deepens the data. That is the same compounding structure we look for when we ask whether an AI or marketplace business actually owns something defensible — and it sits directly alongside Friday’s Ajua–WayaWaya lesson about who controls the record. Where that story warned that a false record can persist, Bango is an attempt to build a true one, crowd-sourced and self-correcting.

“The information system is fractured — it leaves room for sellers to say any price and buyers have no option but to pay it.”

The founders found that information alone did not solve the problem — so they built a commerce layer on top of the price-intelligence infrastructure, sourcing from farmers and aggregating demand to cut delivery costs.

For Chronos the read is structural, not a call on a 2,500-user startup. First, it is a clean illustration of the data-to-commerce playbook we screen for: start by owning a unique dataset (real transaction prices, not list prices), establish a feedback loop, then monetise the access the data implies. The discipline to notice that transparency alone didn’t convert — and to build the logistics layer rather than assume information was enough — is exactly the operator instinct that separates a data product from a data demo. Second, the cautions are real and worth naming: user-generated data is only as good as its integrity, the self-correcting argument is plausible but untested at scale, and the commerce layer (Shopr, Market Day) drags the business back into the brutal economics of African last-mile logistics, where Chowdeck and others already compete hard. The thread to hold forward connects to Stabyl and the whole edition: the most defensible African businesses increasingly own a layer of proprietary truth — liquidity, rails, or in this case prices — that others have to consult. Worth reading as a small, concrete example of data-as-moat in a sector we usually overlook.

South Africa · AI · Healthtech AI deployment
Khoi Tech, founded in Soweto in 2020 by Seati Moloi, began as a consumer-electronics brand (Afripods earphones, Afriwatch smartwatch) and has evolved into an enterprise healthtech company building AI-powered employee wellness, remote patient monitoring and driver-fatigue management platforms. Bootstrapped largely on internal revenue and founder reinvestment, it integrates AI to turn health and wellness data into proactive monitoring and early risk detection for healthcare providers, employers and policymakers. Moloi returned to London Tech Week (8–12 June) for a second year — one of nine SA startups selected by the UK-SA Tech Hub with Telkom and Disraptor — pitching enterprise health and mining-safety technology, not gadgets. The company is in discussions with several VC firms and exploring UK and pan-African expansion. Moloi’s framing: “We didn’t succeed despite being from Soweto; we succeeded because we are from Soweto… Africa must move beyond consuming technology to creating and owning it.”

From a Soweto back office, Khoi Tech exports township-built AI health technology to London

TechCabal · Startups · Phathisani Moyo · Fri 26 Jun 2026

A counter to the assumption that globally competitive AI must come from the usual hubs. Khoi Tech is building enterprise health AI from a township office far from Sandton or Cape Town, and treating that location as an edge rather than a handicap: “the environment forces you to iterate rapidly, understand hyper-local logistics, and build absolute resilience into your business architecture from day one.” The substance is an ecosystem of devices, apps and SaaS — remote patient monitoring, workforce wellness, driver-fatigue safety for logistics and mining — with AI as the layer that turns raw biometric data into decisions. Bootstrapped to the point of pitching UK investors, it is the mirror image of Friday’s Knowlix item: instead of capability shipped into Africa from Munich, capability built in Africa and exported out.

TechCabal — “A Soweto startup’s unlikely journey from gadgets to AI healthcare”

Friday’s Knowlix item posed the question directly: can a self-configuring AI layer shipped from anywhere beat distribution built locally? Khoi Tech is the same question answered from the other direction — a company whose entire claim is that proximity to an underserved market is what makes its product good, now trying to carry that product to a global stage. Reading the two together is the point: the AI-versus-local-distribution contest runs both ways, and the winners may be the operators who turn hyper-local understanding into something that travels.

“We didn’t succeed despite being from Soweto; we succeeded because we are from Soweto — the environment forces resilience into the architecture from day one.”

Khoi Tech’s evolution from consumer electronics to enterprise healthtech reflects a broader shift, where township-born companies increasingly build technologies designed not only for local markets but for global problems.

For Chronos the interest is in the model, held with appropriate scepticism. The encouraging part is the discipline a bootstrapped path enforces: a company that funded its own evolution from earphones to enterprise health AI has had to earn every step on real revenue, which tends to produce sharper product instincts and more honest unit economics than a well-funded peer chasing a deck. The hyper-local-as-moat argument is also genuinely investable — building for underserved users and harsh conditions can yield products that are cheaper, more resilient and more broadly applicable than those designed for rich markets. The honest cautions are equally clear: bootstrapped means under-capitalised for a category (medical AI, devices) that is capital- and regulation-heavy; “in discussions with several VCs” and London Tech Week selection are signals of momentum, not proof of traction; and exporting township-built health tech into the UK means meeting a regulatory and trust bar that is far higher than the home market. The thread to hold forward is whether African AI can move from being a market that is sold to, toward being a place that builds and exports — and Khoi Tech is a small, real data point that the direction of travel is starting to reverse. One to watch for whether the VC conversations convert into a round and the UK conversations into a contract.

Kenya · AI · Governance Probes a thesis
Kenya has been confirmed as host of the Fourth Summit on Responsible AI in the Military Domain (REAIM), set for April 2027 — the world’s leading forum on military-AI governance, previously held in The Hague (2023), Seoul (2024) and A Coruña (2026). It is the first time an African country will help shape the rules for a technology that may define global security for decades. The context matters: Africa accounted for over 40% of the world’s armed conflicts in 2025, so the continent will be among the first to face the consequences of AI in surveillance, targeting and autonomous drones — yet is largely absent from building these systems. There is still no global treaty on autonomous weapons, no accepted definition of the human role in military AI, and no consensus on accountability for civilian harm. Much of the technology is dual-use: targeting computer vision is also mass surveillance; intelligence analytics also drive law enforcement and border control.

Africa books a seat where the rules for military AI get written — the governance gap, in plain sight

TechCabal · Delve into AI · Adonijah Ndege · Thu 25 Jun 2026

An analysis dressed as a conference announcement, and the analysis is the value. The piece’s spine is that AI capability is moving into the highest-stakes domain — war — far faster than the rules to govern it, and that the same governance vacuum extends into finance, healthcare and education, where adoption has outpaced legislation. Kenya’s hosting is read not as event news but as a sovereignty move: a deliberate attempt to be in the room when the rules are written, after a history in which Africa shaped neither internet nor social-media governance and then spent years living with systems designed elsewhere. The dual-use point is the sharp one for any investor or operator — the line between “military” and “civilian” AI is dissolving, which means the regulatory regimes now being drafted will reach far beyond defence.

TechCabal — “The global debate over AI warfare is coming to Nairobi”

Friday’s Paystack item tracked AI capability arriving at the consumer’s checkout; the open question was how far that capability would travel. This is the far end of the road — the same underlying technology arriving in the military domain, where the rules are not just unfinished but barely begun. Reading the two together is the point of putting this in the edition: AI is landing everywhere at once, from the point of purchase to the point of conflict, and in every case the governance is trailing the deployment. That gap is itself an investment variable, not just a policy one.

“The countries present when the rules are written will have an advantage. The countries absent may find themselves living under rules drafted by others.”

Much of the technology under discussion is dual-use — targeting computer vision is also mass surveillance, intelligence analytics also drive law enforcement and border management — so the distinction between military and civilian AI is blurring.

For Chronos this probes a thesis rather than naming a trade, and it is worth partners’ attention for two reasons. First, the governance gap is a real and underpriced risk across our AI exposure: when adoption outpaces legislation, the rules that eventually arrive can reprice whole categories overnight — data, surveillance, biometric and dual-use technologies most of all. Any African AI position we hold or screen should be stress-tested against governance frameworks that do not yet exist but are now being actively drafted, with Kenya helping hold the pen. Second, the sovereignty framing is a useful lens on where durable advantage in African tech may accrue: the continent’s recurring pattern has been to adopt systems designed elsewhere and absorb the consequences, and the countries (and companies) that move from rule-takers to rule-shapers may capture disproportionate value — the same “own the layer everyone has to run through” logic that runs under Stabyl and the telco-Starlink stack, applied to regulation itself. The honest caveat is that this is a 2027 summit and a long-arc governance story, not a catalyst; the value is in adjusting how we weight regulatory risk on AI now, not in any single event. Worth reading in full as a clear map of where the rules are — and aren’t — being written.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Run the sceptic test — make the model attack your thesis before you commit to it

Today’s edition is full of confident-sounding theses: own the liquidity pool, become the rail, hyper-local-as-moat, governance-gap-as-risk. The danger with a clean thesis is that the model — and you — will happily keep reinforcing it. The fix is to deliberately switch the model from ally to adversary. Hand it your view and instruct it to argue the strongest possible case against you: not a token “here are some risks” list, but the brief a smart short-seller or a rival partner would write to kill the idea. You are not looking to be talked out of the deal; you are looking to find the one objection you can’t answer before the IC does.

A worked example

Paste your thesis and the supporting facts, then prompt: “You are a sceptical investor who thinks this is a bad investment. Write the most rigorous bear case you can, using only the facts I’ve given plus what you know — no strawmen. Rank your objections from most to least damaging, mark which ones would actually kill the thesis versus merely dent it, and end with the single piece of evidence that, if true, would most change my mind.” Read the bear case, then ask the model to score how well your original thesis survives it. If the strongest objection is one you hadn’t already priced in, you’ve found your next diligence question. Use it on every deal that feels too obvious.
