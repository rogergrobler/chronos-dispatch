Global · Fintech · Banking Analysis World's-best-thinkers
In this week's Fintech Brainfood, Simon Taylor — the newsletter the partners read for the plumbing under money — makes a single argument through a week of US bank-charter news: the charter has stopped being the hard part. Circle received final OCC approval for a national trust bank; Klarna filed in Utah for an industrial bank charter; Sony's crypto unit won conditional OCC approval to issue dollar stablecoins; American Banker counts roughly two dozen fintechs, crypto firms and lenders that have applied for or received charters in the first half of 2026 alone. Taylor's line: “The long drought of new de novo charters made these look impossible to get. They weren't. Nobody was granting them.” And the sting in the tail — “Keeping a charter is the work, times ten, forever” — conforming to every future rule, on pain of consent orders, the way Anchorage got a trust charter in 2021 and an AML consent order by 2022. A licence is an option; the business is what you build on top, or fail to.

Getting the charter was the easy part — the licence was never the business

Fintech Brainfood · Simon Taylor · Sun 12 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: when a regulator makes licences abundant, the licence stops being the moat — the scarce thing becomes the ability to operate one, the deposits you can actually gather, the risk you can actually price, the rules you can actually keep. That is the same lesson landing on the next item down the page in Nairobi, and it is the honest test for any “we have the permit” investment case that crosses our desk: a permit is a starting gun, not a finish line, and the interesting question is always what gets built after the ribbon is cut.

Fintech Brainfood — Simon Taylor, “Getting a charter was the easy part”

Taylor's roundup is a US story on its face — Circle, Klarna, Sony, a debit-network sale between four megabanks — but the structural point travels, and it travels straight into the African fintech items on this page. When an administration hands out charters “like conference lanyards,” possession of one stops signalling anything. The signal migrates to what a charter is famously bad at conferring: a deposit base cheap and sticky enough to fund a loan book, an underwriting engine that prices risk without blowing up, a compliance function that can keep conforming to rules that keep changing. Klarna wants a Utah bank precisely to copy its European model, where German and Swedish deposits fund lending at deposit rates; the charter is just the door to that economics, and walking through it is a decade of work.

“The long drought of new de novo charters made these look impossible to get. They weren't. Nobody was granting them. Keeping a charter is the work, times ten, forever.”

The one nugget worth carrying separately is buried at the bottom of Taylor's list: Ramp launched cards, bank accounts and spend controls for AI agents — each agent gets its own identity, budget, expiring credentials and audit trail, installed as a connector inside Claude, ChatGPT or Cursor in, they claim, fifteen seconds. That is the corporate half of agentic commerce arriving before the consumer half, and it rhymes with today's closer on where AI value actually lands. The forward question we'd carry from this into every fintech case: in a world where the licence is cheap and the agent-rails are being laid this quarter, does durable value sit with whoever holds the permit, or with whoever builds the operating capability — the deposits, the underwriting, the controls — that a permit merely makes legal? The counter-case: in some markets the licence really is the scarce asset, because the regulator keeps the door shut; the trick is telling those markets apart from the ones where the door is now wide open.

Kenya · Fintech · Expansion Briefing Longitudinal
Moniepoint, the Nigerian fintech unicorn, has appointed Rose Muturi as chief executive of its Kenyan operations — four months after buying a 78% stake in Sumac Microfinance Bank to get a deposit-taking licence in the market. Muturi is not there to run Sumac (which keeps its own management); she is there to drive Moniepoint's group strategy in Kenya. Her CV is the point: former CEO of Branch Kenya, where she turned a digital lender into a regulated neobank via the Century Microfinance acquisition, plus senior roles at Tala, HF Group, TransUnion, Chase Bank and Standard Chartered, founder of the Digital Lenders Association of Kenya. Moniepoint has also bought restaurant-software firm Orda and is hiring across finance, product and people in Nairobi. As TechCabal frames it: “Buying a bank gives you a licence. Building a bank is a different job entirely.”

Moniepoint bought a Kenyan bank licence — now it hires the person who has actually built one

TechCabal · Kenn Abuya · Tue 14 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the item above made concrete on the continent — the Sumac deal bought the permit, and the Muturi hire is the admission that the permit was the easy part. Moniepoint is now assembling the Nigerian playbook (payments plus banking plus lending plus SME software on one platform) in East Africa's most competitive fintech market, piece by piece. And it is a genuine operator move of the kind the thumbs keep rewarding: not a funding headline, but a unicorn quietly stacking licence, leadership and local team into a durable position in a market it means to hold for a decade.

TechCabal — “Former Branch CEO Rose Muturi joins Moniepoint to lead Kenya operations”

Read this against the lead and the sequencing tells the whole story. Step one, March: Moniepoint buys 78% of Sumac and, with it, a deposit-taking microfinance licence — the door into Kenyan banking. Step two, June–July: it hires Rose Muturi, someone who has already taken a Kenyan lender through exactly this transition, from digital credit to regulated neobank, once before at Branch. The licence took an acquisition; the capability took a career. That is Simon Taylor's abundant-charters argument playing out in a market where the charter was never even the abundant part — and it is why the appointment is arguably the bigger signal than the deal that preceded it.

“The acquisition gave the company the licence required to operate. Expanding the business will require local banking expertise as competition intensifies among banks, fintechs and mobile-money platforms.”

For a firm that watches African fintech from the outside, Moniepoint is worth tracking as a live test of whether the Nigerian model — merchant payments, banking, credit and business tools bundled on a single platform — is a portable system or a home-market accident. The bull case is that the ingredients now assembled (a licence via Sumac, an SME operating system via Orda, a proven local operator via Muturi, and a Nairobi team being hired around them) are the same stack that made Moniepoint work in Lagos. The scepticism is that Kenya is not Nigeria: M-PESA is a deeper incumbent than anything Moniepoint faced at home, and Kenyan SMEs already have banking and mobile-money options its Nigerian customers lacked. The forward question we'd carry: when a fintech exports a playbook by buying the licence and hiring the operator, does the edge come from the bundle itself — or from the proprietary data and distribution the bundle generates once it is running, which is the only part a competitor can't simply also acquire?

Kenya · Regulation · Digital Lending Analysis Hyper-local
On Tuesday the Central Bank of Kenya licensed 25 more digital lenders, taking the total to 252 since it began issuing these licences in 2022 — a market that, four years ago, had zero regulated players and operated in a legal vacuum of undisclosed rates and abusive collection. The CBK says it has received more than 800 applications; only 252 have passed the consumer-protection and fit-and-proper checks required to operate legally. The regulated slice has issued 8.37 million loans worth KES 150.56bn ($1.16bn) as of May 2026. Notably, Rose Muturi — the new Moniepoint Kenya CEO one item up — founded the Digital Lenders Association of Kenya that grew up alongside this framework. The regulator is not policing a market so much as manufacturing one: turning a predatory free-for-all into a licensed category a serious operator can build inside.

Kenya has 252 licensed digital lenders. Four years ago it had none.

TechCabal Daily · Fintech · Wed 15 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is regulatory design as market-making — the CBK used a licensing regime to convert an ungoverned, reputationally toxic sector into a vetted category with recourse, credibility and 8.37 million loans of real volume behind it. And it is the constructive counterpart to yesterday's SIM-swap ruling the partners marked up: same guardrails instinct, but built before the harm rather than assigned after it — the difference between a central bank writing the rulebook and a judge writing the remedy.

Zero to 252 in four years — a licensed category conjured out of a legal vacuum
TechCabal Daily — “Kenya has 252 licensed digital lenders” (15 Jul 2026)

Yesterday the partners marked up a Kenyan court making banks and telcos share the bill for SIM-swap fraud — guardrails written after a loss. This is the same market building its guardrails the other way: a central bank standing up a licensed category from nothing, so that the guardrails exist before the borrower gets hurt. The two together sketch how an African financial market actually gets its rules — part legislature, part regulator, part judge — and Kenya is doing all three at once. The 252 number matters less than what it represents: the CBK has turned digital lending from a reputational liability, all rogue apps and threats to expose borrowers, into a supervised sector with a complaints channel and a credibility signal that unlicensed rivals can't fake.

“Licensed lenders get a credibility signal that unlicensed competitors can't match — and borrowers who had no recourse now have a regulator they can complain to.”

The connection to the two items above is not decorative. Moniepoint is walking into exactly this market, and Rose Muturi helped build the lender association that grew up inside this framework. When a regulator manufactures a clean, licensed category, it lowers the reputational cost of entry for a serious operator and raises the barrier for the cowboys — which is precisely the environment in which a well-run, well-capitalised platform can consolidate. The scepticism worth holding: 252 licences is also 252 competitors, licensing does not by itself make anyone profitable, and the unlicensed lenders the CBK is still chasing don't carry the compliance cost the regulated ones do. The forward question we'd carry: when a regulator turns a grey market into a licensed one, does the value accrue to the incumbents who now have cover to scale, or to the infrastructure — credit bureaux, KYC, fraud and collections rails — that all 252 of them are now obliged to buy?

Rwanda · Payments · Infrastructure Briefing Hyper-local
From 14 July, almost every domestic digital payment in Rwanda — bank-to-bank, bank-to-wallet, wallet-to-bank and wallet-to-wallet — now routes through eKash, the national payment system built by RSwitch and launched in December 2025. Instead of every bank and fintech building direct connections to every other, each provider sends a single instruction to eKash, which routes the payment to the recipient's institution. Fees run up to RWF20 ($0.014) per transaction (institutions set what users actually pay), with transfers of up to RWF10m (~$6,800) supported. It is a piece of the country's cashless-economy push: make the national rail the shared infrastructure, so interoperability stops depending on whichever bilateral links a given bank happened to build. The customer notices nothing; the market structure changes underneath them.

Rwanda routes every retail digital payment through one national rail

TechCabal Daily · Banking · Wed 15 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the “own the rail, not the risk” logic the partners keep marking up, but at sovereign scale — the state making the interoperability layer a public utility rather than leaving it to a tangle of private bilateral connections. And it is a structural-design decision with real consequences: when a national switch becomes the mandatory rail, it standardises the customer experience, compresses per-transaction economics toward near-zero, and quietly resets where value can and can't be captured in a whole payments market.

TechCabal Daily — “Rwanda wants every retail digital payment on one system” (15 Jul 2026)

This page keeps returning to a single question — who owns the rail everyone else has to rent — and Rwanda has just answered it in the bluntest possible way: the state does, and everyone routes through it. eKash is the same interoperability instinct behind India's UPI, Brazil's Pix and the account-to-account rails now spreading across Africa: strip out the bilateral spaghetti, mandate one national switch, and let banks and wallets compete on product rather than on whose plumbing reaches whom. Yesterday the partners upvoted Klump for owning the checkout placement instead of the loan; eKash is the version where the rail itself is nationalised infrastructure, and the competition moves entirely to the layer above it.

“Rwandan banks and fintechs no longer have to build individual payment connections with every institution they want to work with — the national switch does it once, for everyone.”

For anyone thinking about African payments, the pattern worth holding is that the rail is increasingly a public good, not a private moat — which is good for consumers and for the fintechs building on top, and structurally hard for anyone hoping to earn a toll on the rail itself. When interchange on the national switch is a fraction of a US cent, the money is not in moving the payment; it is in what sits alongside it — the credit decision, the merchant software, the data exhaust, the value-added services. The caution is the mirror of the promise: a mandated national rail concentrates operational and systemic risk in one place, and government-run infrastructure can under-invest, break, or be bent to policy ends. The forward question we'd carry across every African payments market moving this way: once the rail is a utility, which layer above it — merchant services, embedded credit, data and identity — is where a private operator can still build something durable?

Malawi · Regulation · Data Briefing Wouldn't-have-seen-otherwise
Malawi's Communications Regulatory Authority (MACRA), the country's data-protection authority, has proposed regulations requiring significant data controllers and processors — banks, mobile networks and anyone handling large volumes of personal data — to register annually and pay a tiered fee: about $29 for small businesses, over $4,000 for large corporations. The rules operationalise Malawi's Data Protection Act of 2024: a controller decides why and how your data is collected, a processor handles it on someone's behalf, and both would now have to register so the regulator knows who holds what. It is an unglamorous, easily-missed item — a small country writing the enforcement layer under a data law — but it is part of a continent-wide build-out of data-governance regimes that quietly raises the compliance cost of operating anywhere on the continent.

Malawi wants every company holding your data to register first

TechCabal Daily · Regulation · Wed 15 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the data-governance twin of the licensing story two items up — another African regulator building a register so it knows who is holding sensitive information before something goes wrong, rather than after. And it is a cost signal that compounds: as one country after another stands up data-protection enforcement, annual registration and tiered fees become another line item every operator on the continent has to carry, and another rail the compliance-and-identity vendors get to sell into.

TechCabal Daily — “Malawi proposes data protection registration rules” (15 Jul 2026)

On its own, a Malawian registration fee is a footnote. As a data point it belongs to a pattern this page has tracked across editions: African regulators moving, one jurisdiction at a time, from having a data-protection law on the books to actually enforcing one. Kenya licenses its lenders, a Kenyan court assigns liability for fraud, Rwanda mandates a payment rail, Nigeria harmonises its digital rulebook — and now Malawi builds a register of who holds personal data. None of these is dramatic alone; together they are the slow construction of the regulatory scaffolding that any pan-African operator has to climb, market by market.

“By requiring organisations handling large amounts of personal data to register, the regulator knows who is collecting what — and can hold them accountable when things go wrong.”

For a firm assessing anything that operates across multiple African markets, the read is that the compliance surface is thickening, unevenly and continuously. That cuts two ways. It raises the cost and complexity of scaling across borders — every new market is a new registration, a new fee schedule, a new supervisor — which favours operators with the balance sheet and back-office to absorb it and disadvantages thinly-staffed startups. And it steadily enlarges the market for the picks-and-shovels of compliance: the KYC, identity, data-governance and reg-tech vendors that every registered controller now has to buy. The forward question we'd carry: as African data and financial regulation matures market by market, does the durable advantage sit with the incumbents who can afford the compliance overhead, or with the regional reg-tech layer that turns fifty-four separate rulebooks into a single product?

Global · AI · Capital Analysis World's-best-thinkers
Simon Taylor's other essay this week is the sharpest short read on the AI capex boom we've seen: by every historical measure the level of AI investment is the biggest bubble ever recorded — five hyperscalers on course to spend over $1trn across 2025–26, capex-to-revenue blown past every prior benchmark, the BIS warning in its annual report that this curve “has never resolved gently in recorded history.” But Taylor's argument is that this bubble is unlike every other because the asset can't be freely bought (GPUs are allocated years out, Nvidia writes equity cheques to its own customers) and because demand and revenue are immediate, not lagged — canal mania needed canals dug; this needs a browser tab. His conclusion is not a market call but an operating one: the margin is wildly unevenly distributed, value concentrates at the frontier and the infrastructure layer, and the winning move for an operator is to own your data, route your workloads, and treat compute as a third form of capital.

The bubble with no unsold inventory — and the only conclusion an operator needs

Fintech Brainfood · Simon Taylor · Thu 9 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: strip out the will-it-crash drama and this is a structural map of where AI value actually lands — hardware and frontier labs keep the margin, open-weight models run the volume, and the company in the middle “pays full retail for genius it never needed.” And the punchline is pure Chronos doctrine: the operators who win the next decade allocate a third kind of capital — compute and intelligence — deliberately, alongside money and people, and they compound the advantage by owning their data and routing their own workloads rather than renting a frontier model for everything.

Hardware keeps the margin, labs eat the subsidy — the middle pays full retail
Fintech Brainfood — Simon Taylor, “Will AI make dotcom look like a blip?”

This is the natural companion to the item the partners kept upvoting — Clark's Remote Labor Index — read from the capital-allocation side rather than the labour side. Taylor grants the bears their strongest point: the BIS says the shape of this capex curve has never resolved gently, OpenAI reportedly lost ~$38.5bn in 2025, and open-weight models like GLM 5.2 are collapsing frontier pricing power. But he argues the AI boom breaks the historical template in two ways that matter: the asset is rationed rather than freely traded, so speculation lives in the equity one layer up; and revenue is immediate rather than lagged, so “you can't overbuild something that's sold out.” The result is a repricing that can destroy enormous capital “without un-inventing a single capability.”

“For most of history you allocated two kinds of capital, human and financial. The operators who win the next decade allocate a third: compute, tokens and intelligence — on purpose.”

The reason to run this on the same page as Moniepoint, Kenya and Rwanda is that its conclusion is the same one, one abstraction up. Taylor's enterprises don't run one frontier model for everything; they run a “council of LLMs” — a cheap, fine-tuned open-weight model on proprietary data handling the routine 85%, a frontier model conducting the hard calls. Santander cut a process from €5 a run to €0.03 by routing it to a small local model at the same quality. The value doesn't accrue to whoever rents the flashiest model; it accrues to whoever owns the data, controls the routing, and treats compute as something to allocate rather than merely consume — which is exactly the “own the rail, own the data” instinct running through every African item above. The counter-case Taylor himself flags: nearly 90% of firms report no productivity uplift after three years, because most bought seats and called it a transformation; the ROI is real but hoarded by the technically capable, and the diffusion will take far longer than the access. For our portfolio the question is uncomfortable and concrete — which of the operators we look at are building the capability to allocate this third form of capital, and which are just renting genius they never needed?

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Save the prompt — build a small library of the ones that worked

Yesterday's tip was the 2×2 — force the model to name the two axes that matter. Today's is quieter and compounds harder: when a prompt produces something genuinely good — a screening skeleton, a devil's-advocate pass, a clean one-page summary in your own voice — don't let it evaporate in the scroll-back. Save it. The single biggest difference between people who get a little out of AI and people who get a lot is not cleverness in the moment; it's a growing personal library of reusable prompts they've already debugged. The good prompt you wrote at 11pm on a deal is an asset. Treat it like one: name it, keep it somewhere you'll find it, and reach for it next time instead of re-inventing it from scratch. Over a few months this is the difference between starting cold every time and starting from your own best previous thinking.

A worked example

Keep a running note — call it “Prompts that worked” — and each time one lands, paste it in with one line on what it's for: “Turns a messy set of board-pack numbers into a five-line KPI read.” “Stress-tests an investment thesis as a hostile IC member.” “Rewrites a draft in my voice, no corporate fluff.” Better still, ask Claude to help: “That output was exactly right. Turn the prompt I just used into a clean, reusable template with placeholders for the parts that change, so I can run it again on a different company.” You'll be surprised how fast a dozen of these become the scaffolding for how you actually work.
