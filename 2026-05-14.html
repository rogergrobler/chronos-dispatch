Lesaka joins the tracker — first profit hit, Mobilemart absorbed, Bank Zero deal in the wings

Spock summary · Q1–Q3 FY26

Lesaka Technologies — the JSE/Nasdaq-listed South African payments and fintech consolidator — was added to the tracker yesterday. Recent operating data: net profit of R61m in Q2 FY26 (first profitable quarter since the Net1 restructure), operating income up 804% YoY in Q3, Mobilemart absorbed for $2.5m in February, Atom Operations earlier. The proposed Bank Zero acquisition (announced June 2025) is the larger pending move.

TechCabal: Lesaka first profit

The shape of Lesaka now: three divisions — Merchant (the legacy core, growth slowed), Consumer (revenue +38% YoY), and Enterprise (+58% YoY, swung profitable). The Enterprise division now houses Mobilemart, expanding the bank/corporate/municipal payment infrastructure footprint. The strategic logic is consolidation: stitch together sub-scale South African payments players into a single platform with enough scale to defend against pure-digital entrants.

The Bank Zero acquisition is the move that changes the framing. If it closes, Lesaka adds a regulated bank charter to its payments rails. The Financial Mail piece "Where reach meets tech: the strategic logic of Lesaka's Bank Zero deal" (February) makes the case: Bank Zero's tech stack plus Lesaka's distribution is a credible challenger to the SA Big Four — particularly in the merchant and SME segment where Tyme is also building.

"Lesaka's operating income jumped 800% YoY in Q3 — a profitability inflection, not a one-off." — CNBC Africa

Threads to track from here: (i) closing of the Bank Zero deal and post-merger integration, (ii) Consumer division trajectory now that the Tyme/Lesaka competitive overlap is direct, (iii) Q4 FY26 earnings as the test of whether the profit print is durable. The Financial Mail's mid-May piece "Here comes the test" signals the market is now watching for sustained delivery, not just the consolidation story.

Africa & EM · TechCabal
Second African fintech in the FT list this week — extending Tuesday's TymeBank thread. The Nigerian community-finance model is a different template than the SA neobank one. Worth watching whether African fintech is now a recognisable global growth category.

Rank Capital named 7th fastest-growing fintech in Africa by FT

TechCabal · Thu 14 May 2026

Rank Capital, the Nigerian community-finance platform, took 7th place in the Financial Times' Africa Fastest-Growing Companies ranking. The piece highlights the "community-powered" model — small-group savings and credit pools, leveraging mobile-money infrastructure. This follows TymeBank's appearance in the same list (covered in Issue 002 on Tuesday).

Read at techcabal.com

Rank Capital is structurally different from TymeBank. Where Tyme is a digital-first retail bank with a GoalSave-anchored individual customer base, Rank is closer to a stokvel-on-rails — community-defined savings and credit pools where the unit of underwriting is a group rather than an individual. The growth narrative is roughly: pool-based credit lets the platform underwrite customers a traditional bureau couldn't price, and the social cohesion of the pool reduces default risk below what individual lending would predict.

Tuesday's "deeply local" thesis from TechCabal (Issue 003) maps onto this directly. Rank's model is not an adapted Western category — it's built around the African informal-savings structure (stokvel, ajo, susu) that doesn't have a direct US analog. Two African fintechs in the FT list this week, both built on locally-native primitives, is a thread worth pulling.

"Two African fintechs in the FT growth list this week. Both built on locally-native primitives. Neither resembles a US comparable." — observation

The Issue 002 thread on TymeBank centred on portfolio validation. The Issue 004 thread on Rank Capital adds a category-validation dimension — African fintech is now a recognisable global growth bucket, not a frontier curiosity.

Sløk: 1970s energy-shock parallels, "the last mile of fighting inflation is a marathon"

Torsten Sløk · Apollo Daily Spark · Thu 14 May 2026

Apollo's Chief Economist Torsten Sløk drew explicit parallels with the 1970s energy shocks in today's Daily Spark, citing the Middle East supply disruption (covered in Issue 003) alongside persistent fiscal-side pressures. Sløk's read: the last mile of fighting inflation is a marathon, not a sprint, with rates likely to stay elevated across the curve.

Read at apolloacademy.com

The 1970s parallel matters more for what it implies about policy reaction than what it says about commodity prices. Sløk's argument: persistent supply-side inflation combined with elevated fiscal deficits leaves central banks with a narrower path. The result is "higher for longer" not because the Fed wants it, but because it has fewer levers if cuts trigger renewed inflation expectations.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 003 covered the 10.5M bpd Middle East shutin as a supply-side signal. Sløk is now putting that supply event into a macro framework that translates into rate-curve expectations.

"The last mile of fighting inflation is a marathon, not a sprint." — Torsten Sløk, Apollo Daily Spark

For Chronos: rate-curve expectations are an input to the discount rate we use across every DCF, and to the cost of capital our LPs implicitly require. If "higher for longer" is the durable regime, IRR hurdles in our private positions stay tight. The narrative also reframes the Lima fundraise environment — LPs evaluating EM private credit against staying in elevated-yield US fixed income is a harder comparison than it was 18 months ago.

EM Charts · Markets
EM tech is now a larger share of MSCI EM than US tech is of the S&P 500. Continues Tuesday's MSCI EM concentration thread — the "EM diversifier" narrative is increasingly fictional. Material for the next Lima LP conversation.

EM tech now a larger index share than US tech — and that's not a footnote

Markets data · This week

A widely-circulated chart this week confirms that the technology sector accounts for a larger share of the MSCI Emerging Markets Index than it does of the S&P 500. Taiwan Semiconductor and Samsung are the dominant names; together with Tencent and Alibaba they push tech-share past the US benchmark. This continues from Tuesday's Issue 002 chart on MSCI EM concentration.

BlackRock weekly commentary

The headline matters because it changes what an LP is buying when they "allocate to EM". A conventional pension allocator who marks 5% of assets to MSCI EM thinks they're diversifying away from US tech. They aren't. They're buying a slightly different tech basket — heavier on semiconductor hardware, lighter on software platforms, but still fundamentally a tech-concentration trade.

The Tuesday thread (Issue 002) established that top-10 names are ~33% of MSCI EM with TSMC alone at 12%. This week's data extends it: by sector composition, MSCI EM is now MORE tech-concentrated than the S&P 500.

"The MSCI EM benchmark is no longer EM. It's a tech-heavy Asia trade with a few smaller names." — observation

For Lima fundraise conversations: this is a sharper version of the diversifier argument. African private PE is now genuinely uncorrelated with what an LP gets from "EM exposure" via the public benchmark — because the benchmark has quietly become a concentrated tech trade. The framing strengthens the case for African private allocation as a real diversifier, not just a return-chase.

US low-carbon investment dropped 9% YoY in Q1; battery manufacturing down 47%

Heatmap News · This week

Rhodium Group analysis cited by Heatmap shows US clean-energy and transportation investment fell to $61bn in Q1 2026 — down 9% YoY and the second consecutive quarterly decline. Battery manufacturing investment dropped 47% YoY after federal EV tax credit elimination. At least 20 proposed data centres were cancelled in Q1, totalling $41.7bn and 3.5GW of demand, citing local pushback.

Read at heatmap.news

The data confirms what the Eversource refusal in Issue 002 hinted at — local-political pushback on hyperscaler load growth is now showing up in cancelled projects, not just utility commentary. 3.5GW of cancelled demand in one quarter is a real signal about where new compute capacity can be physically located.

Battery manufacturing is the bigger headline number. A 47% YoY drop is policy-driven (tax credit removal) and reflects how exposed the US clean-energy investment base was to the federal incentive structure. The transition isn't reversing globally — but the US share of new investment is contracting.

"3.5 GW of US data-centre demand cancelled in a single quarter, citing local pushback. The siting constraint is now binding." — observation

The information is ambient. No portfolio pull-through. It's here as a backdrop for any AI-infra or energy-related conversation we'll have over the next quarter.

AI Frontier · The Batch
Continuing the operating-model thread from Issues 001 and 003. The labs are explicit now that deployment is the bottleneck. Andrew Ng's specific framing — "agentic workflows beat better models" — sharpens what "operating model" actually means.

Andrew Ng: agentic workflows are now beating better models on real tasks

The Batch · DeepLearning.AI · This week

Andrew Ng's recent Batch letters argue that for most real-world tasks, applying agentic workflows to a competent base model produces better results than swapping in a more capable model. The implication: enterprise value isn't where the next 10x of model capability sits — it's in the orchestration layer that wraps current models in iterative review, tool use, and structured reasoning.

Read at deeplearning.ai

Ng's framing converges with the threads from Issue 001 (Fintech Brainfood: 90% see no AI gain because of operating-model gaps) and Issue 003 (OpenAI forming a deployment company because capability isn't the bottleneck). The agentic-workflows-over-models point is the precise mechanism: a GPT-4-class model with proper iteration, tool access, and structured prompts outperforms a frontier model used naively.

What this changes about how we read AI-applied businesses: the moat isn't model selection (which is commodity), nor compute (which is rentable), but the workflow design that turns a competent model into a reliable system. That workflow design is the operating model.

"Agentic workflows on a competent model beat a frontier model used naively. The moat is in the workflow design, not the model choice." — Andrew Ng (paraphrased)

The longitudinal thread: Issue 001 surfaced the productivity-gap puzzle. Issue 003 saw the labs respond by forming deployment companies. Issue 004 sharpens the prescription — agentic workflow design is the specific capability that's actually scarce.
