Build it, buy it, extend it, or retire it — the contest is no longer for customers. It is for the infrastructure beneath them."

South Africa · Banking · Strategy Chart
South Africa's biggest lenders — FNB, Capitec, Standard Bank, Nedbank, and now Absa — have quietly built mobile virtual network operators (MVNOs), turning connectivity from an add-on into core banking infrastructure. FNB Connect's data consumption grew 98% year-on-year between July 2025 and May 2026 (more than 40 petabytes), with device sales above R600m ($36m); FNB says multi-product customers who take both banking and telecom show higher retention, lower churn and greater lifetime value. The country's MVNO market is projected to more than triple from 4.4m active SIMs in 2025 to 14.4m by 2030, driven largely by banking MVNOs. Absa is now preparing to join, which would complete the circle among the major retail banks.

South Africa's banks are becoming telecom companies — connectivity is the rail they build to own the customer's whole digital day

TechCabal · Banking · Phathisani Moyo · Wed 24 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — connectivity is no longer a product, it is acquisition. FNB calls its MVNO a “customer engagement platform and a data-driven business,” using affordable devices and bundled benefits to pull customers in and keep them. Two — the moat is structural, not promotional. Africa Analysis argues banks already hold the four pillars of MVNO success — brand, customer base, distribution and customer operations — plus the credit data to underwrite handsets and tailor bundles that telcos cannot match. Three — data is the new loyalty currency. As voice gives way to WhatsApp, banks reward customers with data rather than discounts, deepening usage of credit and investment products across the ecosystem.

The growth is the banks · connectivity becomes embedded infrastructure for banking itself
TechCabal — “Why South Africa's banks are becoming telecom companies”

We flagged this current on Friday (Issue 027), when the MVNO market's projected tripling to 14.4m SIMs first crossed the desk and Capitec Connect already sat near 41% of its segment. Today's piece adds the operator detail and the strategic logic, and it slots cleanly into this week's thread: where Yango built the city's stack and Prosus turned a holding company into an operator, South Africa's banks are building the connectivity layer themselves — not to sell airtime, but to own the customer's entire digital day.

“South Africa's banks are no longer content to sit inside customers' wallets. They are competing to become the digital ecosystem surrounding them.”

The investable insight is the asymmetry. A bank can enter telecoms because it already owns the four things an MVNO needs — brand, base, distribution, service — plus a credit history no telco holds. The reverse is far harder: a telco cannot easily become a bank. That asymmetry is exactly the “re-bundling” answer to Wednesday's founder-friendly-capital essay — where that piece warned capital is unbundling ownership from economics, here the incumbents are doing the opposite, stacking financial and non-financial services into a single relationship the customer cannot cheaply leave.

The thread to hold forward: South Africa is running an early, observable experiment in ecosystem-led banking that the whole continent will read. If banking MVNOs genuinely lift retention and lifetime value rather than just subsidising data, the model travels — and the question for any African financial-services position becomes whether the incumbent owns the surrounding ecosystem or merely rents space inside someone else's. The risk worth watching is regulatory and margin: connectivity is a thin, capital-hungry business, and a bank that mis-prices data to buy loyalty can import a telco's economics without a telco's scale.

Africa · M&A · Banking Tests a thesis
In February, Egypt's Beltone Holding paid $227.13m (€197.6m) for Baobab Group, a pan-African lender operating across seven countries. Three months later, by Q1 2026, Baobab contributed 53% of Beltone's EGP6.8bn ($136.7m) operating revenue — its single largest business line. Group gross lending grew 236% year-on-year to EGP101.1bn ($2.03bn), of which Baobab alone was EGP60.9bn ($1.22bn); roughly six of every ten pounds the group now lends originate from the acquired business. The deal also brought EGP37.3bn ($749.8m) in deposits and a footprint that now extends well beyond Egypt. Profit after tax slipped 1% to EGP695m ($14.0m) on one-off integration and expansion costs.

Beltone's $227m Baobab bet is already paying off — buy the rail, don't build it market by market

TechCabal · Banking · Temitayo Jaiyeola · Tue 23 Jun 2026

A live test of the build-versus-buy question that runs under this whole edition. Rather than grind out cross-border expansion country by country, Beltone acquired a ready-made pan-African lending platform and let it become the group's centre of gravity within a quarter. The move mirrors a broader African consolidation wave — in March, Visa-backed Moniepoint bought restaurant-management platform Orda to deepen its merchant ecosystem — in which scale and capability are increasingly purchased, not constructed. The early caveat is honest: profitability dipped on integration costs, the unglamorous tax every acquirer pays before synergies show up.

Capability bought, not built — the acquired business becomes the group within a single quarter
TechCabal — “Lender Baobab becomes Beltone's biggest business three months after acquisition”

This is Wednesday's lead question answered from the other side of the table. The 22-investor column told us a licensed lender disbursing at scale is not a return — the return is a buyer who pays. Here is the buyer: Beltone, writing a $227m cheque for exactly the kind of pan-African lending platform the African M&A wave is built to consume. The exit one founder waits for is the capability another operator chooses to buy rather than build, and the speed of the read-through — 53% of revenue in one quarter — is what makes the buy-side maths work.

“Rather than expand market by market, the company acquired a pan-African lender with operations across seven countries — and within three months it had become the group's largest source of revenue.”

For a Chronos screen, two things are worth underlining. First, this is a clean data point for the “name the buyer” discipline we set out on Wednesday: African cross-border consolidation is now producing real strategic acquirers paying nine-figure cheques for in-continent platforms, which is precisely the liquidity event the asset class has lacked. Second, it is a reminder that the multiple lives or dies on integration. Beltone's profit dipped on one-off costs, and a deal that looks accretive on revenue can still destroy value if the acquired book carries hidden credit risk or the cultures do not merge. The headline says the bet is paying off; the footnotes say ask for the loan-loss trajectory before you believe it.

The thread to hold forward: watch whether Egypt becomes a hub for outbound African financial-services M&A. If a Cairo-listed holding company can buy a seven-country lender and re-rate on it, the template — acquire the platform, inherit the deposits, expand the balance sheet — will be copied, and the supply of credible buyers for the continent's better lending businesses thickens. That is good news for anyone holding one, and a prompt to ask, of any African credit position, who the three plausible acquirers are and what they have actually paid.

Nigeria · Fintech · Funding Hyper-local
Nigerian fintech Daya has raised a $2.4m oversubscribed pre-seed round to build stablecoin-powered payment infrastructure for African businesses. Hivemind Capital (New York) led, with Lattice Fund, Alliance DAO, the Aptos Foundation and Singapore's Globelink Investment participating. Founded in October 2025 by Tomiwa “Aleph” Lasebikan and Paul Joe, Daya gives businesses dollar-denominated accounts, settles transactions in stablecoins, and moves money cross-border through regulated banking partners plus blockchain rails — growing more than 40% month-on-month in 2026. This month it opened a settlement corridor to the Middle East with Aptos and Dubai's HashKey MENA. Stablecoins settled roughly $28tn globally in 2025 (Chainalysis); Daya joins Yellow Card and Juicyway betting they become a mainstream rail for B2B payments.

Nigeria's Daya raises $2.4m to lay a stablecoin rail for African business payments

TechCabal · Startups · Emmanuel Nwosu · Wed 24 Jun 2026

A small cheque with an outsized signal. The thesis is infrastructure, not crypto: African businesses still stitch together local banks, domiciliary accounts, FX desks, OTC relationships and spreadsheets to move money across borders, producing delayed settlement, opaque FX and trapped working capital. Daya's pitch is to collapse that into one layer — receive in dollars, hold or settle in stablecoins, pay out in local currency — using blockchain for settlement and regulated institutions for fiat on- and off-ramps. The investor list (Hivemind, Lattice, Alliance DAO, Aptos, Globelink) is a vote that the rail, not the wallet, is where the value sits.

TechCabal — “Alliance-backed fintech Daya raises $2.4 million to build stablecoin payment rails”

Read Daya next to Wednesday's Yellow Card item and the pattern is unmistakable: the African stablecoin story has decisively moved from consumer trading to regulated B2B settlement and treasury. Yellow Card shut its retail app and walked into a Swiss vault to become supervised institutional plumbing; Daya is being funded, at the earliest stage, to build the same kind of pipe. The money in stablecoins is accruing to whoever owns the compliant rail between businesses and borders, not to the token or the app on top.

“The question is no longer whether stablecoins can carry cross-border business payments, but who owns the regulated rail they run on.”

For Chronos the read is twofold. First, this is another data point that the binding constraint in African cross-border commerce is settlement friction, and that the durable businesses being built against it are infrastructure plays with regulated-partner dependencies — which means the diligence question is always about the banking and licensing relationships behind the blockchain, not the chain itself. Second, the early-stage, oversubscribed, US- and Asia-led cap table is a useful tell about where conviction capital is flowing even as headline African venture stays thin: into the picks-and-shovels of money movement. The caution is obvious — a $2.4m pre-seed in a field that already includes Yellow Card, Juicyway and others is a bet on execution in a crowding category, and most of these rails will not survive as standalone businesses.

The thread to hold forward: watch consolidation. If stablecoin settlement is genuinely becoming Africa's cross-border layer, the same M&A logic that put Baobab inside Beltone today will eventually apply here — the larger regulated rails will buy the smaller ones for their corridors and licences. The interesting question for a future screen is which of today's seed-stage pipes is being built to be acquired, and by whom.

Nigeria · Telecoms · Infrastructure Structural
MTN Group, the continent's largest operator, plans to shut down some of its 3G networks before 2030 and repurpose the spectrum for 4G and 5G, chief commercial, strategy and transformation officer Selorm Adadevoh told its 11 June Capital Markets Day. In Nigeria, 3G penetration has collapsed to 5.32% of connections (April 2026) against 54.41% for 4G — yet 2G still holds 35.93% and is judged “still relevant.” The four original 3G licences cost $150m each in 2007 ($600m total) and powered the BlackBerry era and Nigeria's first mobile-internet economy. The decommissioning frees spectrum and tower capacity for networks “where demand and revenue growth are increasingly concentrated.” Some experts caution that 4G needs 85–90% of connections before a safe switch-off; MTN's 4G population coverage is 84.6%.

After two decades, Nigeria's 3G era nears its final call — MTN prepares to switch a rail off to fund the next one

TechCabal · Telecoms · Frank Eleanya · Mon 22 Jun 2026

An infrastructure-lifecycle story dressed as a nostalgia piece. The technology that carried Nigeria from a voice-first market into a mobile-internet economy is now the one with “an economic equation that is not very promising,” in Adadevoh's words — slower than 4G, less useful than 2G's legacy support, and occupying spectrum and towers that earn more when repointed at newer networks. The decommissioning is capital recycling by another name: retire the asset that no longer pays, redeploy the spectrum beneath it. The dissent is about timing — with 4G at ~54% of connections and one expert wanting 85–90% before a clean shutdown, the migration, not the intent, is the open question.

TechCabal — “After two decades, Nigeria's 3G era is nearing its final call”

Set this beside today's lead and the edition's spine comes into focus. South Africa's banks are building a connectivity rail; MTN is retiring one. Both are the same discipline — deciding which infrastructure deserves capital and which does not — and it is the operating analogue of Wednesday's Prosus story, where the market finally paid for a portfolio that earns rather than a single moonshot. Switching off 3G is not a retreat; it is the unglamorous portfolio management that frees spectrum and balance sheet for the 4G and 5G layers that actually generate return.

“With fewer users on 3G, telcos are committing resources to a network that no longer delivers adequate returns. Decommissioning frees spectrum and tower capacity for where the revenue growth is.”

For Chronos the relevant lens is asset lifecycle and capital allocation, which is why this earns space alongside the deal news. The same operator, MTN, is simultaneously retiring old infrastructure here, building subsea AI cables, and pushing into direct lending off its own book — a coherent story about an incumbent recycling capital out of mature, low-return assets and into the rails of the next cycle. For any infrastructure or telecoms position, the question this prompts is blunt: is management actively retiring what no longer earns, or carrying legacy assets out of inertia? A 3G shutdown is a small signal of a management team willing to do the unsentimental work.

The thread to hold forward: the migration risk is real and worth tracking. Switch 3G off before 4G coverage and device affordability are truly there, and an operator strands the very low-income and rural users that 2G still serves — a reminder that capital recycling in frontier markets carries a social and regulatory cost that pure-financial logic understates. The intent is sound; the execution window is where the value, and the reputational risk, actually sits.

AI · Power · Frontier Wouldn't have seen otherwise
In Import AI #462, Jack Clark surfaces a major new study from Oxford, the UK AI Security Institute, Stanford and the LSE: across four experiments — 18,978 conversations with 6,923 people — frontier AI was “reliably more persuasive than expert humans,” even when those humans chose the topic, prepared for hours, practised against the model, and were paid £1,000 bonuses. On consequential behaviour, AI was nearly 3x more effective than professional canvassers at raising real-money donations to Save the Children. The edge comes from the sheer rate at which AI deploys information: constrain it to human writing speed and length and the gap collapses to zero. The strongest persuaders were Claude Opus 4.1 and 4.6, followed by models from OpenAI, Google and xAI.

Frontier AI now out-persuades expert humans — and whoever controls that capability holds a new lever on everyone else

Import AI #462 · Jack Clark · Sun 22 Jun 2026

The finding is clean and the implication is not. Training humans narrowed but never closed the gap; only throttling the AI to human speed did. The authors frame a societal fork: persuasion this cheap and this effective could consolidate influence among already-powerful actors — advertising and marketing become far more potent — or, if widely available, it could arm under-resourced actors (small charities, public defenders, grassroots advocates) against better-funded rivals. “The question is no longer whether AI can out-persuade humans but how, where, and on whose behalf this capability will be exercised.” No recommendation attaches; the point is to see the capability clearly before it is priced in.

Import AI #462 — “Superpersuasion; self-sustaining AI; paths to ASI”

This is the rail that sits above all the others in today's edition, and the one partners are least likely to have seen. Every other item is about owning physical or financial infrastructure — connectivity, lending, settlement, spectrum. This is about owning a cognitive one: the capacity to change what people believe and what they do with their money. The study's hardest result is the Save the Children test — not a lab score but real donations, where a frontier model beat trained professional fundraisers by a wide margin. When influence becomes a measurable, purchasable capability, the question of who controls it stops being abstract.

“One effect of AI that can out-persuade even human experts could be a consolidation of influence among already-powerful actors.”

For Chronos there are two distinct reads. The first folds straight back into Wednesday's SAFA item: the speculative idea of “owning the crowd” becomes concrete the moment persuasion can be automated and rented — any business sitting on an identifiable base now faces both an opportunity (engage it far more effectively) and a threat (a competitor, or a bad actor, can do the same at scale). The second is for the Spock framework itself: this is exactly the kind of capability evidence we screen for — a frontier model demonstrating a real-world, revenue-relevant edge over skilled humans — and it should sharpen how we assess any portfolio company whose moat rests on customer relationships, sales forces or marketing spend, all of which this technology reprices.

The thread to hold forward is governance, not deployment. The authors are deliberately neutral — market allocation makes advertising more potent and creates externalities; state control risks concentrating a dangerous capability — and that neutrality is the right posture for us too. We are not in the business of acting on this; we are in the business of noticing, early, that a general-purpose persuasion capability now exists, works on real money, and will quietly change the economics of every consumer-facing business we look at. Worth re-reading in full.

Nigeria · Fintech · Ecosystem For the portfolio
Paystack, the Stripe-owned Nigerian fintech, has launched a Small Business Program, starting with a bundle that gives 2,000 eligible merchants partner discounts worth up to ₦4m ($2,923) across commerce, bookkeeping, logistics, design, workspace and customer communication — partners include Canva, Shuttlers, Bumpa, Simplebks and FezDelivery. Eligibility requires an active Nigerian Paystack account with at least 10 transactions in the prior 30 days. Two further initiatives — a hands-on Launchpad and a funding Grant — will follow. The move extends Paystack's push beyond payments, after it acquired a microfinance-bank licence in January. Nigeria's MSMEs are 46.3% of GDP, 96.9% of businesses and 87.9% of employment (2021 NBS-SMEDAN survey).

Paystack extends its rail past payments — bundling the whole small-business stack to deepen the merchant relationship

TechCabal · Startups · Opeyemi Kareem · Tue 23 Jun 2026

The strategic move under a modest launch. Paystack is doing to Nigerian merchants what FNB is doing to South African banking customers and what Yango did with its internal tools: taking a business built on one rail — payments — and wrapping the surrounding services around it so the relationship deepens and the customer has fewer reasons to leave. “If Paystack is helping businesses collect payments,” the programme lead put it, “we also have an opportunity to help connect them to the tools, partners, networks and support that make those payments translate into growth.” The microfinance-bank licence in January was the first step past payments; this is the second.

TechCabal — “Paystack rolls out $2,900 support package for Nigerian small businesses”

Strip away the press-release framing and this is the merchant-side version of today's recurring move: a payments processor extending its rail into the rest of the customer's operations — bookkeeping, logistics, commerce, design — to convert a transactional relationship into an ecosystem one. It is the same logic as the SA banks building telecoms and Yango selling its internal stack: own more of the customer's day and the switching cost rises. That Paystack is Stripe-owned makes it a useful proxy for how a global platform thinks about deepening into an African base it already holds.

“We want to help small businesses move from simply transacting to building the systems, relationships and opportunities they need for their next stage of growth.”

For Chronos the relevance is to any portfolio or pipeline business whose growth depends on small-merchant relationships — the read-through is that payments alone is becoming table stakes, and the defensible position is the surrounding stack of tools and credit. Paystack's January microfinance licence plus this programme sketch a clear direction: from processing payments, to lending against the data those payments generate, to bundling the operating tools that keep the merchant inside the ecosystem. It is the embedded-finance flywheel run by an incumbent with distribution, and it raises the bar for any standalone tool trying to sell the same merchants one feature at a time.

The thread to hold forward: watch whether the Launchpad and especially the Grant materialise with real capital, because a discounts bundle is cheap goodwill while a funding programme is a genuine commitment to the merchant base. If Paystack starts deploying its own balance sheet into the small businesses on its rail, it moves from facilitator to financier — the same build-out MTN is attempting in lending — and the competitive question for anyone serving African SMEs becomes whether they own the payment relationship that makes such lending possible, or merely sit beside it.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Make the model your sceptic — ask it to argue against your own conclusion before you commit to it

Two items today reward a sceptical second pass. Beltone's Baobab bet “is already paying off,” the headline says — but the same piece quietly notes profit dipped on integration costs. And a study finds AI can out-persuade expert humans, which is exactly why you should not let a confident, fluent model talk you into your own view. The most valuable thing these models do is not agree with you faster; it is disagree with you well. Before you act on a thesis — back this company, run this deal, take this position — hand the model your reasoning and instruct it to take the other side as hard as it can. You are not looking for reassurance. You are buying, for free, the bear case a good analyst would have made you sit through.

A worked example

Paste your conclusion and the reasoning behind it, then prompt: “You are a sceptical investment-committee member who thinks I am wrong. Give me the three strongest arguments against this view, the single piece of evidence that would most change my mind, and the one question I have not asked. Do not soften it — assume I have already heard the bull case.” Read what comes back, then decide. If the bear case folds under scrutiny, you hold your view with more conviction; if it does not, you have just avoided an expensive mistake before it cost anything. Make this the last step before any decision that matters.
