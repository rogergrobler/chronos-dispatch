Africa & EM · Nigeria → East Africa · Credit Infrastructure Funding
CreditChek, a Nigerian credit-data company, raised $600,000 to enter East Africa — Janngo Capital leading, with returning investor Assembly Investors and newcomers Vastly Valuable Ventures and Unipeg Capital. Founded in 2022 by Kingsley Ibe and Lionel Orishane, it gives banks, fintechs and microlenders credit intelligence, income verification and real-time risk assessment built on credit-bureau, open-banking and alternative data. It is expanding into Kenya, Uganda and Rwanda, aimed at what Janngo's Fatoumata Bâ calls Africa's estimated $331bn MSME financing gap.

CreditChek raises $600k to build the credit-data layer beneath East Africa's lenders

Habiba Katsha · POCIT (via TechCabal) · Tue 9 Jun 2026
CreditChek · Janngo Capital · TechCabal · Spock

Three reads. One — the infrastructure, not the loan. CreditChek doesn't lend; it sells the decisioning data — income verification, real-time risk scoring — that lets others lend to thin-file borrowers. The moat is the data pipe, not the balance sheet. Two — alternative data is the wedge. By pulling from open banking and non-bureau sources it scores the borrowers a traditional file misses — the same capability that makes Blnk's book work, sold as a service. Three — the gap is the market. Bâ frames it as Africa's ~$331bn MSME financing gap; a $600k cheque is small, but it buys distribution into three new credit ecosystems at once.

POCIT — CreditChek's East Africa raise

Yesterday's lead was Payaza earning a rating; today is the layer beneath it. A rating prices an institution's own debt; CreditChek prices the borrowers that institution lends to — and sells that capability to every lender at once. It is the clearest picks-and-shovels read on the credit-deepening thesis the screen has tracked all week: the durable margin in African credit is accruing to whoever owns the underwriting data, not whoever holds the loan.

"By enabling lenders to make better decisions using alternative data, the company is helping expand access to financing for millions of underserved individuals and businesses while addressing Africa's estimated $331 billion MSME financing gap." — Fatoumata Bâ, Founder & Executive Chair, Janngo Capital, via TechCabal

The portfolio line runs straight to Optasia: a decisioning surface that prices thin-file borrowers is valuable wherever it is dropped, and it compounds with every new market and every new lender that plugs in. The watch-item is data rights and bureau cooperation across three regulators at once — the capability travels only as far as the data-sharing rules of Kenya, Uganda and Rwanda will let it.

AI Frontier · Platform Shift · Value Capture Thinker
On the a16z podcast, technology analyst Benedict Evans argued that foundation models are commoditising — moving from research breakthrough to infrastructure, the way the internet and the personal computer did. As the models become accessible and the underlying technology matures, he says, competitive advantage shifts from owning the model to building applications on top of it; the value accrues to the services layer, just as it did with email and browsers over raw internet plumbing.

Benedict Evans: the foundation model is now a commodity — value moves up to the application

Benedict Evans · The a16z Show (via StartupHub) · Thu 4 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — models are becoming plumbing. Evans likens today's LLMs to the early internet and the PC: once the hard part is a utility, owning it stops being the edge. Two — value migrates to the app. The companies that won the internet built email and browsers, not the pipes; the AI equivalent is the workflow, not the weights. Three — the question changes. "What can AI do?" becomes "what problem does it solve for me?" — the barrier to building drops, and ingenuity at the application layer, not model access, becomes the scarce input.

StartupHub — "Foundation Models: A Commodity, Says Benedict Evans"

This is the framework sitting under the week's African items. If the model is a commodity, then the value in any AI thesis lives in proprietary data, distribution and workflow — exactly where CreditChek (data), MTN (distribution) and Standard Chartered (workflow) are each making their move. It also reframes the compute-as-commodity thread from earlier in the week: cheap, metered intelligence is good news for the application builder and bad news for anyone whose moat was access to the model.

Companies that focused on creating services like email or web browsers, rather than building the internet infrastructure, "ultimately captured more value." — Benedict Evans, The a16z Show, via StartupHub

For the screen, the test sharpens. When scoring a company's AI story, discount anything whose edge is "we use a good model," and weight anything whose edge is a dataset, a customer relationship or a process no competitor can copy. The model is table stakes; the application is the asset — and that is the lens to carry into every Spock scorecard from here.

Africa & EM · Venture Structure · Institutional Capital Structural
Norfund, Norway's development finance institution, committed $60m to Ventures Platform's second fund — pre-seed to early-Series-A across Africa. The notable part is the structure: rather than pick startups, the DFI is backing a manager. Ventures Platform has invested in 75+ companies and 140+ founders (Paystack, Moniepoint, PiggyVest, LemFi, OmniRetail, Thrive Agric), and its portfolio has pulled in over $1bn of follow-on capital. Existing LPs include the IFC, British International Investment, Proparco and Standard Bank. African startups raised ~$4.1bn in 2025, with Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt and South Africa taking roughly 82%.

Norfund routes $60m through a fund manager, not startups — African venture capital institutionalises

Habeeb Ajala · Tech With Africa / African Exponent · Sat 6 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — the DFI is buying a portfolio, not a bet. Backing Ventures Platform's fund rather than companies spreads risk and rents local judgement — the institutional answer to early-stage selection. Two — follow-on is the scoreboard. Ventures Platform's companies have attracted $1bn+ after its cheques; the multiplier, not the entry valuation, is what an LP is really underwriting. Three — concentration is the unsolved problem. With ~82% of African funding in four markets, a fund pushing into Francophone West Africa is as much a geographic diversification play as a financial one.

Tech With Africa — Norfund's $60m into Ventures Platform

The organisational-design signal the screen weights: how capital is structured tells you more about a market's maturity than how much of it there is. A DFI choosing a manager over a cap table is the same logic a fund-of-funds applies in developed markets — and it is a vote that African early-stage selection is now a skill worth paying a specialist to hold.

Norfund joins existing limited partners including the International Finance Corporation, British International Investment, Proparco and Standard Bank Group in backing the manager rather than the companies. — Tech With Africa

For Chronos the read is two-sided. As allocators, the follow-on multiplier is the metric that matters — a seed manager is only as good as the later capital it unlocks. As operators, the concentration number — four countries, ~82% of the continent's funding — is the standing reminder that "African" exposure is mostly Nigerian, Kenyan, Egyptian and South African exposure until a fund deliberately buys its way out.

Africa & EM · Nigeria · Telco Economics
At MTN Nigeria's "Data on Trial" event in Lagos, CEO Karl Toriola rejected demands for cheap, unlimited mobile data as a physical and economic impossibility. Cellular capacity is finite per tower; true uncapped mobility exists only behind premium pricing or fair-usage throttling. Cheap unlimited, he argued, would saturate towers, degrade service for everyone, and starve the capital expenditure needed for spectrum, fibre backhaul and power — against a backdrop of currency devaluation, inflation, and the 50% tariff increase the NCC approved last year.

MTN does the arithmetic of spectrum — and tells Nigeria cheap unlimited data is a fiction

Peter Oluka · Techeconomy · Mon 8 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — capacity is physics, not policy. Unlike fibre, a cell tower has a hard ceiling of simultaneous streams; "unlimited" on mobile is everywhere bounded by fair-usage rules or roughly $400-a-month pricing. Two — the real warning is capex starvation. Toriola's aviation analogy — unlimited cheap tickets bankrupt the airline — is about the capital to maintain and expand the network, not consumer fairness. Three — the macro vice is the subtext. Devaluation and inflation have pushed costs up while purchasing power falls; the sector wants tariff room, and "unlimited" is the line it will not cross.

Techeconomy — "The Hard Mathematics of Spectrum"

Yesterday MTN's One TV bet that owning the rail beats owning the catalogue; today MTN draws the rail's hard limit. The very distribution asset that makes One TV plausible — 307m subscribers, Mobile Money — runs on spectrum that does not scale like fibre, and Toriola is publishing the constraint rather than hiding it.

"You can never build enough capacity for everyone to be on an unlimited bundle, and you think you will provide quality service that will be decent." — Karl Toriola, CEO, MTN Nigeria, via Techeconomy

It threads back to the compute-as-commodity argument from earlier in the week, inverted: compute got cheap and metered, while spectrum is scarce and must be rationed. For any portfolio business that resells connectivity or rides telco rails, the lesson is to price for the bottleneck, not the marketing promise — and to watch the next NCC tariff round, because the operators have just told the regulator where the floor is.

Africa & EM · Venture Funding · AI & Labour
TC Insights reports African startups crossed $1.3bn in funding by 3 June 2026, with Spiro's $215m e-mobility round the month's opening mega-deal. The H1 pace ($1.044bn excluding Spiro) tracks 2025's $1.056bn, leaving the ecosystem needing only ~$121m more to beat H1 2025's $1.42bn record. May's deals were led by Nala ($50m), LemFi ($34.8m) and MAX ($8m). The sharper signal: 2026 has already logged 1,000+ tracked layoffs against 698 a year earlier, with companies such as Jumia and Zap Africa now explicitly citing AI, not inflation, as the cause.

Africa's funding clears $1.3bn — and 2026's AI-cited layoffs already top 1,000

Joseph Oloyede · TC Insights · Thu 4 Jun 2026
TC Insights · State of Tech in Africa H1 2026 tracker · Spock

Three reads. One — resilience on the top line. $1.3bn by early June, within touching distance of last year's H1 record despite a global capital chill — investor appetite for African innovation is holding. Two — the layoff curve has bent. 1,000+ jobs cut in 2026 against 698 in the same window of 2025, and the cited reason has shifted from market correction to AI automation. Three — the two are one story. Capital is concentrating in fewer, leaner, more automated companies; funding up and headcount down is not a contradiction but the model.

TC Insights — "The billion-dollar sprint"

This is the continent-scale version of yesterday's Standard Chartered item. The bank published its own substitution; TC Insights now has the aggregate — 1,000+ roles gone in 2026 with AI named as the driver — and it lands in the same week Benedict Evans explains the mechanism: when the model is a commodity, the efficiency it buys shows up first on the payroll.

"These are no longer just market corrections due to inflation; companies are now explicitly citing AI as the driver." — TC Insights, State of Tech in Africa

For the screen the discipline is to read funding and employment together: a sector raising more while hiring less is compounding margin, which is good for the equity and corrosive for the consumer base — the same tension Kenya's PMI flagged on Monday. The watch-item for H1 is whether the $121m gap to a new record closes on a handful of Spiro-shaped mega-deals or on breadth; the former is a concentration story, the latter a genuine deepening.

Africa & EM · Côte d'Ivoire · Financial Inclusion
REMA/OS, an Ivorian fintech founded in December 2025, launched a B2B offline payment protocol: an SDK that banks embed in their own apps so users transact peer-to-peer over Bluetooth with no internet or mobile data. It targets rural communities and agricultural cooperatives across the UEMOA bloc, where 100m+ adults are unbanked and Côte d'Ivoire alone has 4,000+ cooperatives in poorly-connected areas. Bootstrapped, it monetises via bank licensing fees, transaction fees and integration — with no direct consumer-acquisition cost.

In Côte d'Ivoire, REMA builds payments that need no internet at all

Lindsay Eke · Tech With Africa / Disrupt Africa · Tue 9 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — connectivity is the constraint it removes. Wave and most rivals need users online; REMA's Bluetooth protocol works where there is no signal — which is precisely where the unbanked are. Two — the model is B2B, not consumer. Selling an SDK to banks sidesteps the acquisition cost that has bled consumer fintechs, monetising through licensing, transaction and integration fees. Three — the market is structurally large. 100m+ unbanked adults across UEMOA and 4,000+ Ivorian cooperatives is an inclusion gap that connectivity-dependent rails simply cannot reach.

Tech With Africa — REMA/OS offline payment protocol

The whole week has been about which layer owns the value — rating, data, distribution, model. REMA picks the one everyone assumes away: the network itself. An offline-first protocol is a reminder that across much of the UEMOA the binding constraint is not credit scoring or distribution but a working connection, and that the company solving it sells to banks rather than chasing consumers.

"There is currently no offline-first payment protocol deployed at scale in West Africa. That is the gap REMA addresses." — Ibrahim Junior Konaté, co-founder, REMA/OS, via Disrupt Africa

It is early — proof-of-concept, bootstrapped, unproven at scale — and the obvious risks are security on an offline rail and whether banks will integrate a year-old SDK. But it is exactly the kind of hyper-local, infrastructure-first bet the partners would not otherwise see, and a useful counterweight to a funding map that says African fintech is four capital cities and a credit-scoring API.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Put the team's best prompts where everyone can reach them — build a shared Project

Nic's note on yesterday's tip was the right next step: saving your own winning prompt is good; sharing it is better. A Project — Claude's shared workspace — lets a small team pin the prompts, context and reference documents it keeps returning to: the board-pack template, the deal-screen question set, the house view on a sector. Every partner then starts from the same high-water mark instead of each re-deriving it. The value compounds twice — once because nobody re-writes the prompt, and again because the context (what "good" looks like for us, which numbers we care about, how we phrase a risk) lives in one place and quietly improves every answer drawn from it. It is the discipline any partnership already applies to its investment memos: write the house standard down once, keep it where the team works, and let the next person inherit it rather than reinvent it.

A worked example

A "Chronos Deal Screen" Project holding the firm's scoring rubric, three past screens we were happy with, and one standing prompt: "Screen [COMPANY] against our rubric — flag the single number that would kill the deal and the single number that would make it, in under 200 words." Drop a new teaser in and the answer arrives already speaking our language, because the Project carries the context the prompt used to have to spell out. Add the next good prompt as you find it, and the shared file — not any one partner's memory — becomes where the firm's best thinking accumulates.
