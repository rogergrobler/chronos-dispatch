Africa & EM · Kenya · Fiscal & Digital Rails Budget Day
Kenya's Treasury tabled its largest-ever budget on Thursday — Ksh4.82 trillion for FY2026/27 — with debt service alone swallowing 31% of every shilling and a deficit of Ksh1.11tn (5.3% of GDP). Funding it leans on the Finance Bill 2026, whose roughly Ksh120bn of new measures reach straight into the digital economy: a 16% VAT on the fees of 40-plus payment platforms (M-Pesa, Airtel Money and peers) and a 25% excise on phones charged at activation. Safaricom says the combined effect would lift mobile-money transaction costs by 33.4% — VAT on a Ksh5,000 M-Pesa transfer rising from zero to Ksh9.12 — while KPMG warned it could set financial-inclusion gains back “almost a thousand steps.”

Kenya tables its biggest-ever budget — and the Finance Bill puts a price on the mobile-money rails

National Treasury · Citizen Digital / Money254 · Thu 11 Jun 2026
National Treasury · Citizen Digital · Safaricom via Money254 · Spock

Three reads. One — the rails are now a tax base. A decade of financial inclusion built mobile money into the default rail; the fiscus has noticed, and a 16% VAT on platform fees treats that rail as infrastructure ripe to tax. Two — the pass-through is the politics. The Treasury insists ordinary users are not targeted; the operators reply that VAT and excise flow straight to the customer, and 33.4% is the number they put on it. Three — debt is the real author. With interest payments alone exceeding the entire education budget, the Finance Bill yield is load-bearing — weaken it in committee and the whole consolidation plan unravels into more borrowing.

Money254 — “Finance Bill 2026 will increase mobile-money fees by 33.4% — telcos”

Yesterday MTN drew the hard limit of spectrum and told a regulator where the price floor sits; today a treasury answers from the other side of the same rail and tells the operators where the tax sits. It is the cost-of-the-digital-rails thread, advanced one move: the very inclusion infrastructure the continent spent a decade building is now mature enough — and lucrative enough — for a cash-strapped state to meter.

“Average weekly earnings … the combined effect of VAT and excise duty would significantly increase transaction charges by 33.4 per cent.” — Safaricom, via Money254; KPMG warned the measures could set inclusion back “almost a thousand steps.”

The pattern travels well beyond Nairobi. Every African market that turned mobile money into a public utility has handed its treasury a broad, legible, hard-to-evade tax base, and fiscal pressure across the region makes the Kenyan template a leading indicator rather than a local quirk. For any business that rides those rails, the watch-item is incidence: who actually absorbs a levy on the pipe — the platform, the merchant, or the thin-margin user the rail was built to reach.

The structural tell is in the budget arithmetic. Debt service consuming 31 cents of every shilling is what forces the reach into the digital economy in the first place; the National Infrastructure Fund being seeded partly by selling the state's Safaricom stake closes the loop — the government is taxing the rail and selling the operator in the same fiscal breath.

Africa & EM · Nigeria · Payments → Credit Structural
At the launch of the Central Bank's Nigeria Payments System Vision 2028, Moniepoint founder Tosin Eniolorunda argued the next phase of growth is not more payments but credit layered directly onto the payment rails — using the transaction data those rails already generate to underwrite the millions of small businesses formal credit has always missed. CBN Governor Olayemi Cardoso framed the ambition as a collaboration problem across regulators, banks and fintechs; Moniepoint frames it as a data one. The country's National Payment Stack has already cleared more than 153,000 pilot transactions.

Moniepoint's next act: pour credit onto the payment rails, and let the data do the underwriting

Samuel Daniel · Nairametrics · Tue 2 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — the data is the moat, not the rail. Payments established visibility and trust; the value now is in turning that exhaust into underwriting for borrowers a traditional file cannot see. Two — the operator wants the whole stack. Moniepoint built dominance on agent banking and payments; layering credit on top is vertical integration dressed as financial inclusion. Three — it sharpens yesterday's question. Does the underwriting edge accrue to a neutral data utility sold to every lender, or to the distribution owner sitting on its own captive flow?

Nairametrics — “Moniepoint CEO pushes for credit layering on Nigeria's payment infrastructure”

Yesterday's lead was CreditChek — an independent decisioning layer that sells underwriting capability to every lender at once. Today the continent's most aggressive payments operator says it will build the same capability inside its own walls, on its own captive transaction flow. The two read as a single strategic fork: the picks-and-shovels neutral utility versus the integrated distribution owner, both chasing the same Africa-wide credit gap from opposite ends.

“The next phase of growth will come from layering services like credit onto existing payment flows, using the visibility and trust already built through financial transactions.” — Tosin Eniolorunda, founder & CEO, Moniepoint, via Nairametrics

For the screen, the discriminating test is data rights and defensibility. A neutral utility compounds with every new lender that plugs in; an operator compounds with every merchant it locks in — but only one of them is exposed if a regulator decides transaction data is a shared public good rather than a private asset. Nigeria's PSV 2028 framing of ‘collaboration’ is exactly where that line will be drawn.

The portfolio-relevant read is that ‘payments’ is maturing into ‘financial services distributed through payment rails’ — lending, savings and insurance riding flows already embedded in everyday commerce. The durable margin accrues to whoever owns the underwriting decision, wherever it is dropped.

AI Frontier · Payments Infrastructure · Agentic Commerce
Mastercard has switched on Agent Pay for Machines — an open protocol that lets AI agents pay one another at machine speed, down to fractions of a cent, with the spending permissions a human grants an agent written onto a public blockchain (initially Polygon) so any counterparty can verify the bot is acting as instructed. Thirty-one launch partners include Adyen, Stripe, Coinbase and Cloudflare; Stripe and Tempo's rival Machine Payments Protocol and Coinbase's x402 are laying competing track for the same machine economy. Chief product officer Jorn Lambert was candid that it will not move revenue next year, but called it a “meaningful new addressable market” over five.

Mastercard switches on payments built for machines — the rails for agent-to-agent money go live

Ben Weiss · Fortune · Wed 10 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — the incumbents are racing to own a rail before the traffic exists. Agentic payment volumes are still a rounding error, yet the card networks, Stripe and Coinbase are all building standards now to be the default later. Two — trust moves on-chain. Putting agent permissions on a public ledger, not a private database, is the design choice that lets strangers' bots transact — verification, not the payment, is the hard part. Three — the moat is the standard. Whoever's protocol becomes the lingua franca captures the toll; this is a land grab dressed as plumbing.

Fortune — “Mastercard launches protocol to let AI agents pay each other”

This is the application layer Benedict Evans described yesterday, made concrete. If the foundation model is a commodity, value migrates to the workflow and the rails beneath it — and the rail for machine-to-machine money is exactly the kind of durable, standards-defined asset that compounds. The notable part is the verification design: agent permissions on a public chain so counterparties can check a bot before they transact with it.

“Am I expecting that this is going to be a huge revenue driver for Mastercard next year? No. Do I think it'll be a meaningful new addressable market for us over the next five years? I think so.” — Jorn Lambert, chief product officer, Mastercard, via Fortune

For any portfolio business that moves money, the watch-item is fragmentation versus convergence. Three incompatible protocols — Mastercard's, Stripe/Tempo's, Coinbase's — is how every payments shift starts; the question is whether one becomes the rail or whether an interoperability layer sits on top, because the toll accrues very differently in each case.

Read against the African items, it is a study in contrast: Nairobi is taxing a mature human-payment rail while New York builds a machine one from scratch. Both are arguments about who owns the pipe — and the value, as ever, sits with the owner, not the traffic.

Markets & Capital · The Business of IPOs Thinker
Marc Rubinstein's Net Interest reaches back to Ford's January 1956 float — then the largest IPO in US history, a 722-firm syndicate, the company referred to in all correspondence only as “X” — to anatomise how the business of going public has changed, and what the coming SpaceX and Anthropic listings herald. Goldman, which advised the Ford Foundation in 1956 and floated itself in 1999, is lead on both and just ran an $85bn secondary for Google. The arc: underwriting fees compressed from the double digits to roughly 2%, the syndicate collapsed to a handful of names, and the prestige mandate became a data-and-distribution business.

When the ducks are quacking: what Ford's 1956 float still teaches about the SpaceX IPO

Marc Rubinstein · Net Interest · Thu 5 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — the IPO is a relationship business wearing a transaction's clothes. Goldman's half-century of Ford work began with a two-year advisory grind, not the float; the league table follows the relationship. Two — the economics inverted. Fees fell from 15–25% on small mid-century deals to about 2% on the marquee ones — scale and prestige are loss-leaders that buy the next mandate. Three — the timing tell. “When the ducks are quacking, feed them” — capital is available, so the giants are finally coming to market.

Net Interest — “When the Ducks are Quacking”

The longitudinal thread runs to Optasia's JSE listing — sub-Saharan Africa's first fintech IPO — and forward to today's Goldman item: the same firm narrating the clogged exit is the one positioned to clear it. Rubinstein's history is a reminder that the IPO has always been less a financing event than the visible tip of a decades-long advisory relationship, and that the fee has never been where the money is.

“We are definitely in a moment where there is more greed than there is fear … if you're capital consumptive and it's available — take the capital if you know that you're going to need it.” — David Solomon, Chairman & CEO, Goldman Sachs, June 2026, quoted in Net Interest

For an allocator the practical read is that the public listing is no longer the default exit but one option among secondaries, continuation vehicles and strategic sales — and that the firms underwriting the SpaceX and Anthropic floats are the same names quietly building the secondary market that competes with them.

The deeper point for a partnership: prestige mandates are priced as relationships, not transactions. The 2% headline fee is the cover charge; the franchise is the next fifty years of being the call that gets returned when half a billion dollars of stock needs to move.

Africa & EM · Capital Structure · DFIs Structural
Launch Base Africa's latest deal sweep captures a shift the headline funding totals hide: development finance institutions are quietly retreating from equity into debt, and the cheques increasingly back the plumbing rather than the product. Among the under-the-radar deals, a cross-border clearing-and-settlement fintech operating in Ghana and Nigeria — building multi-currency netting, FX-settlement aggregation and delivery-versus-payment across 15-plus currencies into Latin America, Asia and the Middle East — drew an undisclosed debt facility from Proparco via Digital Africa's Bridge Fund, to extend its regulatory-licensing footprint across fragmented emerging markets.

Africa's development financiers retreat to debt — and back the plumbing, not the product

Staff Writer · Launch Base Africa · Wed 10 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — debt is the new DFI default. In a capital-scarce year the development financiers are choosing contracted cash flows over venture upside, and the mix is tilting hard toward loans and hybrids. Two — the settlement layer is the prize. A clearing-and-netting rail spanning 15-plus currencies is unglamorous, licence-heavy infrastructure — precisely the kind of moat patient debt likes. Three — the corridor is the strategy. The cheque buys regulatory footprint across fragmented markets, not a single-country bet.

Launch Base Africa — “African Startup Deal Tracker — Newest Deals”

A fortnight ago Norfund routed $60m through a fund manager rather than pick startups; today the same instinct goes a layer deeper — lend to the settlement infrastructure rather than own the equity at all. Read together, the two items map a clear migration in how development capital expresses an African thesis: from companies, to managers, to the plumbing beneath both.

“Development finance institutions are returning to the debt market … the financing is structured as an undisclosed facility from Proparco, via the Bridge Fund by Digital Africa.” — Launch Base Africa, Deal Tracker

For the screen the signal is where durable returns are pooling in a tight market. Equity upside is being rationed; infrastructure with licences, switching costs and contracted flows is being funded. A clearing rail that aggregates FX settlement across fragmented corridors is exactly the kind of asset that earns a toll regardless of which startup wins on top of it.

The watch-item is that DFI debt is cheaper and more patient than commercial debt, which can quietly distort the cost of capital for the infrastructure layer — subsidising the plumbing while equity for the product stays scarce. That is a structural feature of frontier markets worth pricing, not assuming away.

Markets & Capital · Private Markets · Liquidity Thinker
On Goldman Sachs' own Exchanges podcast, two of its private-markets chiefs called the exit machine “jammed” — buyout hold periods stretched to roughly seven years, distributions far below the historical 15–20% norm, the average venture-backed company now taking 14 years to go public, and retail private-credit investors recently unable to withdraw. Their verdict: a structural inflection, not a crisis. Private-credit default rates are climbing (Fitch 7.0% in April; Proskauer 2.73% in Q1) but nowhere near GFC levels, and the secondary market — $240bn in 2025, up roughly 48% — is the relief valve, which Goldman expects to double toward $500bn.

“The circulatory system is not working”: Goldman anatomises the jammed private-markets exit

Nick Lichtenberg · Fortune · Wed 10 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — the problem is the exit, not the entry. Capital went in for a decade; it is the getting-out — distributions, IPOs, sales — that has seized up. Two — illiquidity is the product, not the bug. Goldman's defence of the retail squeeze is that investors misunderstood what they bought; expect that framing to be tested. Three — innovation now lives private. Fourteen years to IPO is not a delay but a choice: the capital and the patience are available off-market, so most new value compounds before the public ever sees it.

Fortune — “The circulatory system is not working: Goldman on what's really wrong with private markets”

This is the partners' own asset class held up to the mirror, and it threads straight to today's Net Interest item: the IPO Goldman says is finally building pressure is the release valve for exactly this logjam. The candid admission — from a firm with billions riding on the answer — is that three-year rolling private-equity alpha has gone negative for the first time in decades, because public markets ripped while smoothed private marks lagged.

“The circulatory system is not working. And so that's making fundraising harder. It's driving consolidation. It's driving a lot of change in the industry.” — Michael Brandmeyer, global head & CIO, External Investing Group, Goldman Sachs Asset Management, via Fortune

Read against the African capital items, the picture completes itself: when distributions dry up in developed markets, patient debt and development finance become the marginal capital in frontier ones — the same retreat-to-the-plumbing instinct, expressed at opposite ends of the risk curve.

For Chronos the discipline the piece underwrites is to price the exit at entry. “Gummed up” is not “broken,” but it compounds into real cost the longer hold periods run, and the secondary market — a record $240bn last year — is becoming the default liquidity route rather than the fallback. The cocktail-party question Goldman keeps fielding is the one every LP is now asking.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Stop typing your first draft — talk it out, and let the AI shape it

The blank page is slowest when you try to write and think at the same time. The fix is to separate the two: hit the microphone, talk for two minutes the way you would brief a colleague in the corridor — rambling, out of order, half-formed — and let the model turn that spoken mess into structured prose. Most of us speak far faster and far more naturally than we type, and the ideas that surface when you are talking are usually the honest ones, before the internal editor has tidied them into blandness. The model is unbothered by the “ums,” the doubling-back, and the “actually, scrap that” — it hears the intent and gives you a clean draft to react to. You are no longer writing from nothing; you are editing something, which the brain finds dramatically easier.

A worked example

Before a board call, open a voice chat and say: “Right, I need to push back on the budget but not blow up the relationship — the burn is too high, runway is maybe seven months, and I want them to cut sales hires before marketing, but I also want to acknowledge the revenue beat … help me say that firmly but warmly.” Ninety seconds of talking, and you have a tight three-paragraph note you can edit in your own voice — instead of a cursor blinking on an empty screen while you decide where to start.
