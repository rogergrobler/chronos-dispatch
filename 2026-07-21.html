Nigeria · Fintech · Regulation Analysis Thesis-test
The Central Bank of Nigeria has, between March and June, issued or exposed for consultation a cluster of policy documents — on market concentration, financial holding companies, operational ring-fencing, ownership disclosure and anti-money-laundering systems — that together rewrite the growth playbook Nigerian fintechs have run for a decade. That playbook was to own every layer: issue wallets, acquire merchants, process transactions, buy a microfinance-bank licence, lend, take deposits. Two moves break it. A market-structure circular says any firm controlling more than 25% of consumer card issuing may not simultaneously control more than 15% of merchant acquiring (and vice-versa), with monthly market-share reporting and compliance by end-2026. And a ring-fencing framework requires each regulated subsidiary to hold capital and liquidity individually, "regardless of group-level resources" — stripping out the shared-infrastructure efficiencies that made licence-stacking attractive. The regulator's own line: compliance is no longer buying the right software; governance, not scale, is the new moat.

Nigeria's central bank rewrites the rules of fintech growth — and caps owning the whole stack

TechCabal · Temitayo Jaiyeola · Mon 20 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is a direct test of the thesis this page has been building all month. For weeks the durable position looked like owning more of the stack — the rail, the licence, both sides of the market. Nigeria's regulator has just drawn a hard line through exactly that, capping how much of payments any one firm may own and forcing each licence to stand on its own capital. And it reframes what maturity costs an operator: the efficiencies of running many regulated businesses as one group — shared data, shared infrastructure, cross-subsidy — are being deliberately taxed away, so future value has to come from depth in a chosen segment rather than sprawl across all of them.

The regulator just made "own every layer" illegal above a threshold — and taxed the efficiency of running them as one
TechCabal — “Nigeria's central bank is rewriting the rules for fintech growth”

Read this against the arc of the last two weeks and it is the sharpest thesis-test we have run. Issue 042 through 047 kept circling one idea: the money is migrating off the surface and into the plumbing, so the durable move is to own more of the rail — Paystack on collections, Payaza buying licences abroad, ntel becoming the landlord of the towers. Nigeria's central bank has now looked at the domestic version of that same instinct — fintechs that issue wallets, acquire merchants, process, lend and bank, all under one roof — and decided it has gone far enough. The 25%/15% cap is an explicit ceiling on owning both sides of the payments market; the ring-fencing rules make each licence carry its own capital so the group can no longer run as a single efficient balance sheet. Nigeria is not alone: India's RBI capped UPI market share after PhonePe and Google Pay came to dominate, and Europe's PSD2 forced banks to open their rails. This is the regulator saying the "own the whole stack" endgame produces systemic risk it will not permit.

“Growth will now increasingly be measured by how resilient a fintech's system becomes … governance must now be baked into how fintechs think and build.”

For a firm that underwrites African financial-services operators, this is the item that most directly forces us to mark our own thesis to market. The bull reading is that this is maturation, and it rewards exactly the businesses we should want to own: a rule that makes scale-for-its-own-sake expensive advantages the operator with real governance, clean capital and depth in a defensible segment over the one that grew by stapling licences together. Paystack restructuring under The Stack Group and Moniepoint's move into retail banking off a ₦412tn merchant base are the models being stress-tested. The bear reading is equally concrete and cuts at our recent enthusiasm: if the regulator caps how much of the stack you can own and taxes the efficiencies of integration, then "own more of the rail" has a hard ceiling in the largest African fintech market, and the unit economics of the platform plays we admire get worse, not better. The forward question we carry into any Nigerian fintech case: does the business we're looking at compound because it owns adjacent layers — the thing the CBN is now capping — or because it is genuinely the best operator in one layer, which is what the new rulebook rewards? And the honest caveat: these are circulars and exposure drafts, not yet all in force, and Nigerian regulation has a history of arriving loudly and enforcing unevenly — the direction of travel is clear, the bite is not yet proven.

South Africa · Identity · Infrastructure Analysis Structural design
South Africa is preparing the first overhaul of its SIM-registration regime since RICA introduced mandatory registration nearly two decades ago. The reform — developed jointly by the mobile operators (via industry body ACT) and the Department of Justice — would shift SIM registration from "present an ID document at purchase" to real-time verification of the buyer against the Department of Home Affairs database, making a mobile number as trustworthy an identity credential as the checks banks already run. The stakes are explicitly financial, not just telecoms: a mobile number now secures access to bank accounts, payments and messaging, so a fraudulently registered SIM is a fraud vector into the whole financial system. The push follows South Africa's FATF grey-listing and a run of SIM-fraud arrests; penalties already reach R5m ($303,000) or ten years' jail, with coordinated enforcement flagged to begin from July 2026 under the country's developing digital-identity framework.

South Africa wants every SIM card to become a state-verified digital ID

TechCabal · Phathisani Moyo · Mon 20 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the identity layer being rebuilt beneath every bank and fintech in the country — the unglamorous credential that quietly underwrites onboarding, payments and fraud control, now to be wired directly to Home Affairs. And it is a clean instance of a design choice we keep meeting: rather than let each operator or bank verify identity its own way, South Africa is centralising the check into one shared, state-owned rail that everyone plugs into. Whoever runs that rail — and how well it works — shapes the fraud losses and onboarding costs of every financial business built on top of it.

TechCabal — “South Africa wants every SIM card to become a trusted digital ID”

Set this beside yesterday's Ghana 5G item and the pair frames a recurring infrastructure-design question. Ghana concluded that a single appointed rail under-builds and handed the 5G build to competing owners; South Africa is reaching the opposite conclusion about identity, deciding that verification is too important and too fraud-exposed to leave to each operator's own patchwork, and routing it into one shared check against the Home Affairs database. The logic is that identity is a natural shared-utility layer — its value is precisely that everyone trusts the same source of truth — whereas network capacity benefits from competitive duplication. Same designers, same continent, opposite answers, and both defensible on their own terms. The tell here is that the reform was driven by the operators themselves, who concluded RICA "no longer reflects how digital crime has evolved" after fraud accelerated from around 2019.

“The risks posed by SIM-enabled fraud now extend beyond individual consumers to the financial system and national security.”

For a firm holding South African financial-services and fintech exposure, this is infrastructure that touches the cost base of everything we own. The bull case is straightforward: a trustworthy, real-time identity rail lowers fraud losses, cuts onboarding friction, and de-risks the digital-banking and lending businesses that depend on knowing who the customer is — and it moves the country toward the FATF compliance it needs to clear its grey-listing, which is itself a tailwind for every regulated operator. The scepticism is about execution and dependency: the system only helps if the Home Affairs database is accurate and available, a bet South Africa's identity infrastructure does not always reward; centralising verification concentrates both a single point of failure and a surveillance capability; and consumers have been told the details will come later, which means the operational reality is unproven. The structural point worth holding for any financial thesis in the market: the identity layer is quietly becoming a piece of shared national infrastructure that our portfolio businesses will build on but not control — its reliability becomes their reliability, and its weaknesses become their fraud line. The caveat: enforcement is flagged for July 2026 but the verification mechanics are undisclosed, so this is a direction and a framework agreement, not yet a working system.

East Africa · Regulation · Mobility Briefing Hyper-local
South Sudan has imposed new e-visa fees on its neighbours: $50 for citizens of fellow East African Community members Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda and the DRC, and $100 for Somalis, Burundians and Nigerians — while Tanzanians enter free and South Africans get 30 days visa-free. Published on the country's e-visa portal on Monday, the fees implement an immigration policy from last week and cut directly against the EAC's two-decade push, anchored in its Common Market Protocol, to allow the free movement of people, labour and capital across the eight-member bloc. The practical bite falls on Kenya and Uganda, South Sudan's main links to regional markets: their transport firms, traders, engineers, teachers, healthcare workers and bank staff cross the border constantly. Kenyan lenders KCB Group, Stanbic and Equity Group all run South Sudanese subsidiaries whose technology, risk, audit and compliance staff shuttle between Nairobi and Juba.

South Sudan re-erects the toll: a $50 visa on its own EAC neighbours

TechCabal · Adonijah Ndege · Mon 20 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the exact inverse of the cross-border-rail story we keep surfacing. Where Payaza buys licences to erase the friction of moving money across borders, South Sudan reintroduces friction on moving people across them — a state choosing revenue and control over integration. And it is a concrete reminder that African regional integration is not a one-way ratchet: the AfCFTA-and-common-market thesis that underwrites a lot of pan-African expansion cases has to be underwritten member-state by member-state, because any government can put a tollgate back up on a Monday.

A member of the free-movement bloc quietly charges the bloc — integration is reversible, one government at a time
TechCabal — “South Sudan's $50 e-visa fee threatens East African labour mobility”

The reason this earns a slot on a page mostly about rails and capital is that it is the counter-example to a thesis we lean on. A great deal of pan-African expansion logic — the case for building payment rails, banking networks and logistics across borders — rests on the assumption that integration deepens over time: PAPSS spreading, AfCFTA lowering barriers, the EAC common market opening labour flows. South Sudan just demonstrated the assumption's fragility. A member that joined the EAC in 2016, bound in principle to visa-free movement, has unilaterally put a price on entry for its closest neighbours, and treats them worse than non-members Tanzania and South Africa. For the Kenyan and Ugandan firms — banks, transporters, professional-services providers — whose operating models assume frictionless staff movement to Juba, the $50 is trivial per trip but real in aggregate and, more importantly, a signal that the direction of integration cannot be taken as given.

“The different treatment of EAC citizens is likely to raise questions over reciprocity and Juba's compliance with those commitments.”

The read for our own thinking is a discipline, not a deal. Any thesis that prices in regional integration — a bank expanding across the EAC, a fintech assuming AfCFTA settlement, a logistics business built on open corridors — has to hold the integration premium lightly and stress-test it member-state by member-state, because the political economy of a frontier market can override a treaty on short notice. KCB, Stanbic and Equity will absorb this fee without a dent to their balance sheets, but the more useful observation is directional: even inside a bloc explicitly committed to free movement, a cash-strapped government reached for a border toll as a revenue-and-control lever. The bull counterpoint, worth keeping honest, is that one member defecting does not unwind a bloc — the EAC and AfCFTA trend still points toward integration, and reciprocity pressure may reverse this — but the base case for any cross-border case should assume friction can return, not that it only ever falls.

Kenya · Banking · Governance Briefing Longitudinal
Guaranty Trust Bank Kenya is looking for a new managing director after its Nigerian parent, GTCO, recalled Jubril Adeniji at the end of his tenure and returned him to Nigeria. Adeniji — appointed in 2023 after running the group's Tanzanian subsidiary — will stay 90 days while the bank seeks regulatory approval for a successor, during a period in which GTCO is trying to expand its Kenyan presence. The detail that makes it a Chronos item rather than an HR notice: it is the simplest, cheapest expression of cross-border control. A parent that wants to steer a subsidiary does not need to restructure it or buy more of it; it moves its own people. It also lands amid a wave of Kenyan banking-leadership churn — days earlier, former Safaricom executive Michael Mutiga was named Stanbic Bank Kenya CEO — as regional parents reshuffle who runs their East African operations.

GTBank Kenya loses its MD to head office — control, exercised through people

TechCabal · Adonijah Ndege · Mon 20 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this threads yesterday's Vodacom item forward and downward. Where Vodacom converted 55% of Safaricom into the right to name the CEO, GTCO shows the same principle at the operating level — the parent already holds control and simply recalls the person running the subsidiary, no shareholder vote required. And it is a live read on how Nigerian and South African banking groups actually run their East African expansions: through the deployment and recall of a small cadre of trusted executives, which makes the identity and mandate of a subsidiary MD a more informative signal than most balance-sheet lines.

TechCabal — “GTBank Kenya begins search for new MD as Jubril Adeniji returns to Nigeria”

Yesterday's Safaricom item was about control at its most expensive — a R35bn move to majority ownership, then a shareholder resolution to convert that ownership into the right to appoint the chief executive. This GTBank story is the same theme at its cheapest and most routine. GTCO does not need a resolution or a transaction; it owns the Kenyan subsidiary outright, so exercising control is a matter of recalling one executive to Lagos and installing another. The two items bracket the same truth from opposite ends of the cost curve: the ultimate lever of ownership is the power to choose who runs the thing, whether you have to pay $2.1bn for it or simply issue an internal transfer. Set alongside the Stanbic Kenya CEO change days earlier, it also reads as a season of deliberate leadership reshuffling across East African banking as regional parents position their operations.

“GTCO recalled Jubril Adeniji at the end of his tenure … he will remain in office for the next 90 days while the bank seeks regulatory approval for a successor.”

Why it matters to us beyond the personnel note: when we hold or assess a subsidiary of a larger banking or financial group, the parent's willingness and ability to move senior people is a real and often underweighted variable. It is a source of value — a disciplined parent can parachute in proven operators and lift a subsidiary's execution — and a source of risk, because a recall can interrupt momentum, and a subsidiary's strategy can turn on a head-office decision the local market never sees coming. The forward question we carry into any group-subsidiary case: how much of this business's trajectory depends on one or two executives the parent can redeploy at will, and do we understand the parent's bench and intent well enough to price that? The regulatory wrinkle worth noting: the 90-day handover exists because the Central Bank of Kenya must approve the successor, a reminder that even wholly-owned control over leadership is mediated by the local regulator — ownership proposes, the regulator confirms.

Pan-Africa · Capital · Venture Analysis Wouldn't-have-seen-otherwise
Launch Africa Ventures — a pan-African VC with 180-plus portfolio companies across 25 countries — has done the opposite of the market in 2026, completing 15 new investments plus nine follow-ons while the rest of the industry retreats from the earliest cheques. The context is a barbell: Africa raised $711m in Q1, up ~27% year-on-year, but the $100k–$500k deals that create companies fell about 30% (140 to 92), the earliest cheques dropped harder (73 to 32), and debt overtook equity for the first time. Launch Africa's head of investments, Uwem Uwemakpan, lays out a disciplined framework worth reading in full: every deal must pass "Exit Realism" — three-to-five nameable acquirers across banks, telcos, global platforms, industrials and DFIs, not just Series-A VCs — because African exits now cluster in the $50–150m trade-sale range. In June the firm returned $2.5m to first-fund LPs after 11 exits; Fund II shifts from Fund I's high-volume spray to 5–15% ownership stakes with reserved follow-on capital.

Launch Africa writes the cheque the market abandoned — a masterclass in allocation discipline

TechCabal · Muktar Oladunmade · Mon 20 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the same allocation discipline that ran through Monday's Prosus and Friday's Absa items, pointed the third way — not selling out or buying more of a compounder, but deploying into the gap everyone else is fleeing, on the reasoning that if nobody writes the first cheque in 2026 there is no Series A class in 2029. And the interview is unusually candid tradecraft for the partners: how a fund underwrites exits it cannot rely on the market to provide, why concentration changes diligence, and why capital scarcity rather than company scarcity is now setting entry prices — the kind of allocator thinking that travels well beyond African venture.

TechCabal — “Why Launch Africa keeps writing early-stage cheques despite market slowdown”

We have run allocation-discipline items three days running now, and this completes the set. Friday's Absa was "buy more of the compounder you understand." Monday's Prosus was "sell the last of the position that no longer earns its place, into strength." Launch Africa is the third face of the same coin: "deploy into the layer everyone is abandoning, because that is where the return is being priced." All three are the same underlying rule — put capital where the marginal return is highest and the crowd is thinnest — and it is instructive to see it expressed by a seed manager rather than a listed holding company. The reasoning is genuinely contrarian by design: the firm read a Q1 in which early cheques nearly disappeared not as a signal to wait but as the moment to own the pipeline others will need in three years, and it is willing to hold that view because it does not depend on the growth-capital market recovering on its schedule.

“If nobody writes that cheque in 2026, there's no Series A class in 2029. We'd rather own that pipeline than inherit someone else's gap in three years.”

The part most worth the partners' time is the exit machinery, because it is exactly the problem any African-focused investor has to solve and rarely articulates this cleanly. Launch Africa refuses to underwrite Fund II on the hope that a well-capitalised Series A class exists when it needs liquidity; instead every cheque must pass "Exit Realism" — three to five named acquirers spanning banks, telcos, global platforms, industrials and DFIs, on the observation that African exits are clustering in the $50–150m trade-sale range rather than the VC-to-VC relay. It has formalised secondaries as a standing programme, originates acquirer relationships years before a company is exit-ready, and targets partial exits that generate liquidity along the way rather than waiting for one buyer to take a whole position. The move from Fund I's 100-plus scattershot investments (follow-on rights passed to LPs) to Fund II's concentrated 5–15% stakes forces Series-A-grade diligence at seed — separate co-founder interviews, unit economics that must work before the cheque, not after. For a firm like ours the read is less "back this fund" than "borrow this framework": the tension it names — concentration makes a stake easier to sell but shrinks the pool of buyers who can take it — is the same tension in any concentrated private book, and the answer it reaches (widen who counts as a buyer, control exit origination yourself, hold the integration premium lightly) is portable to how we think about our own positions. The honest caveat: this is a manager describing its own strategy in its own favour, and a contrarian first cheque only looks like discipline if the Series A layer does eventually thicken — underwriting to trade sales is prudent, but it is also an admission that the base case is a thinner market for years.

Nigeria · Capital Markets · Behaviour Analysis Wouldn't-have-seen-otherwise
A generation of young Nigerians — many too young to remember the 2008 crash that wiped an estimated ₦6.96 trillion off the market and scarred their parents — is turning stock investing into a monthly habit through apps like Bamboo, Cowrywise, Trove and Risevest. The data behind the anecdote is striking: domestic retail investors traded ₦2.86tn ($2.07bn) of equities between January and May 2026, up 138.76% year-on-year, and now account for 36.22% of all activity on the Nigerian Exchange. The backdrop is one of the world's strongest rallies — Nigerian equities have returned about 67% in dollar terms this year, overtaking South Korea as the best performer among 92 exchanges Bloomberg tracks. Bamboo says it briefly became the exchange's largest broker by weighted market share in April, executing 542,582 trades, most of its base aged 18–34. Yet the NGX is still just $113.8bn in market cap against the JSE's ~$1.46tn — a striking headroom gap for a country of 200m-plus.

Their parents were burned in 2008. Nigeria's young are buying stocks anyway.

TechCabal · Temitayo Jaiyeola · Sat 18 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is control of capital at its most granular — the same ownership theme running through the week, but exercised by individuals deciding, every month, to put money to work rather than leave it in a savings account. And it is a wouldn't-have-seen-otherwise structural signal: a mobile-app layer plus accessible financial education is building a domestic retail investor base out of almost nothing in 18 months, deepening Nigeria's capital markets in a way that, if it holds, changes the liquidity and listing calculus for every African business weighing a local listing.

A domestic retail base built in 18 months — and a market still a fraction of the JSE for eight times the people
TechCabal — “Their parents lost fortunes. They're buying Nigerian stocks anyway.”

This closes the week's ownership thread at the level of the individual. The whole arc — Payaza owning rails, Vodacom naming a CEO, GTCO recalling an MD, Launch Africa placing early cheques — has been about who controls the allocation of capital and infrastructure. Here the allocators are millions of young Nigerians deciding, at month-end, to move ₦20,000 into equities through an app, and doing it despite — or in deliberate defiance of — the 2008 crash that shaped their parents' fear of the market. The structural mechanics are the familiar African fintech story applied to capital markets: a smartphone layer (Bamboo, Cowrywise, Trove, Risevest, plus the NGX's own Invest platform) collapses the cost and friction of buying shares, while podcasts, newsletters and social feeds have demystified the knowledge that used to sit in brokers' offices. The result is a domestic retail base — 36.22% of activity, +138.7% in volume year-on-year — that essentially did not exist 18 months ago.

“The retail growth of the market has happened in the last 18 months … to expect that the volume from the retail boom will suddenly outpace institutional investors is not practical.”

Why it matters to a firm like ours, beyond the human-interest warmth of the piece: a deepening domestic retail base changes the exit and listing calculus for African businesses, which connects directly back to the Launch Africa item above. Part of the reason African exits cluster in trade sales rather than IPOs is thin, illiquid public markets; a genuine, sticky retail bid — if it survives its first real drawdown — is exactly what over time could make a local listing a credible exit rather than a theoretical one. The bull case is a virtuous cycle the sources name: better corporate governance and tighter NGX regulation rebuild trust, a 67% dollar rally creates visible winners, and the $113.8bn-versus-$1.46tn gap against the JSE is the headroom. The scepticism is the ghost the headline invokes: this is a first-generation retail base that has mostly known a rally, several of the investors quoted describe buying "the popular names on the app" with little analysis, and 2008 is a reminder that a leverage-fuelled retail boom can reverse violently — the test is not the inflows now but the behaviour in the first sustained fall. The measured read: a real structural deepening of a shallow market, worth tracking as a live variable in any Nigerian listing or exit thesis, with the honest caveat that its durability is genuinely unproven until the cycle turns.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Set your standing preferences once — Custom Instructions

Yesterday's tip was to give a recurring account its own Project. Today's is one level broader: set your defaults for every chat, once. Most models let you save Custom Instructions — a short standing brief that silently prepends to everything you ask. The point is to stop re-typing the same corrections. If you find yourself constantly saying "use UK English," "lead with the conclusion, then the data, then the action," "no corporate throat-clearing," or "assume I'm a private-equity principal, not a novice," that belongs in Custom Instructions, not in every prompt. Write it once and every answer arrives already in your register — you spend your attention on the substance, not on re-training the tone.

A worked example

A two-part brief works best. Tell it who you are: "I'm a managing partner at a Singapore-based PE firm investing across African financial services; I think in theses, moats and exit paths." Then tell it how you want answers: "UK English. Lead with the single most important conclusion in one line. Support with specific numbers, not adjectives. End with the decision or open question. No preamble, no 'great question', no bullet-point sprawl unless I ask." From then on, a cold question like "what should I make of Nigeria capping payments market share?" comes back sharp and in your voice — no setup required.
