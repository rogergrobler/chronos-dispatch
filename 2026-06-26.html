Nigeria · Markets · Fintech Chart
Retail participation in the Nigerian stock market has grown 138.76% year-on-year. Domestic retail investors traded ₦2.86 trillion ($2.07bn) of equities between January and May 2026 — an average of ₦18.94bn ($13.72m) a day across 151 trading days — and now account for 36.22% of all activity on the Nigerian Exchange (NGX). The surge is powered by mobile-first apps — Bamboo, Trove, Risevest, Cowrywise — that let users buy shares from their phones in amounts traditional brokers could never serve profitably. In April, Bamboo overtook the institutional firm CardinalStone to become the exchange’s largest broker by weighted market share (542,582 trades, 11.13% share), despite an average trade size of just ₦71,609 ($51.89) against CardinalStone’s ₦2.57m. 73% of Bamboo’s users are aged 18–34.

Nigeria’s retail investors traded $2.07bn in five months — the investing relationship has quietly moved onto the phone

TechCabal · Markets · Temitayo Jaiyeola · Thu 25 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — distribution beat the incumbents. A four-year-old app with a $52 average ticket is now the NGX’s number-one broker; owning the customer relationship, not the balance sheet, decided the league table. Two — the institutions still own the volume. Domestic institutional investors were 51.45% of transactions, and a February PenCom rule lifting pension equity limits drove a ₦6.79tn ($4.92bn) institutional buying week — the retail wave rides on top of an institutional tide. Three — the market is still shallow. The NGX is worth $109.3bn serving 200m+ people, against Johannesburg’s $1.46tn for 60m; mobile has brought the people in, but depth now depends on more listings and products, not more apps.

The flow is retail · the relationship migrates from the broker’s desk to the customer’s phone
TechCabal — “Driven by mobile apps, Nigerian retail investors traded $2.07 billion in five months”

Thursday’s edition was about who comes to own a rail. Today opens one layer up: once the rail exists, who owns the customer who runs on it. The NGX answer is striking — the broker that won is the one that owned the phone. Bamboo did not out-trade CardinalStone on value (its average ticket is 1/36th the size); it out-distributed it, turning hundreds of thousands of small, young investors into the largest single source of weighted activity on the exchange. The relationship, not the cheque size, set the ranking.

“The relationship migrates to whoever owns the customer’s phone — the broker that won the league table did it on a $52 average ticket.”

Mobile investing has brought more Nigerians into the market than ever, but whether it can build a deeper, more liquid exchange now depends on more listings and new products rather than more apps.

For Chronos there are two reads worth holding. First, this is a clean demonstration of the thesis that runs through our African screens: in shallow-penetration markets the durable advantage is distribution and the customer relationship, and incumbents that own balance sheets can still be out-positioned by challengers that own attention. It echoes Thursday’s SA banks-as-telcos story from the other direction — there the incumbent bundles to keep the customer; here the insurgent unbundles access to win them. Second, the caution is structural and easy to miss in the headline: this is still a $109bn market whose retail flow rides on an institutional tide (the PenCom rule change, banking recapitalisation), and a young, app-driven retail base can reverse as fast as it arrived. The investable question for any NGX-listed position is whether the new liquidity is sticky or sentiment, and whether the exchange deepens enough — derivatives, listings, products — to hold the investors mobile has delivered. We will watch the listing pipeline as the real tell.

Nigeria · Fintech · Remittances Tests a thesis
Monzo, the British neobank with more than 15 million users, has entered the Nigerian remittance market: customers can now send naira directly from the Monzo app, powered by a long-standing partnership with Wise, which received an approval-in-principle from the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) as an International Money Transfer Operator (IMTO) in March. The first transfer is free until 22 August; after that, standard Wise fees apply. The UK–Nigeria corridor is one of the world’s busiest — Nigeria received over $20.9bn in remittances in 2024, with the UK consistently a top-three source. The move arrives in a crowded lane: LemFi took Tether backing to add stablecoin settlement, Kenya’s WapiPay just secured a Canadian payments licence, and Revolut is building toward a South Africa launch by 2028.

Monzo embeds a licensed rail to reach Nigeria — the remittance relationship moves inside the neobank

TechCabal Daily · Fintech · Opeyemi Kareem · Thu 25 Jun 2026

A build-versus-embed decision read in plain sight. Monzo did not build a remittance product or chase a licence; it embedded Wise’s regulated infrastructure into an app millions of Nigerians in the UK already use as their primary bank account. The product logic is bluntly stated: the best remittance experience is the one that requires the fewest new steps. This is not a price play — after the promo, Monzo charges standard Wise fees — it is a stickiness play, keeping users inside one app rather than losing them to a specialist the moment they want to send money home. The open question is regulatory: until Wise moves from approval-in-principle to full authorisation, the product has a ceiling.

The rail is becoming a commodity · the prize is the customer who never has to leave the app
TechCabal — “Monzo just made it easier to send money to Nigeria without switching apps”

This tests a thesis we keep returning to: in money movement, the durable advantage is owning the regulated pipe, but the durable relationship is owning the app the customer already opens. Monzo has neither built the rail nor bought the licence — it rents Wise’s — and is betting that distribution plus convenience beats the specialists on the only axis that compounds: never giving the customer a reason to leave. It is the mirror image of Thursday’s Yellow Card and Daya items, where the value accrued to whoever owned the compliant rail. Here the rail is a commodity input and the value sits with whoever owns the daily relationship.

“The best remittance experience is the one that requires the fewest new steps — so the licence becomes someone else’s feature.”

Monzo isn’t building a remittance product from scratch; it’s embedding Wise’s licensed infrastructure into an app that millions of Nigerians in the UK already use as their primary bank account.

For Chronos the relevance is to any payments or fintech position whose moat is supposed to be the rail itself. The Monzo move is a warning that a licensed corridor can be commoditised the moment a large distributor with an existing customer base decides to embed it — the specialist’s licence becomes someone else’s feature. The corridor is now genuinely crowded: Monzo, LemFi, WapiPay and Revolut are all chasing the same diaspora flows, which compresses fees and shifts competition to who owns the customer. The diligence question for any remittance or cross-border holding becomes whether it owns a defensible customer relationship or merely a licence that a neobank can rent around it. The thread to hold forward is the same M&A logic as the rest of the week: in a crowding corridor, the smaller licensed rails become acquisition targets for the distributors that own the users.

Nigeria · Fintech · AI For the portfolio
Paystack, the payments company owned by The Stack Group (and ultimately Stripe), has launched Paystack Index — an experimental product that lets users in Nigeria check out with supported merchants using AI agents. Built with TSG Labs, it works today with Claude, ChatGPT and OpenClaw, with early access opening first to Zap users in Nigeria for everyday tasks: airtime and data across the major networks, wallet funding and transfers via Zap, and food ordering through Chowdeck. Index acts as the connection layer between a user’s AI agent, Zap, supported merchants and Paystack’s payment rails — interpreting the request, checking permissions, routing to a merchant and settling — without storing card numbers, CVVs or PINs. The launch lands as 88% of surveyed Nigerians report using generative AI in the past year and 62% for everyday tasks (Google-Ipsos).

Paystack puts AI agents at the checkout — the payment relationship moves to the moment a model decides

TechCabal · Fintech · Opeyemi Kareem · Thu 25 Jun 2026

The strategic move under an experimental label. On Thursday’s edition Paystack was extending its rail past payments into the small-business stack; today it extends in the other direction — toward the consumer’s point of decision. The bet, in CEO Shola Akinlade’s words, is that “as AI agents become a more common way for people to search, decide, and take action… checkout has to evolve too,” and that AI agents could become “another important interface for commerce.” The controlled beta is explicitly a learning exercise: which tasks users repeat, and whether AI becomes a genuine discovery channel for merchants. Notably, the same TSG Labs is also exploring stablecoin infrastructure, including in South Africa.

TechCabal — “Paystack launches AI-powered checkout for Nigerian consumers”

This is the item that most directly answers Thursday’s frontier-AI warning, where a landmark study showed models can out-persuade expert humans. The obvious next question was: persuade them to do what? Here is one concrete answer — complete the purchase. Paystack is wiring the agent that decides directly to the rail that pays, and doing it as the incumbent processor rather than a startup. If discovery and decision move to AI assistants, the company that owns the checkout infrastructure beneath them is positioned to tax that shift without owning the model — the same picks-and-shovels logic that built the African fintech rails in the first place.

“If checkout migrates into AI agents, the businesses that win are the ones the agents can transact with natively.”

We believe AI agents could become another important interface for commerce, where people move from asking questions to completing real tasks.

For Chronos this is exactly the kind of AI-deployment-for-the-portfolio signal we up-weight: not a press release about AI ambition but a working product that puts a frontier capability against revenue, with sensible guardrails (no stored card data, transparent merchant routing, user approval of every payment). The read-through for any consumer-facing holding is sharp — if checkout migrates into AI agents, the businesses that win are the ones the agents can transact with natively, and the ones that lose are those that depend on the customer opening their app and tapping through a funnel. The honest caution is that this is a curated beta in a single market and habit formation is unproven; agentic commerce could stall on trust, fraud or merchant economics. The thread to hold forward is whether Index becomes a real discovery channel — if AI agents start steering meaningful demand to merchants, that reprices the value of owning the checkout, and it is worth watching which of our portfolio and pipeline businesses are built to be transacted with by a model rather than tapped through by a human.

Africa · AI · SME software AI deployment
Knowlix AI, a Munich-based startup, has launched an AI-powered business platform for small businesses across 29 African countries and other global markets, handling customer relations, invoicing, inventory, projects and other back-office processes. At its core is an “AI Teammate” that drafts quotations and invoices, converts meeting notes into tasks, prepares inventory reorders and automates routine processes within owner-approved guardrails, learning each business’s workflow and tone over time. The company has no African customers yet but launched with built-in support for local tax configurations, currencies, accounting standards and legal requirements across markets including Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya, Egypt, Rwanda and Senegal. It is global from day one, with a free trial and paid plans from $24.90/month, and built on an open-source foundation. The GSMA estimates AI could add $2.9tn to Africa’s economy by 2030.

A Munich startup ships an “AI Teammate” to run the African small-business back office

TechCabal · Startups · Opeyemi Kareem · Thu 25 Jun 2026

The same agentic shift as the Paystack item, aimed at the back office rather than the checkout. The pitch — “Knowlix AI sets itself up and runs the back office, so a small business can operate like a Fortune 500” — targets the historic gap in SME software: enterprise tooling was always too expensive to set up, not too expensive to want. Knowlix is betting that AI which configures and operates itself collapses that setup cost to near zero. It enters a crowded field — Odoo, Zoho, HubSpot and Microsoft globally; Moniebook, Orda Africa and Trembi locally — wagering that embedding AI into the operations themselves, rather than selling standalone tools, is the wedge.

TechCabal — “Munich-based startup brings AI-powered business tools to African small businesses”

Read alongside Paystack’s checkout agent, this sketches the two ends of the same thesis: AI is moving from answering questions to executing operations, at the consumer’s point of purchase and at the merchant’s back office simultaneously. What is notable here is the entry strategy — a European company launching into 29 African markets at once, pre-localised for tax, currency and accounting, with no customers yet. That is only rational if you believe the AI layer, not local feet on the ground, is now the thing that wins SME software — a direct test of whether African SME tooling is won by distribution and trust (the local incumbents’ edge) or by capability shipped from anywhere.

“The bet is that a self-configuring AI layer — not local feet on the ground — is now what wins SME software.”

For decades, the software that runs a company was built for the largest companies on earth — not because small owners didn’t need it, but because they could never afford to set it up.

For Chronos the interesting tension is competitive, not just thematic. Several portfolio-adjacent and pipeline businesses sell exactly the tools Knowlix bundles — bookkeeping, invoicing, inventory — to African SMEs, and the implicit claim here is that a self-configuring AI layer can leapfrog the localisation and onboarding moats those businesses have spent years building. The honest counter is that SME software in Africa has always been won on distribution, trust and on-the-ground support, none of which a Munich launch with zero customers has yet earned, and a $24.90 plan still has to clear real willingness-to-pay among micro-enterprises. The thread to hold forward is whether capability-shipped-from-anywhere can beat distribution-built-locally in SME software — if it can, it reprices a lot of African B2B software moats; if it cannot, it confirms that the operating-system-for-SMEs prize still belongs to whoever owns the merchant relationship, which is the same conclusion the Paystack and banks-as-telcos stories keep pointing to.

Kenya · Digital Lending Tests a thesis
Tala, the venture-backed digital lender operating in Kenya, Mexico, the Philippines and India, is cutting jobs across its global workforce, affecting fewer than 10% of its Kenya-based staff as it “streamlines our functions and centralises operations to align with our strategic roadmap.” In Kenya, seven of 85 employees have been notified of intended redundancy, with a consultation process under way; the cuts follow about 28 redundancies (~3%) in April 2025. Tala says centralising will support its “global objective of embedding our services into partner ecosystems at scale.” Founded in 2011, it offers unsecured digital loans through its app; in 2025 CEO Shivani Siroya said it had served more than 10 million customers, originated over $6bn in loans and reached a $300m annualised revenue run-rate. It has raised more than $522m across 13 rounds, including a March 2025 debt round.

Tala trims staff and centralises — the standalone lender retools to embed inside other people’s ecosystems

TechCabal · Startups · Kenn Abuya · Thu 25 Jun 2026

A small redundancy with an outsized strategic tell. The numbers are modest — seven roles in Kenya — but the stated direction is the story: a lender that built a direct-to-consumer app across four countries is reorganising to embed its services into partner ecosystems rather than acquire and serve every borrower itself. After a decade of proving the standalone digital-lending model (10m+ customers, $6bn originated, a $300m run-rate), Tala is signalling that the next phase is distribution through others’ rails, not more of its own. The phrase to underline is “embedding our services into partner ecosystems at scale” — the same embedded-finance gravity pulling at every operator this week.

TechCabal — “Digital lender Tala cuts jobs globally, affecting fewer than 10% in Kenya”

This tests one of our standing theses on African credit: that the durable position is owning the data and the distribution, and that standalone consumer lenders eventually migrate toward becoming embedded infrastructure inside larger ecosystems. Tala’s reorganisation is a quiet vote for that future from one of the model’s pioneers — the same direction implied by Issue 029’s Nigerian credit-gap data, by M-KOPA lending against its own device base, and by Paystack moving from payments toward lending against payment data. The standalone app got Tala to scale; embedding is how it now intends to defend the economics.

“The standalone app got Tala to scale; embedding inside other ecosystems is how it now intends to defend the economics.”

Tala remains fully committed to its Kenyan market and customers… centralising functions would support our global objective of embedding our services into partner ecosystems at scale.

For Chronos the read is twofold and deliberately unsentimental. First, the strategic signal is more important than the headcount: a mature, well-funded lender concluding that the future is embedded-rather-than-direct is a useful data point for how we value any standalone digital-lending position — the question becomes whether it owns a distribution relationship others will pay to plug into, or whether it is a balance sheet competing for the same borrowers everyone else can now reach. Second, the operational caution is real: “centralising operations” and repeated small layoffs can signal disciplined focus or quiet margin pressure in a sector where default cycles and funding costs bite hard, and the company gave little detail on what actually changes. The thread to hold forward is whether the embedded-finance pivot shows up in the numbers — lower customer-acquisition cost, partner-driven origination — or whether it is restructuring dressed as strategy. For any credit position, the test is the same one we set on Thursday: name who would pay to embed you, and why.

Kenya · M&A · Governance Wouldn't have seen otherwise
On 28 April 2021, TechCrunch reported that Kenya’s Ajua had acquired the AI startup WayaWaya “to consolidate consumer experience play in African SMEs.” Five years on, WayaWaya’s founder Teddy Ogallo says the acquisition never happened — and he has spent those years trying to undo a deal he insists was never agreed. By his account, what existed was a consultancy arrangement: in March 2021 he signed on as Ajua’s VP for Product APIs and Integrations and transferred a piece of software he personally owned (an integration platform called Janja), with no shareholder negotiations, board approvals, valuation or transfer of WayaWaya’s ownership. Internal briefing documents, however, told executives to “sell the acquisition side.” When the Competition Authority of Kenya sought transaction records in September 2021, Ajua’s counsel argued both firms were Delaware-incorporated and outside Kenya’s merger laws. Ajua’s founder now says “the transaction was cancelled back in 2023, and the asset divested.”

Ajua said it acquired WayaWaya. Five years on, the founder says it never happened — and the record won’t correct

TechCabal · Exclusive · Adonijah Ndege · Thu 25 Jun 2026

The cautionary counterpoint to a narrative we have been tracking all week. Thursday’s Beltone–Baobab item celebrated African M&A maturing into a real asset class with credible buyers writing nine-figure cheques. This is the shadow side: an “acquisition” announced for the prestige of consolidation — timed to a 2021 venture boom when an exit signalled ecosystem maturity — that the target says was a consultancy dressed up as a deal. The reporting is careful and ambiguous on purpose: consultancy agreements and a software transfer were real; shareholder approvals and a sale were, per Ogallo and the records seen, not. The damage is reputational and operational — years of clients and a regulator asking a founder to prove he still owns his own company.

TechCabal — “Ajua said it acquired Kenya’s WayaWaya. WayaWaya says it never happened”

This is the item partners are least likely to have seen and the one most worth their attention, because it speaks directly to diligence rather than to a sector. The week’s spine has been the maturing of African digital infrastructure into something investable; this is a reminder that the record of that maturity — the press releases, the startup databases, the search results that we and everyone else lean on to reconstruct deal history — can be wrong and can stay wrong for years even when the protagonist actively contests it. A regulator inquiry, repeated corrections and a founder’s folder of documents were not enough to dislodge a single TechCrunch headline.

“A regulator inquiry, repeated corrections and a founder’s folder of documents were not enough to dislodge a single headline.”

The least we deserve is confirmation that we are operating as our own company with the original founders and investors.

For Chronos the lesson is concrete and process-level. First, never take a reported acquisition — especially a pre-2023 African one announced into a hype cycle — as fact without primary evidence: the transaction agreement, proof of payment, board resolutions and regulatory filings. The CAK asked Ajua for exactly those documents and, on the record seen, did not get a substantive answer; we should hold ourselves to the same evidentiary bar before relying on any deal in a target’s history. Second, the Delaware-incorporation argument used to sidestep the Kenyan regulator is a useful flag for how cross-border structuring can put African operating realities beyond the reach of African oversight — relevant to how we think about governance and recourse in any continental deal. The thread to hold forward is simply this: as African M&A volume rises, so does the value of independently verifying it, and “it was in the press” is not diligence. Worth reading in full as a governance case study.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Make the model draw the 2×2 — force a messy decision onto two axes before you argue about it

Today’s edition is a parade of strategic choices: build the rail or embed someone else’s, stay standalone or fold into a partner’s ecosystem, chase retail flow or institutional. When a decision has too many moving parts to hold in your head, the fastest route to clarity is to force it onto two axes. Hand the model the situation and ask it to propose the two variables that actually separate the options, plot them on a 2×2, and name what lives in each quadrant. You are not outsourcing the judgement; you are making the trade-off visible so you and the partners argue about the right thing rather than talking past each other.

A worked example

Paste the decision and its context, then prompt: “Propose the two axes that best separate these options. Draw the 2×2, place each option in a quadrant, and tell me which quadrant is most crowded, which is empty, and what an option in the empty quadrant would have to look like. Then name the single axis I am most likely underweighting, and why.” Read what comes back and you will usually find the real disagreement was about which axis matters — not about the options at all. Use it to open any debate where everyone is certain but nobody agrees.
