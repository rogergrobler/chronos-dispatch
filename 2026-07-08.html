Africa · Private Equity · Capital Flows Analysis Longitudinal
Sango Capital, the Johannesburg private equity firm founded by ex-Bridgewater partner Richard Okello, has been appointed to manage roughly $458–460m of committed capital across three African fund portfolios previously run by New York-based Kuramo Capital — a transfer initiated by a consortium of institutional LPs. Two of the funds had passed their fund life; the third is a 2019 vintage, well inside a normal 10–12-year cycle. The likely driver sits in Washington: from 1 July 2026, the levy on US university endowment income rose from a flat 1.4% to a tiered structure reaching 8%. Harvard has sold $750m of taxable bonds after a $450m offering the month before; Princeton weighed $320m, Stanford sold $327m. Adams Street argues endowments may cut private equity structurally, not cyclically. Kuramo, meanwhile, is pivoting to African sovereign-backed pools: a $170.6m Nigerian DICE fund-of-funds mandate and a naira-denominated impact vehicle targeting $1bn.

A 2019 fund changes hands seven years early — US endowment stress arrives in African private equity

Ecofin Agency (Idriss Linge) · Finance · Tue 7 Jul 2026

In plain English: a US endowment owns a stake in an African PE fund. On 1 July the tax on its investment income quadrupled and its federal research funding got squeezed, so it needs cash now — and a stake in a 2019 African fund is one of the least liquid things it holds. It sells, at whatever the market bears. Sango's entire business model is being the buyer on the other side of that trade: it closed a $120m secondary across four African funds earlier this year and has just added half a billion of Kuramo's book. The seller's problem has nothing to do with Africa — which is exactly why the price is interesting.

Ecofin Agency — “Sango Capital Takes Over Kuramo Africa Funds as U.S. Endowment Liquidity Pressure Spreads”

Monday's lead analysis argued that the AI build-out's 8–15% hurdle rate was pulling the world's marginal dollar away from African assets. This adds a second, independent force pushing in the same direction: US tax policy. An endowment squeezed by an 8% levy does not re-examine its Lagos fund on fundamentals; it sells whatever is illiquid and non-core, and African PE is both. Two unrelated machines — one in a data centre, one in the tax code — are now producing the identical output: Western institutional capital exiting African private markets on the seller's schedule, not the asset's.

“Sango is positioning itself as a specialist in managing the backlog of African private equity positions that older investors no longer want, or can no longer afford, to hold.”

Three reads. First, the secondary market this creates is a pricing signal Chronos rarely gets: when distressed Boston meets specialist Johannesburg, the clearing price tells you what disciplined buyers actually pay for African private assets — a live comp for every mark in the ecosystem. Second, the Kuramo half of the story is Monday's $2tn thread moving from argument to fact: as Western LPs exit, the replacement capital is Nigerian sovereign-backed pools — the DICE mandate and a $1bn naira vehicle — which changes who GPs answer to, what currencies funds think in, and which exits count as success. Third, the structural question: if Adams Street is right that endowment selling is a permanent re-weighting rather than a cash-flow wobble, then the LP base that built African PE over two decades is not coming back at the old size — and the firms that thrive will be the ones underwriting to African liquidity, African buyers and African exits. The forward question: when the next quality African fund raises, who is at the first close — and in which currency?

Africa · Connectivity · Regulation Analysis Thesis test
Two Starlink stories in 24 hours, two different walls. South Africa: the licencing question has become a political fight inside the GNU. Communications Minister Solly Malatsi's 11-page letter to Parliament rejects claims that his Equity Equivalent Investment Programme (EEIP) reform — which would let foreign operators earn BEE credits through investment rather than 30% ownership — was engineered for Starlink; committee chair Khusela Sangoni invoked “State Capture era” precedents. ICASA's position cuts deeper: under the existing Electronic Communications Act, satellite operators cannot get a network licence at all — only Parliament can change that. Kenya: Starlink has stopped taking new customers in seven counties including Nairobi and Mombasa, redirecting buyers to a waitlist after demand outran allocated network capacity. Subscribers tripled to 24,999 in nine months to March after kit prices fell from KES 89,000 to KES 49,900, with a KES 1,300 ($10) 50GB plan — still under 1% of Kenya's fixed-internet market.

Starlink's African week — the law is full in Pretoria, the network is full in Nairobi

TechCabal (Phathisani Moyo; Adonijah Ndege) · Telecoms · Mon 6 – Tue 7 Jul 2026

In plain English: the same company hit both kinds of African market limit in the same week. In South Africa the binding constraint is legal-political — no amount of demand gets Starlink a licence until Parliament amends the Act, and the fight has now moved from telecoms policy to who-met-whom. In Kenya the constraint is physics — each satellite beam carries finite capacity per cell, and in dense counties the cells filled up at fewer than 25,000 subscribers. One market is arguing about whether to let the future in; the other let it in and discovered it comes rationed.

TechCabal — “South Africa’s Starlink licencing row becomes political battle” TechCabal — “Starlink pauses new subscriptions in parts of Kenya”

The Kenya half is the quietly important one, because it punctures the assumption that LEO satellite is effectively infinite rural broadband. Starlink's capacity binds per cell, and it binds first exactly where the customers cluster — cities. At under 1% of Kenya's fixed market, Nairobi is already waitlisted. That means satellite remains a complement to terrestrial networks, not a substitute: the traffic-dense urban core stays with fibre and fixed wireless, whose per-user economics improve with density in a way a shared beam never can. Monday's AI-geography item made bankable power the real industrial policy; this makes spectrum-and-capacity the same kind of constraint one layer up.

“It is impossible to be unduly influenced to do something one is already doing.” — Solly Malatsi

The South African half is a governance stress test wearing a telecoms costume. The substantive question — should BEE accept equity-equivalent investment from multinationals that cannot sell 30% — is genuinely consequential for every foreign operator eyeing SA infrastructure, and it is now hostage to a proxy war between GNU partners with State Capture rhetoric already deployed. ICASA's point stands regardless of who wins: the reform requires primary legislation, so the timeline is parliamentary, measured in years. Two threads: Namibia sent Starlink away again in June, and Safaricom kept winning Kenyan broadband even at Starlink's peak growth — across the continent, the incumbent operators' moat is turning out to be a mix of density economics and regulatory patience. The forward question: which African regulator first writes a satellite-specific licence class — and whether SA's EEIP fight produces a template or a cautionary tale.

Global · Fintech · Best Thinkers Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Simon Taylor's Sunday Brainfood dissects Robinhood's new Earn product: 7% APY, offered inside the core app to all 27 million users, insured by Lloyd's against DeFi risks. The mechanics: deposits flow into a Morpho vault curated by Steakhouse, lending over-collateralised to institutions via spUSDG, USDe and SyrupUSDG. The yield is roughly half native and half incentives — Taylor's line: “roughly 3.5% of the yield is marketing spend, for now.” The plan is that as USDG volume grows, the stablecoin's Treasury float — which USDG shares with distributors like Robinhood — replaces the subsidy. Also in the issue: Plaid exploring a US IPO at ~$8bn; Meta tried to buy Kalshi before building its own prediction-market app; and Erebor — Palmer Luckey's bank — grew deposits from $1.1bn to $4.05bn in a single quarter on ~400 new customers (average account north of $7m, mostly above the FDIC cap), while holding zero loans.

Taylor on Robinhood's 7% — half the yield is marketing, the other half is the float coming home

Fintech Brainfood (Simon Taylor) · Yield Mechanics · Sun 5 Jul 2026

In plain English: put $10,000 into Robinhood Earn and about $350 a year of your 7% comes from genuine over-collateralised lending; the other $350 is Robinhood and the USDG consortium paying to acquire you. The endgame is that the subsidy gets replaced by the stablecoin's own Treasury interest — the same float that Monday's lead showed 140 companies agreeing to give away via Open USD. Two versions of one war: the consortium hands the float to businesses; Robinhood hands it to consumers as a headline rate. Either way, the float has stopped being the issuer's profit and become everyone's ammunition.

Fintech Brainfood — “Where Robinhood's 7% yield comes from”

Read this next to Friday's Rubinstein piece and the pattern completes. Stretch pays a dividend management adjusts to hold $100; Robinhood pays a yield that is half subsidy until the float arrives. In both cases the instrument's headline number is a decision, not a market price — the difference is that Robinhood's composition is published and Lloyd's-insured, while Strategy's is discretionary. The discipline for any yield product we ever look at: decompose the rate into what the assets earn and what someone is paying you to stay, because only the first half survives a downturn.

“Roughly 3.5% of the yield is marketing spend, for now.”

Erebor is the other half of the issue worth holding: $3bn of deposit growth in a quarter, ~400 customers, average balances above $7m, nearly all uninsured — Taylor names the shape himself: SVB. The difference is that Erebor holds zero loans, operating as a near-narrow bank, profitable without lending. Concentrated uninsured deposits plus no duration risk is a genuinely new bank shape, and it exists because a certain clientele will pay for a bank that cannot be choked off. The thread back to Africa: the deposit war being fought with float-sharing and 7% headlines will arrive on African USD rails — via the exchanges and stablecoin wallets in Monday's briefings — long before local-currency banks can match it, which is precisely the pressure the DR Congo item described from the street level. The forward question: when deposits earn 7% on a US rail accessible from a Nairobi phone, what does a Kenyan bank pay — and in which currency does the country save?

Africa · Sovereign Credit · Capital Flows Briefing Longitudinal
Africa Finance Corporation priced a $500m five-year senior unsecured Eurobond at a 5.375% coupon on 2 July — its tightest-ever spread over US Treasuries for that maturity, improving on 5.55% in 2024. The book closed at roughly twice the offering, rated A (S&P) / A3 (Moody's), led by Goldman Sachs, Standard Chartered, JPMorgan and Standard Bank among others. The detail that matters: an African central bank participated in an AFC issuance for the first time. Proceeds fund infrastructure and industrial projects; AFC has invested $19bn since 2007.

AFC borrows at its tightest spread ever — and an African central bank buys in for the first time

Ecofin Agency (Walid Kéfi) · Sovereign Credit · Sat 4 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the capital drain has a quality exemption — in the same week US endowments were selling African PE stakes, investment-grade African infrastructure paper found twice the demand at record-tight pricing; the tide is out for risk, not for Africa per se. And the first African central-bank order is Monday's $2tn thread moving in practice — African official reserves buying African development paper instead of only US Treasuries, one small allocation at a time.

Kenya · Central Banking · Regulation Briefing
President Ruto signed the Central Bank of Kenya (Amendment) Bill 2026 into law on Monday, creating Kenya's first statutory framework for emergency liquidity assistance. The CBK may now lend to banks that are solvent, viable and systemically important, against acceptable collateral, repayable within 12 months (extendable), on conditions the regulator sets. The law also rewrites the CBK's objectives around financial stability and — a quieter clause — expands the reserve assets it may hold and trade beyond gold and FX to gold coin, bullion, silver and platinum. Context: the sector earned KES 83.5bn ($645m) pre-tax in Q1, up 13.6%, on KES 8.73tn ($67bn) of assets, tightly coupled to M-PESA's payment rails.

Kenya writes its lender-of-last-resort rules before it needs them — and quietly broadens its reserves to metals

TechCabal (Kenn Abuya) · Banking · Mon 6 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: codifying emergency liquidity in calm weather is what a maturing financial system looks like — the conditions (solvent-only, collateralised, 12 months) are textbook Bagehot, arriving just as Kenyan private balance sheets get more exotic, from crypto-collateralised lending to bank-linked wallets. And the reserve-asset clause — silver, platinum, bullion — reads directly onto the Issue 036 thread: another African central bank building optionality beyond the dollar, this time in statute.

Nigeria · Payments · Chart Briefing Longitudinal
GTCO's fintech subsidiary HabariPay processed ₦80.9tn ($59bn) in 2025 — nearly 3x the prior year — and earned ₦9.74bn PAT, making it Nigeria's most profitable bank-owned fintech (Access's Hydrogen moved slightly more value, ₦85.9tn, for ₦1.65bn PAT). The 2026 plan: deploy 200,000 PoS terminals and grow terminal-processed value tenfold to ₦12tn, powered by GTBank's zero PoS processing fees — “for life. It is not a promo,” per group CEO Segun Agbaje — for merchants above ₦7.5m monthly turnover. The gap to close is vast: Moniepoint alone runs 1m+ terminals processing over ₦10tn a month.

Nigeria's banks start playing the fintech playbook — GTCO buys merchants with zero fees, for life

TechCabal (Temitayo Jaiyeola) · Payments · Tue 7 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the mirror image of last week's “fintechs becoming banks” — the banks are now becoming fintechs, and both sides are converging on the same prize: the merchant relationship and the deposits, data and lending it carries. The weapon is today's recurring move — give away the visible revenue line (fees) to own the flow, exactly what Open USD did to the float and Robinhood is doing to yield.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Hire the model as your sceptic — make it attack the argument before anyone else can

The 2×2 structured the decision; Monday's pre-mortem imagined its death. The third tool in the set attacks the argument itself, as written. Models are trained to be agreeable — hand one your draft and ask “what do you think?” and it will find three ways to say “strong work.” So remove the option. Assign it the role of the person whose job is to find the holes: the LP who has seen a hundred decks, the counterparty's lawyer, the partner who votes no. The instruction that does the work is banning agreement — when the model cannot approve, it must locate the weakest load-bearing claim, and it is unreasonably good at finding which sentence your whole argument leans on.

A worked example

Paste in a draft note or memo and prompt: “You are the most sceptical member of an investment committee, and you are not permitted to agree with me. Identify the three weakest claims in this document — quote each sentence exactly. For each: what evidence would prove it wrong, and how would I get that evidence this week? Then rewrite the single most misleading sentence the way an honest sceptic would phrase it. UK English, no preamble, no compliments.” Run it, fix what survives scrutiny, then run it once more — the second pass on the repaired draft is where the quality jump happens. When the sceptic starts inventing weak objections, the document is ready.
