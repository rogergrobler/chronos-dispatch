Nigeria · South Africa · Banking Analysis Deal structure
Absa Group — South Africa's third-largest lender by assets — is considering converting its Nigerian representative office into a merchant bank, chief executive Kenny Fihla told Bloomberg on Wednesday, in reporting carried by TechCabal on Thursday. The conversion would let Absa take corporate deposits, extend loans, and offer investment banking and project finance in Nigeria, where it currently operates through the representative office plus separate subsidiaries covering capital markets and securities — trade finance, investment banking and markets products only. The scale it brings: banking operations across South Africa, Kenya, Ghana, Uganda, Zambia, Tanzania, Botswana, Mozambique, Mauritius and Seychelles, more than 13.4 million customers, and R58.79bn ($3.61bn) of total income in the six months to June 2026 — of which South Africa, Kenya and Ghana generated more than 80% of profit. The market it would enter: Nigerian banking's market capitalisation reached ₦10.5 trillion ($7.7bn) in 2025, crowded with tier-1 locals — Access Holdings, Zenith Bank, First Bank — and already contested by Standard Bank and FirstRand. Merchant banking is a separately licensed business in Nigeria: Absa needs Central Bank of Nigeria approval before it can take a single deposit.

Absa answers the build-or-buy question with a third instrument — a licence: the representative office in Ikoyi may become a Nigerian merchant bank

TechCabal · TC Daily · Yemi Kareem & Zia Yusuf · Thu 20 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a South African bank weighing a Nigerian expansion, of the kind announced every other month in the opposite direction. The one that matters is the completion of a natural experiment. Within seven days, South Africa's three largest banking groups have given three different answers to the same Nigerian question: Standard Bank is negotiating equity in the distribution winner, FirstRand runs a commercial partnership with Optasia, and Absa now proposes a licence of its own. Note also what Absa is not doing — no retail flag, no agent-network war. Merchant banking is the corporate layer, the one place in Nigerian financial services where a foreign balance sheet still beats local distribution.

TechCabal Daily — “Absa wants a bigger piece of Nigeria's banking market”

The structural read is that the South African banks' return to Nigeria is happening at the wholesale layer, and the choice of layer is the whole strategy. The fintech decade destroyed the economics of Nigerian retail banking distribution — that was the lesson of yesterday's OPay card and of the CBN's agent-network interventions before it. It did very little to the economics of corporate and project finance, where the assets are large, the clients are documented, and the scarce input is a balance sheet with an investment-grade parent rather than fifty million app downloads. A merchant banking licence is precisely a claim on that layer: corporate deposits, syndicated lending, project finance for the infrastructure build Nigeria keeps announcing. It is also the cheapest of the three instruments on display this week. Standard Bank's pre-IPO stake requires paying a negotiated private mark for influence without control. FirstRand's partnership renews or lapses on commercial terms. A licence costs regulatory patience and paid-in capital, and what it buys is not exposure to somebody else's franchise but the right to build your own where the incumbents' moat is thinnest. The sequencing matters too: Absa comes to this a week after publishing what its own digital estate costs to run — R8.78bn of technology spend in a half-year, 28% of the cost base. An institution that knows precisely what building distribution costs at home has chosen the one Nigerian entry route that requires building almost none.

Equity, partnership, licence — three banks, one week, one market. The same question priced three ways is not coincidence. It is a market being told the answer is no longer obvious.

The forward marker: an actual licence application lodged with the CBN, and whether the entity is capitalised from group or raised locally. Until then this is a CEO signalling ambition in an interview, which costs nothing.

South Africa · Platforms · Pricing Power Analysis Chart of the day
From 1 October, Meta ends the free reply window it has run since launching WhatsApp Business in 2018. Every business reply sent through the WhatsApp Business API in South Africa will be billed per message: a service or utility reply — a bank alert, a shipping update — at 12 cents ($0.0075), a marketing message at 62 cents ($0.0379). The change hits businesses of all sizes — banks, telcos, ecommerce platforms and small merchants that adopted WhatsApp as their primary service desk during eight years in which replying within the 24-hour customer window cost nothing. The arithmetic TechCabal offers: a South African retailer sending 10,000 marketing messages now pays up to R6,200 ($385); for a bank processing millions of automated service replies, the 12-cent line compounds into millions of rands of new operating cost. The context that explains the timing: WhatsApp has 96% reach among South African internet users and a 98% open rate, and Meta's global monetisation push is chasing a “WhatsApp Business Economy” projected at over $45bn in 2026. South Africa is, relatively, getting a deal: the marketing rate in Nigeria is higher at $0.0516 per message.

Meta ends the free lunch it spent eight years serving — from 1 October every WhatsApp Business reply in South Africa costs 12 cents, and every marketing push 62

TechCabal · TC Daily · Yemi Kareem & Zia Yusuf · Thu 20 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a platform pricing change with a start date. The one that matters is the toll-booth phase of a classic platform sequence arriving in African enterprise messaging — subsidise adoption until the counterparty's alternatives atrophy, then meter the channel. What atrophied over eight free years was the call centre, the SMS gateway and the native app's push notification; what replaced them is a channel one company owns. The per-message billing model is the detail with the sting: it is the economics of SMS, reconstructed by the platform that destroyed SMS, at a higher price and with nowhere else to go.

Eight years of free was the customer-acquisition cost — the 96% reach is the asset it bought, and October is when the asset starts paying rent
TechCabal Daily — “No more free WhatsApp Business in South Africa”

The structural read is that this is the moment a distribution subsidy converts into a distribution tax, and the conversion price tells you what Meta believes about switching costs. Look at the ratio first: a marketing message costs five times a service reply. That is not a cost-based price — the bytes are identical. It is a value-based one: service replies are priced near infrastructure cost to keep the channel sticky, outbound push is priced at the value of guaranteed attention, because a 98% open rate is something no email list, app notification or SMS campaign can offer. The two prices together are a statement of strategy — keep the desk cheap, meter the megaphone. For the businesses underneath, the decision tree is uncomfortable in a specific way: the alternatives did not merely lose share over eight years, they atrophied. Call-centre capacity was cut against WhatsApp deflection numbers; SMS gateways were decommissioned; the native app's push channel was never built because 96% of customers were already somewhere better. Rebuilding any of those costs more than the toll. Meta knows this, which is why it can introduce SMS economics at above-SMS prices into a market that abandoned SMS on cost. The portfolio-facing point is immediate and practical: any operating company using the WhatsApp Business API as a service or collections channel — and in South Africa that is close to all of them — has a new line item arriving on 1 October that scales linearly with customer contact. The time to model the October run-rate, renegotiate BSP contracts and re-tier message templates is now, not in the first invoice cycle.

The service reply is priced at cost and the marketing push at value — one keeps you on the channel, the other charges you for what the channel became while it was free.

The threads: to Issue 068's item 3 (ICASA's zero-rating deadline: six operators, five months, thousands of public-benefit websites) — the same market, the same quarter, the same underlying asset, repriced from opposite directions: the regulator pushes the cost of the data channel onto operators, while the platform above them pushes the cost of the message channel onto businesses; a South African firm holds both invoices. To Issue 067's item 2 (Nigeria's CBN legislating the cash channel back from agents) — the recurring shape of the year: whoever controls a channel eventually charges for it or is made to; the only question is whether the charging party is a regulator or a platform. To Issue 068's item 1 and today's item 1 — Standard Bank and Absa are each paying, in equity and licence respectively, for exactly what Meta is now monetising: distribution that cannot be rebuilt at a competitive price. To the Chronos portfolio — the transferable exercise is a one-page October exposure count for each operating company: monthly WhatsApp service volume times R0.12, marketing volume times R0.62, against the cost of shifting low-value traffic to app push or email; and for any portco selling to consumers via WhatsApp, note that Nigeria's $0.0516 marketing rate makes the same funnel 36% more expensive there than in South Africa. The sceptic's ledger: the absolute numbers are small for service traffic and most SMEs will pay without noticing; alternatives exist at the margin — RCS, app push, email — and Meta has repriced this product repeatedly (per-conversation to per-message inside three years), so today's card rates are not stable facts; the $45bn economy figure is a vendor-adjacent projection; the 96% reach statistic comes from a market-research house, not Meta; and a price that pushes high-volume transactional traffic back toward SMS would be self-defeating, which argues Meta will tier before it strangles. The forward marker: whether South African banks absorb the cost or pass it through as a visible “WhatsApp fee” — the moment this line item reaches the customer, it becomes a repricing of digital banking itself.

South Africa · Reform · Political Economy Briefing Structural design
President Cyril Ramaphosa launched Phase 3 of the Government-Business Partnership at Summer Place in Johannesburg on Wednesday 20 August, alongside Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana. The headline target is unchanged and unmet: growth above 3% — the Phase 2 target set in October 2024 for end-2025, against which the economy delivered 1.1% last year and, in Ed Stoddard's reading, “may well be contracting”. The deliverables are dated. Crime and corruption: seven “high impact” prosecutions by Q1 2027, “backed by private sector legal, forensic and analytic capacity”. Logistics: six private train operating companies running on Transnet lines in 2027. Energy: the South African Wholesale Electricity Market launching by early 2027 — delayed from April 2026 — replacing Eskom's single-buyer monopoly with transparent, competitive pricing, plus 550km of new transmission line by March 2027. Mining: the cadastre system rolled out nationally by March 2027, the latest in a long list of promised dates, now carrying the Presidency's weight. Mining, tourism, agriculture and agro-processing join the partnership as new focus sectors. Credited to Phase 2: the removal of South Africa's FATF grey listing and the recent credit-ratings upgrades.

The growth target stays unmet and the dates get harder — Phase 3 prices South African reform in deadlines: an electricity market, six private train operators and a cadastre, all by 2027

Daily Maverick · Business Maverick · Ed Stoddard · Thu 20 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a relaunch of a partnership whose signature growth number has now been missed by two full points. The one that matters is a single line in the energy annex: the wholesale electricity market, dated to early 2027. That is the end of Eskom's single-buyer monopoly — the largest structural redesign of South African electricity since Eskom existed — and it lands 48 hours after the same government used that monopoly as a policy instrument to reprice smelter power. The state is simultaneously wielding the monopoly and scheduling its abolition. Hold both cards; only one describes the future.

Daily Maverick — “Ramaphosa launches Phase 3 of Government-Business partnership”

The structural read is that Phase 3 is best understood not as an economic plan but as a risk-transfer schedule — a list of state functions being opened, one by one, to private capacity, with dates attached. Read the deliverables as a set and the pattern is uniform: prosecutions backed by private legal and forensic capacity; freight rail carried by private train operating companies; electricity priced by a market rather than a single state buyer; mining rights allocated by a transparent cadastre rather than departmental discretion. Every line replaces an administrative allocation with either private execution or price discovery. That is the same institutional migration this page has tracked through SARS's e-invoicing proposal and the CBN's channel interventions, but running in the opposite direction: where those states were instrumenting private systems for public ends, Phase 3 has the South African state borrowing private systems because its own no longer execute. The prosecution line deserves the closest attention, because it is constitutionally the strangest: business funding the forensic capacity behind seven named-target prosecutions is either the overdue rescue of a hollowed-out NPA or a precedent in which prosecutorial priority follows private funding — and it will be described both ways, loudly, the moment the first charge sheet lands. The honest scorecard on the partnership so far: grey-listing exit delivered, ratings upgrades real, growth target missed by the full margin. The model produces institutional repairs, not yet output.

Every Phase 3 deliverable replaces an official's discretion with a price or a contract. The growth arrives, if it arrives, as a by-product of that substitution.

The threads: to Issue 068's item 2 (Eskom's smelter tariff — R10bn of debt relief redirected, electricity priced deal-by-deal, sector-by-sector) — the direct tension: Tuesday's policy works only because Eskom is a monopoly that can price by negotiation, and Thursday's schedules that monopoly's replacement with competitive pricing by early 2027; a smelter deal cut in 2026 must survive a market in 2027, and nobody on either podium mentioned the other. To Issue 068's item 4 (SARS inside the invoice) — the companion instrument: one arm of the state instrumenting private commerce, another arm inviting private capacity into state functions; taken together they sketch a state that is simultaneously extending its sensing and outsourcing its doing. To Issue 066's Spar-and-SAA governance card — the logistics thread continues: six private operators on Transnet lines is the systemic answer to what individual boards could not fix. To the Chronos read on South African exposure — the cadastre date is the sleeper: a functioning mining cadastre by March 2027 reopens exploration as an investable category in South Africa for the first time in a decade, and the assets that reprice first are the juniors and the services firms around them, not the majors. The sceptic's ledger: every Phase 3 date is a previously announced date re-announced — the electricity market has already slipped from April 2026, the cadastre carries “a long list of promised dates”, and re-dating is what these launches are for; the 3% target has been missed by an average of two points across two phases and nothing in Phase 3 changes demand; partnership launches are costless signalling with photography; “may well be contracting” sits under all of it; and Stoddard's own caveat cuts both ways — the reforms that did land (FATF, ratings) were the ones with external examiners, which the domestic deliverables lack. The forward marker: whether SAWEM's “early 2027” survives the Eskom smelter arithmetic — a wholesale market and a negotiated industrial tariff cannot both be the pricing mechanism, and which one gives way is the tell on everything else.

Nigeria · Defence Tech · Talent Flow Briefing Wouldn't-have-seen-otherwise
Terra Industries, the Nigerian defence-tech company that closed Africa's largest seed round at $52m this week, has appointed Ben MacWilliams as vice president of strategy. MacWilliams joins from SpaceX, where he led Starlink's market-access efforts across Africa — a remit covering all 54 African markets, through which he helped launch Starlink in more than 20 of them, having previously worked market access in the Middle East and Central Eurasia. At Terra he takes market entry, licensing strategy and government partnerships. The company he joins: Terra builds autonomous systems for governments and infrastructure operators monitoring critical assets such as power plants and mines — long- and mid-range autonomous drones, interceptor drones, sentry towers and unmanned ground vehicles. Alongside the raise it has opened a London office, is building a manufacturing facility in Ghana, and plans to extend its manufacturing footprint across the Gulf, South America and South Asia.

Terra's first big post-raise hire is not an engineer — it is the man who spent years getting African governments to say yes to Starlink

TechCabal · TC Daily · Yemi Kareem & Zia Yusuf · Thu 20 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a startup announcing an executive hire in the raise's afterglow. The one that matters is what the hire says the binding constraint is. For an African defence-tech company, the scarce input is not the drone — it is the licence, the end-user certificate, the ministry's signature. Terra has priced that constraint by making regulatory access its first senior appointment after the money landed. The CV is the strategy: a man whose product, for years, was government permission itself — and the direction of travel matters as much as the role, because a SpaceX executive choosing a Lagos-founded firm is its own data point on where the African opportunity now sits.

TechCabal Daily — “Terra Industries taps ex-SpaceX executive to help expand into new markets”

The structural read is that market access has become a named executive discipline in African tech, and this hire is the cleanest evidence yet of what it is worth. Starlink's African rollout was, beneath the satellite story, a regulatory sequencing machine: identify the jurisdictions where approval is cheapest, land them first, then use each operating licence as precedent against the harder neighbours — twenty-plus countries cleared in a few years against incumbent telco resistance and spectrum politics. That is a repeatable playbook, and it lives in people rather than in documents. Terra is buying the playbook for a harder version of the same game: defence-adjacent autonomous systems face export-control regimes, end-user certificates, and procurement processes in which the counterparty is always a state and the sales cycle is measured in budget years. A company whose product surveils power plants and mines does not scale by app-store distribution; it scales one sovereign signature at a time, which is why the appointment reads less like headcount and more like infrastructure. The talent-flow signal runs alongside: the movement of a Hawthorne executive into a Lagos-founded company inverts a decade of African tech's brain-drain anxiety, and it happened because the sector that can pay for such a person shifted — from consumer fintech, where the moat was distribution, to state-adjacent hard tech, where the moat is permission.

Starlink's real product in Africa was the licence, manufactured twenty times over. Terra has hired the production line.

Cameroon · CEMAC · Credit Infrastructure Analysis Hyper-local
BEE, a Cameroonian asset-financing company, is building credit infrastructure for informal workers across the CEMAC monetary union and the DRC — a region where the World Bank puts more than 65% of jobs in the informal sector (Cameroon itself near 90% per the ILO), regional unemployment at 9.7% runs four times neighbouring UEMOA's, and daily cash earnings leave no paper trail a bank recognises. Founder Patrick Timani's pre-history is the thesis in miniature: an informal lending book for traders and poultry farmers, funded by a German loan at 4% a year and re-lent at 2.5% a month, grown to roughly €300,000 — until COBAC raised the minimum microfinance capital requirement to CFA500m ($828,000) in January 2018 and priced him out of a licence. BEE began instead as a Gojek-style delivery platform, discovered its drivers did not own their motorcycles, financed 50 motos as a test — and pivoted to the model it now runs: BEE supplies clients, technology and distribution; roughly five partner microfinance institutions supply capital and carry the credit risk. Financed motorcycles repay against usage with remote-lock as a graduated backstop; smartphones are financed through supplier-credit lines from Transsion, Samsung and, from 2026, HMD — with interest accruing only from device activation, so unsold stock costs nothing. A seed round now closing is meant to take Cameroonian MFI partners from five to twelve and fund expansion into Côte d'Ivoire and Senegal (same OHADA law, same CFA franc) and the DRC (OHADA, but its own currency and central bank). The scale reference is M-KOPA: over $2bn disbursed to nearly 10 million customers, with 42% of its smartphone customers buying their first device through the platform — in the markets M-KOPA chose. BEE is running the same logic in the region it never prioritised.

BEE lends against the motorcycle, not the payslip — and rents its risk engine to microfinance houses that hold the capital but cannot see the borrower

TechCabal · Francophone Weekly · Lina Kacyem · Tue 18 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is another African asset-financing startup with a motorcycle photo. The one that matters is the division of labour: BEE keeps the technology, the merchants and the distribution, and puts none of its own balance sheet at risk; the microfinance institutions — sitting on capital and a legal mandate they cannot safely deploy, with defaults above 15% when they try — carry the credit. That is the M-KOPA thesis restructured as infrastructure rather than as a lender, pointed at the one region the East African playbook never reached. Held from Tuesday's watchlist for depth; this is the depth.

TechCabal Francophone Weekly — “Millions earn daily. This Cameroonian startup wants banks to lend to them.”

The structural read is that the CEMAC credit gap is substantially a regulatory artefact, and BEE's architecture is the shape of the loophole. Follow the causality: in January 2018 COBAC raised the minimum capital for a microfinance licence to CFA500m, a threshold that stopped Timani — then running a profitable €300,000 informal book — from becoming a licensed lender. Eight years later, the consequence of that rule is that he built the infrastructure to the licensed lenders instead: the MFIs keep the licence and the balance sheet, BEE keeps the origination, scoring and enforcement rails, and earns on flow without capital at risk. The regulation that concentrated the licences created the market for renting capability to licence-holders. Three design details are worth carrying into other contexts. First, the remote-lock is framed as a repayment safeguard with a defined grace-and-escalation process rather than an instant kill-switch — the M-KOPA convention, and the thing regulators will eventually test. Second, for assets that cannot be locked, BEE substitutes social collateral: two guarantors with repayment history, or lending through savings mutuals that already underwrite their members — a formalisation of the tontine rather than a replacement of it. Third, and the one worth stealing: supplier credit from Transsion, Samsung and HMD accrues interest only from device activation, so inventory sits on the shelf at zero financing cost — a working-capital design most formal retailers do not get. The Google-grant misadventure belongs in the record too: $100k deployed into importing 230 motorcycles directly, a forwarder with fake quotes, sixty days of delay, no local spare parts, abandoned bikes — and Timani's own verdict that the capital should have gone into rails, not inventory. A founder giving a costed account of his worst mistake, whose stated lesson is precisely the architecture the company now runs, is a governance signal worth more than most reference calls.

The 2018 capital rule decided who may hold a lending licence. It could not decide who builds the machinery the licence-holders depend on — and that turned out to be the better business.

To the M-KOPA card of 24 July (10 million customers) — the incumbent playbook whose geographic blind spot defines BEE's opportunity: same model, same collateral logic, but in CFA francs and OHADA law, where M-KOPA never went. To the FirstRand–Optasia structure this page keeps returning to — a bank renting origination and scoring capability rather than building it is the same trade as an MFI renting BEE, executed at a hundred times the scale; the pattern is fractal. The sceptic's ledger: five MFI partners is a pilot wearing the word infrastructure; the one number that matters — portfolio at risk on the financed book — is nowhere in the piece, and every operational claim is founder-sourced; remote-locking a person's income-producing asset has a consumer-protection shadow that COBAC has not yet priced; the DRC is not an extension but a rebuild (own currency, own central bank, own rules), whatever the OHADA overlap; a seed round described as “closing” is not closed; and the informal-lending pre-history — borrowing at 4% a year to lend at 2.5% a month — is a spread that says as much about what CEMAC borrowers will bear as about what they lack. The forward marker: a disclosed default rate once the round closes. Infrastructure claims are settled by portfolio-at-risk tables, not by team photos.

South Africa · Household Finance · Retirement Analysis Chart of the day
Two years into the two-pot retirement system, Old Mutual Corporate has surveyed its withdrawing members three times — before launch, after the first six-month window, and after the March 2025 to February 2026 tax year — with the last two rounds drawing responses from about 35,000 claimants. The finding chief customer officer Michelle Acton leads with: before launch, members said the money would go to emergencies and debt; in the latest survey, “the top reason is basic living” — food, supporting family members, school fees, rent and electricity. Where withdrawals do service debt, it is unsecured debt — personal and family loans, not home loans or car finance. Against that, the behavioural indicators have improved: Old Mutual's preservation rates have risen 33%, cash withdrawals on exit fell from 61% to below 50%, and the number of members saying they will withdraw again has almost halved. Two lines of read: the surface story is a retirement-reform progress report with survey numbers. The one that matters is that both halves of the dataset are true at once. The feared soft-life raids never materialised; instead the savings pot has become a grocery budget — and simultaneously, preservation is up 33%, exit cash-outs have fallen below 50% from 61%, and repeat-withdrawal intent has halved. The mechanism works precisely as designed. What it discloses, now that 35,000 claimants have told a fund manager why they took the money, is the state of the South African household income statement — the most direct read on it that exists.

The system worked and the households didn't — the valve holds, and the pressure it releases is the grocery bill
Daily Maverick — “Two-pot, two years later: savings are used to put food on the table”

The structural read is that two-pot has turned out to be two instruments wearing one name: a retirement reform, which is working, and an involuntary household-stress survey, which is working rather too well. Take the reform first, because the result is genuinely counterintuitive: giving members controlled access to a slice of their savings has reduced total leakage from the system. The old regime's escape hatch was resignation — the only way to reach your own money was to leave your job and cash out the lot, which is why 61% took cash on exit. Bounded liquidity killed the incentive to do that, and exit cash-outs fell below 50% while preservation rose 33%. Designing a small legitimate outlet to protect a large pool is a mechanism worth filing well beyond pensions; it is the same logic as the pressure-relief valve in any system that would otherwise rupture at the weakest joint. Now the disclosure half. The withdrawal-reason migration — from “emergencies and debt” in prospect to “basic living” in fact — is the most direct measurement of South African household distress available anywhere, because it is revealed preference with money attached, from 35,000 respondents, collected by an institution with no incentive to dramatise it. Survey inflation expectations, confidence indices and retail-sales prints all infer the household income statement; this one reads it. Food, school fees, rent, electricity: those are not deferrable categories, and a population drawing down long-term assets to fund them is describing an income shortfall, not a spending choice. The unsecured-debt detail sharpens it further — the loans being settled are the personal and family loans that fill the gap between payday and survival, the invisible credit layer that no credit bureau fully sees.

Thirty-five thousand people told their fund manager why they touched their pension. The answer was groceries. There is no cleaner macro data in South Africa this year.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Ask for the ugly draft first — then spend your time editing, not briefing

The instinct with these models is to write ever more elaborate briefs, hoping the first output arrives finished. That is backwards, and it wastes the thing the model is best at. A model produces a complete draft in thirty seconds; you cannot brief for thirty minutes and beat that trade. So invert the workflow: give a two-line brief, explicitly ask for a rough version — “give me the ugly first draft, do not polish it” — and then do your real work in the edit: “paragraph two is the actual opening, cut the first”, “the third argument is weak, replace it”, “halve it”. Editing a concrete draft engages your judgement far faster than specifying an abstract one, because you always know what is wrong with a text in front of you and rarely know, in advance, everything the perfect brief should have said. Three rounds of blunt edits on a fast draft beats one round on a laboured one, every time — and it keeps you in the role you are actually good at: the editor with the red pen, not the client writing a creative brief.

A worked example

Weak, because it front-loads everything: a 400-word brief specifying tone, structure, audience and seven points for a portfolio company note — followed by disappointment. Strong, because it spends the effort where it compounds: “Draft a one-page note to a portco CEO on the WhatsApp Business pricing change — ugly first version, no polish.” Read it. Then: “Cut the preamble, open with the October date. The cost example should use their volumes: 2m service messages a month. Add one line on renegotiating their BSP contract. Drop the paragraph on Meta strategy — they don't care. Halve it.” Two minutes of edits, four moves, and the note is yours rather than the model's. The brief you would have needed to get that in one shot does not exist.
