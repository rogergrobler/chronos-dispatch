South Africa · Macro · Monetary policy Briefing Longitudinal payoff
The decision this page flagged yesterday is in, and it went against consensus. The SARB's Monetary Policy Committee held the repo rate at 7.0% — prime stays at 10.5% — with the vote split 4–2, the two dissenters preferring a 25-basis-point hike. Most economists, Citi among them, had expected a hike with June CPI at 5.0% against the 3.0% target and oil hovering near $100 a barrel as the Middle East crisis entered what Governor Kganyago called "a new and volatile phase". The committee's logic: upside risks to inflation, but downside risks to growth — households squeezed by fuel prices, investment weighed by uncertainty, and a baseline forecast that the economy recovers in H2 "as the shock fades". Kganyago also noted a striking offset in the global picture: the AI boom propping up world growth even as oil drags it down. The market's verdict was immediate — the rand weakened sharply on the announcement, having strengthened the day before on hike expectations. Citi's Gina Schoeman called it "a hawkish hold" and expects a hike at the September MPC.

The SARB blinks — a 4–2 hold at 7% with inflation at 5% against a 3% target

EWN / The Money Show · Paula Luckhoff · Thu 23 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: yesterday's page asked whether the SARB would buy credibility for its two-year-old target with a hiking cycle the domestic economy did nothing to earn — and the answer, by four votes to two, was no. The rand falling on a hold is the tell: when a currency weakens because a central bank didn't act, the market is saying it wanted the credibility defence and will now charge for its absence.

EWN — “SARB keeps interest rates unchanged, surprising markets and analysts”

The committee chose the textbook over the credibility manual. The textbook says look through supply shocks — a petrol price set in the Strait of Hormuz doesn't respond to South African policy rates, and hiking into it taxes an economy that didn't cause the problem. The credibility manual says a target regime still in its infancy cannot afford to let the first sustained deviation pass unanswered. Yesterday's page framed the meeting as a choice between the two; the 4–2 split shows the committee itself was divided along exactly that line. What tips the reading towards "deferred" rather than "abandoned" is the language: Kganyago's "the MPC will act as needed", Schoeman's "hawkish hold", and Citi's base case of a September hike. The bank has kept the gun on the table; it just declined to fire it into a growth slowdown.

The rand fell on the hold — the market wanted the credibility defence, and will now charge for its absence.

The transmission for the businesses on these pages cuts both ways. Prime holding at 10.5% rather than rising is direct relief for the consumer-credit and small-merchant segments where South African fintech lives — the FintechCo traders on today's page, M-KOPA's Every Day Earners, every lending book priced off prime. But a weaker rand imports the very inflation the hold declined to fight, and if oil stays near $100, September's meeting inherits the same dilemma with less room and less credibility. The AI-boom line deserves its own note: a central bank governor now lists artificial intelligence as a structural offset to an oil shock in a monetary policy statement — the technology thread this page runs weekly has reached the MPC's growth model. The caveat from the other side: if the war ends and oil normalises, the hold will look prescient rather than timid, and the two dissenters will be the footnote. The September meeting is now the real first test — this one ended in a draw, and draws don't anchor expectations.

South Africa · Fintech · Informal economy Analysis Structural design
Hours after Pepkor announced the R21.3bn Flash–Shop2Shop merger, Shop2Shop founder and CEO Peter Berry took an investor call and laid out the thesis the deal is buying. His forecast: within five years the informal market formalises — spaza owners become multi-store entrepreneurs, and cash all but vanishes from the townships. The obstacle he named is specific: taxis. The taxi industry moves millions of commuters daily, runs almost entirely on cash, and thereby anchors demand for physical notes across the whole township economy — as long as commuters need cash for transport, traders must handle it too. "Once that layer is solved, cash will disappear. We are working hard on that." The numbers say the shift is well under way: money digitised into Shop2Shop's ecosystem — via cash-accepting smart safes and Tap2Pay acquiring devices — grew from R58bn in the year to March 2023 to R172bn in the year to March 2026, a 44% compound rate, with card payments' share rising from 30% to 40%. The platform serves 177,000 traders with 114,000 devices and 2,900 safes, and processed R131bn in supplier payments in twelve months. Pepkor sizes the informal market at R900bn–R1.5tn; spaza retail alone at R190bn, township fast food another R90bn.

"Solve taxis and cash dies" — Shop2Shop names the last bastion of township cash

TechCentral · Fanie van Rooyen · Thu 23 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: yesterday's lead was the deal; today is the operating thesis underneath it, from the founder's own mouth — and the taxi observation is the sharpest piece of informal-economy analysis this page has carried. And note the endpoint he names: "this market ends up looking similar to the Chinese market, where most payments are digitalised" — the merger is a bet that South Africa's townships follow the WeChat Pay arc, with FintechCo as the rail.

Three years, three times the digitised cash — and the taxi rank still standing between FintechCo and the endgame
TechCentral — “Solve taxis and cash dies: Shop2Shop's township thesis”

The taxi insight deserves to be taken seriously as structural analysis, not colour. Cash is a network good: its value to any one participant depends on how many others still demand it. Berry's claim is that the township cash network has a single anchor tenant — the taxi industry — and that removing it collapses the equilibrium. That is a testable, falsifiable operating thesis, and it explains something the "cashless South Africa" narrative never could: why card penetration soared while township cash remained stubbornly alive. The commuter who must carry cash for the taxi rank spends cash at the spaza; the spaza must bank it through smart safes; the whole chain exists because of the first link. It also explains the sequencing of Berry's ambition — the R172bn already digitised is trade and supplier flow, the part of the chain taxis don't anchor. The consumer-payment layer is what's left, and it runs through the rank.

Cash is a network good with one anchor tenant — and the anchor tenant is the taxi industry.

The scepticism worth carrying is that the taxi industry is the hardest payments problem in South Africa precisely because it is not merely a technology problem: it is politically organised, structurally informal, and has repelled digitisation attempts for two decades — from card readers to app-hailing hybrids. "Great teams on the ground" is not a mechanism, and Berry conspicuously gave no detail. The China comparison also cuts both ways: WeChat Pay's township-equivalent conquest ran on a super-app duopoly, state tolerance of platform dominance, and QR economics that South African interchange structures don't replicate. The threads: yesterday's lead priced the platform at R21.3bn; today's call explains what the platform believes; and the PlusB detail at the end — Pepkor's separate challenger bank, framed as the bridge between "informal, digital and formal economies" — means the group is assembling deposit-taking alongside the rails. Set beside the SARB item above: every rand that moves from notes to FintechCo's ecosystem is a rand the central bank's cash-management machinery no longer sees — formalisation of the informal economy and monetary policy transmission are the same story on different timescales.

Pan-Africa · Fintech · Asset finance Analysis Hyper-local
M-KOPA has reached 10 million customers across Kenya, Uganda, Nigeria, Ghana and South Africa — and the shape of the curve is the story. The first million took eight years (2012–2020, the solar pay-as-you-go era); the next nine million took six, following the 2020 pivot to smartphone financing. The company now onboards roughly 10,000 customers a day through a 40,000-agent network, processes more than two million customer repayments daily, and has held average annual revenue growth of 50% since 2020. The financial substance behind the milestone: a first-ever annual profit reported in October 2025 alongside a 66% revenue jump; more than $1.6bn of cumulative credit extended in Kenya; and ₦231bn (about $170m) disbursed to over one million Nigerians — Nigeria being the fastest market in the company's history to reach a million customers. The model: the financed smartphone is the gateway, with embedded credit, insurance and device protection attached at the point of purchase. CEO Jesse Moore's framing: "Every Day Earners have always been creditworthy. What they needed was credit built around how they really make a living, not a payslip. Informal has never meant unviable."

M-KOPA's second act: eight years to the first million customers, six to the next nine

TechCabal · Opeyemi Kareem · Wed 22 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the bottom-up mirror of today's Shop2Shop item — the same informal-economy thesis executed customer-by-customer rather than platform-by-platform, and the growth inflection dates precisely from the moment the product became a smartphone rather than a solar panel. Two million repayments a day is the quiet number that matters: that is a real-time creditworthiness dataset on ten million informal earners that no bank, bureau or telco in these markets possesses.

The product changed in 2020 — and the curve changed with it
TechCabal — “M-KOPA reaches 10 million customers, six years after its first million”

The structural read: M-KOPA discovered that the asset it finances is also its distribution, underwriting and lock-in mechanism. A solar panel is a one-time sale with a repayment tail; a smartphone is a channel — every financed handset becomes the storefront for the next product (insurance, cash loans, device protection), and the daily micro-repayment stream doubles as a live credit bureau for a population formal finance cannot score. That is why the 2020 pivot bent the curve: the company stopped selling products to the informal economy and started owning a relationship with it. The 40,000-agent network is the physical moat — the same lesson as Shop2Shop's 177,000 traders and Flash's township footprint above: in African fintech, distribution on the ground is the asset the balance sheet never quite captures.

The financed smartphone is not the product — it is the channel, the collateral and the credit bureau in one device.

The scepticism has two layers. First, the disclosure gap several outlets flagged: 10 million is a cumulative customer count, not an active-user number, and beyond the October 2025 profit announcement the company publishes little — margins, default rates and the split between device financing and cash lending remain opaque. Second, the competitive field is crowding: Sun King, d.light and EasyBuy run the same playbook, and phone-financing economics are sensitive to handset price deflation and default cycles in exactly the segments a 10.5% prime rate squeezes — which is why today's SARB hold, and its September sequel, sit closer to this item than geography suggests. The threads: this is the bottom-up half of the informal-economy pincer today's Shop2Shop item runs top-down; it extends the Rank thread (Issue 049) on informal finance institutionalising; and Nigeria as the fastest-growing market plugs into the same consumer-credit demand the airtime-lending ruling (Issue 049) and FairMoney's PoS-lending push both signal. Forward marker: M-KOPA has reached the scale where the exit question — IPO, strategic sale, or the Airtel Money route of a separated fintech listing — starts writing itself. The next capital event on this asset will price the whole informal-lending category.

Nigeria · Regulation · Tax infrastructure Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Nigeria is wiring every qualifying business invoice into a real-time digital feed to the Nigeria Revenue Service. Under the national e-invoicing programme, invoices are transmitted to the NRS as they are issued — each carrying a unique Invoice Reference Number so declared turnover can be checked against actual sales. Large taxpayers are already under compliance monitoring; medium-sized businesses begin mandatory onboarding this month; small businesses follow in 2027. The platform is built to handle 50,000 requests per second, roughly 50 providers have been certified to connect businesses, APIs integrate with more than 50 ERP systems, and — a NITDA non-negotiable — all e-invoicing data must be hosted inside Nigeria, aligning with the central bank's directive that banks localise critical data by January 2027. The regional context: Tanzania (2010), Rwanda (2017), Uganda (2020) and Ghana (2023) all run versions of fiscal e-reporting, and South Africa's VAT Modernisation Project targets phased e-invoicing from 2028. With over 2.5 million registered businesses, Nigeria's rollout could become Africa's largest — and the programme's architects say they studied Poland's suspended KSeF and Kenya's abandoned hardware-based TIMS to avoid their fates.

Nigeria turns every invoice into state data — a tax reform that doubles as an economic nervous system

TechCabal · Frank Eleanya · Thu 23 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: read this as infrastructure, not tax administration — a state assembling real-time observability over its own economy, invoice by invoice, with consumption patterns, supply chains and trade flows as the by-product. And place it in the stack it belongs to: NIN-anchored identity, CBN data localisation, SIM-as-ID next door in South Africa (Issue 048) — the digital-state architecture this page has tracked all month now gains its commercial layer.

TechCabal — “Nigeria's biggest tax reform turns business invoices into real-time economic data”

The design choices are more interesting than the ambition. Nigeria's public-sector IT graveyard is well populated, and the programme's architects are conspicuously building against it: months of pre-development consultation with MTN, UBA, Access Bank and sector focus groups; APIs over forced ERP replacement; a three-year phased rollout explicitly modelled on why Malaysia's big-bang attempt failed and why Kenya had to abandon fiscal hardware for software. The NITDA accreditation layer — 37 applicants, a dozen initially competent, ~50 now certified — is quietly creating a licensed integration industry around the mandate, which is where the commercial opportunity sits: every one of 2.5 million businesses that must connect is a customer for somebody, and the Access Point Provider licence is the toll booth. The data-localisation requirement stitches this to the CBN's January 2027 order — two regulators, one sovereignty doctrine: Nigerian economic data lives in Nigeria.

A licensed integration industry is being built around the mandate — 2.5 million businesses that must connect, and a toll booth called the Access Point Provider licence.

The deeper tension sits with the rest of today's page. E-invoicing makes the formal economy legible — but the businesses this page tracks daily live precisely where invoices don't: the spaza shops Shop2Shop digitises, the Every Day Earners M-KOPA finances, the cash economy Berry says only dies when taxis do. Real-time invoice data will sharpen Nigeria's view of the economy it can already see while the majority of commerce remains dark — unless the fintech rails and the tax rails eventually meet, which is exactly what the AfCFTA cross-border framing in the piece hints at: standardised digital invoices as trade-finance collateral and customs documents, the same corridor logic as the stablecoin-settlement thread (Issues 048–050). The threads back: Nigeria froze its digital rulebook to avoid writing it three times (Issue 043); the split-decision court ruling drew regulator boundaries on Monday (Issue 049); this is the same state, now building rather than adjudicating. The sceptic's line holds: success is an adoption question, not a throughput question — 50,000 requests per second means nothing if the invoices aren't issued. Watch the medium-business onboarding that starts this month; that cohort, not the large taxpayers already under monitoring, is where every e-invoicing programme has lived or died.

Nigeria · Stablecoins · Wealth Briefing Longitudinal
Daya, the Nigerian startup building a stablecoin payment stack for African businesses, is expanding from payments into investments. Daya Stocks — live in testing — will offer more than 400 US-listed stocks and ETFs, including Nvidia, Tesla and the S&P 500 ETF, with fractional ownership from a $1 minimum. The company raised a $2.4m pre-seed only last month (June 2026), backed by Alliance DAO and Aptos among others, having started as a stablecoin neobank for cross-border business payments; it is also part of a HashKey–Aptos initiative building a regulated stablecoin corridor between the Middle East and Africa. The logic of the extension is mechanical: once customer balances already sit in dollar stablecoins for payments, tokenised securities are one integration away — the wallet becomes a brokerage without ever touching the legacy custody chain.

From rails to brokerage: Daya puts the S&P 500 a dollar away from a naira wallet

TechCabal · Emmanuel Nwosu · Thu 23 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the fourth consecutive page on which stablecoin rails absorb another function — trade settlement (Issue 048), remittances via LemFi–BVNK, machine payments via OUSD (both Issue 050), and now retail wealth — each a small company, each the same direction of travel. A $1 entry point to US equities from a naira wallet is, in substance, retail dollarisation of savings — and it walks straight into the dollar-tracing regime Nigeria's central bank was building in Issue 047.

TechCabal — “Alliance-backed Daya expands beyond stablecoins with tokenised US stock trading”

The company is small; the signal is not. The page's stablecoin thread has mostly tracked the rails themselves — who settles, who issues, who governs. Daya marks the next phase: product layers stacking on top of rails that now exist. Once a customer's working balance lives in a dollar stablecoin, every dollar-denominated product — equities, ETFs, yield, eventually credit — is an API call rather than a correspondent-banking relationship, a custody agreement and an FX approval. That collapses the cost structure that kept US securities out of reach of African retail: the incumbent route (local broker, dollar account, minimum tickets) filtered for the affluent; a $1 fractional minimum filters for nobody. The wallet-as-brokerage is the same architectural move as M-KOPA's phone-as-bank elsewhere on this page — the access device absorbing the financial institution.

Once balances live in dollar stablecoins, every dollar product is an API call away — the wallet becomes the brokerage.

The caveats are proportionate to the stage: this is a testing-phase product from a pre-seed company, the piece is silent on which regulator, if any, has blessed tokenised US equities for Nigerian retail, and tokenised stocks vary widely in what legal claim the token actually confers — some are securities, some are derivatives wearing a costume. The macro tension is the interesting part: Nigeria's central bank spent Issue 047 building the pipes to trace every retail dollar, and savings migrating into tokenised S&P 500 positions is capital flight in product form — legal, granular and continuous. Every emerging-market regulator that watched Argentina's crypto-dollar decade knows this movie. Thread forward: watch whether the SEC Nigeria moves first to license it or to stop it — the answer will say which instinct governs the digital-state stack the e-invoicing item above describes: observability, or control.

Global · Banking · Succession Analysis World's-best-thinkers
Jamie Dimon is 70, has run JPMorgan for over 20 years, and this month the board lined up two possible successors — prompting Marc Rubinstein's Net Interest essay on what the job actually requires. The scale of the hand-over: JPMorgan's balance sheet crossed $5 trillion this quarter, it carries $63.7 trillion of derivative notionals, and the Bank for International Settlements ranks it the most complex bank on the planet — ahead of Barclays, HSBC, BNP Paribas and Goldman Sachs — on derivative notionals, $29bn of illiquid level-3 assets and $549bn of trading and available-for-sale securities. Under Dimon, total assets grew almost five-fold from $1.2 trillion, headcount from 168,847 to 320,560, and the annual report from 144 pages to 364. Dimon's own job spec for a successor runs from "analytical" and "culture carrier" to "heart, grit, soul" and the ability to "walk in operating centers and deal with CEOs and prime ministers". Rubinstein sharpens it with Chris Hohn's story of Credit Suisse's pre-crisis CEO admitting he did not understand his own multi-trillion-dollar balance sheet.

What it takes to hand over JPMorgan — Rubinstein on succession at the most complex bank on earth

Net Interest · Marc Rubinstein · Fri 17 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is yesterday's Andrew Ng letter taken to its limiting case — if seniority is integration span, the CEO of a $5tn, 320,000-person, 364-page-annual-report institution is the most extreme integration role in commerce, and Dimon's quality list is an integration-complexity job spec written by its only living holder. The Hohn–Dougan anecdote is the risk statement: complexity that outgrows its manager is how banks die, and succession is the moment the gap gets tested.

Net Interest — “Too Big to Succeed”

The essay's quiet argument sits in its numbers: every measure of JPMorgan — assets, headcount, disclosure length, derivative book — has grown faster than any individual's capacity to hold it in mind, and the institution has compensated by concentrating an abnormal integration load in one twenty-year CEO. That is the succession problem in its pure form: the bank is not handing over a role, it is handing over an accumulated, person-specific map of a $5tn balance sheet that took two decades to draw. Dimon's list — management, people, analytics, detail, culture, curiosity, heart, grit, soul, work ethic, travel, operating centres, prime ministers — reads as a confession that the role has outgrown any specification; you cannot hire against twelve superlatives, you can only test candidates against subsets and pray the integration talent generalises.

The bank is not handing over a role — it is handing over a person-specific map of a $5tn balance sheet that took twenty years to draw.

The threads run in two directions. Back to Ng (Issue 050): AI automates the verifiable and hands the integration complexity to fewer, broader people — JPMorgan is the terminal case, where the integration span has compounded into a single irreplaceable node, and the interesting question is whether AI tooling widens the pool of people who can hold the map or narrows it further. And back to this page's control-through-people thread — Safaricom shedding fintech chiefs, GTBank recalling its Kenyan MD, Nedbank's coming appointments at NCBA: the same lesson at every scale, that who holds the integration map is the real control layer, and the announcements matter more than the org charts. For the companies Chronos assesses, the Hohn–Dougan test is portable and brutal: can the CEO walk through the balance sheet line by line? The honest "no" from Credit Suisse's CEO preceded the institution's slow death by a decade and a half. The caveats: the full mechanics of Rubinstein's answer sit behind Net Interest's paywall — worth a subscription for any partner who wants the succession-design detail — and the piece published last Friday, surfacing today on the last day of its window because the week's African flow crowded it out. Better a day late than never seen.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Talk, don't type — dictate the messy version and let Claude find the brief inside it

The last three tips built your written setup: standing instructions, recurring Projects, a home for every deal. Today's tip is about the input side, and it is the one most partners underuse: voice. Every major AI app — Claude, ChatGPT — takes dictation, and the practical unlock is realising you are not dictating a document, you are dictating raw material. Walking out of a board meeting, driving between engagements, on the trail: open the app, talk for three unstructured minutes — repetitions, half-thoughts, "no wait, scratch that" included — and then ask one question: "Turn that into a structured note: decisions, concerns, actions, open questions." The model is exceptionally good at finding the signal in spoken mess, and the three minutes of talking capture nuance that the two-line note you would otherwise type at 22:00 never will. The compounding version: dictated debriefs accumulating inside a deal's Project become a contemporaneous record of what you actually thought at each stage — the raw material every post-mortem wishes it had.

A worked example

Straight after your next management meeting, before opening email, dictate: everything that struck you, in any order — the CFO's hesitation on the cash question, the number that didn't reconcile, the good feeling about the new COO, the thing you forgot to ask. Then: "Structure that as a meeting debrief: impressions of management, red flags, follow-ups for Isa to diarise, and the one question I must ask next time." Compare it with what you would have typed. The typed version has three bullet points; the spoken one has the hesitation on the cash question — and that hesitation is usually the finding.
