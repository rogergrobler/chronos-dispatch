Ethiopia · AI · Infrastructure Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Vercel, the US cloud platform behind the Next.js framework, has acquired Better Auth — the open-source authentication library built almost single-handedly by Bereket Engida, a self-taught programmer who started coding at 18 in his bedroom in Addis Ababa. Better Auth lets developers embed sign-in, roles and password resets directly into their own applications instead of renting them from Auth0, Firebase or NextAuth, keeping identity logic and sensitive tokens inside the developer's own database. Weekly npm downloads have climbed from ~150,000 at its $5m seed a year ago (Peak XV, Y Combinator, P1 Ventures, Chapter One) to 4.7m today, with 850+ contributors. Engida and his core team join Vercel to lead work on “agent identity” — separate, revocable credentials for autonomous AI agents — and the library stays free and MIT-licensed. Only the third Ethiopian founder through Y Combinator; among the most prominent infrastructure-software exits by a solo African founder to date.

The login layer for AI agents was built in Addis Ababa — and Vercel just bought it

Launch Base Africa · AI × Dev Infrastructure · Wed 9 Jul 2026

In plain English: yesterday's Ramp item handed AI agents a corporate card; the unspoken assumption underneath was that each agent already has an identity to attach the card, the budget and the audit trail to. Better Auth is that identity layer — and the fact that its centre of gravity was a single Ethiopian developer, not a Valley team, is the part the partners wouldn't have seen anywhere else. Guillermo Rauch's framing is the tell: “When an agent acts on your behalf, it runs under your identity and access, so every service it touches sees you, not the agent… agent identity is foundational to agentic infrastructure.” The scarce skill in the agent economy is turning out to be revocable, per-agent identity — and it can be written from anywhere.

Launch Base Africa — “Ethiopian Founder Sells AI Authentication Startup to Vercel”

Two threads from this page converge on this deal. Friday's Ben Evans lead argued value accrues to whoever owns the user touchpoint; Monday's Ramp item (which Nic marked up) put a card in an agent's hand and asked who sees the spending data. The answer both were circling is identity: the platform that decides who an agent is, and can switch it off individually without cutting a user's whole digital presence, sits under all of it. Vercel paid — undisclosed, a year after a $5m seed — to own exactly that primitive, and folded it into Vercel Connect and its agent-workflow platform. This is the same “mandate, limit, receipt” standards fight Monday flagged one layer down, now with a named winner on the identity rung.

“When an agent acts on your behalf, every service it touches sees you, not the agent. Agent identity is foundational to agentic infrastructure.”

For a partnership weighing where defensible software gets built, the geography is the thesis. Better Auth answered a demand the incumbents missed — AI startups wanting to self-host authentication and keep proprietary tokens in-house — and it was answered from Addis Ababa, scaling to 4.7m weekly downloads before a US acquirer noticed the exit before the enterprise. It threads to Prembly's identity-infrastructure story and to the “90% collapse” argument that African AI is a rounding error in dollar terms: true of capex, false of authorship. The forward question for us: if the critical layers of the agent stack can be written far from the hubs but are still bought by the hubs, does African deep-tech value keep leaking out at the acquisition, and what would it take — local growth capital, later-stage rounds — to keep one of these on the continent to scale?

South Africa · Fintech · Payments Analysis Hyper-local
Float — the Stellenbosch-region payments platform that lets shoppers split existing credit-card purchases into interest-free instalments — has entered the UK via Britain's Global Entrepreneur Programme, the Innovator-Founder route that skips the old £200,000 investment threshold. Float's model issues no new credit: shoppers pay in up to 24 monthly instalments against the credit line already on their Visa or Mastercard, keep their loyalty points, download no app, and Float earns only merchant fees. In South Africa it has lifted average order values ~134% across 2,200+ retail partners (Samsung, iStore, The North Face, Cycle Lab) and has raised R280m+ (~$17m) from Standard Bank, Remgro's Invenfin, and Platform Investment Partners. Founder Alex Forsyth-Thompson's thesis: “These shoppers don't need more credit. They just need more time.” Because it sits outside new-credit-agreement rules, it dodges — for now — the BNPL regime the UK is consulting on.

“More time, not more credit”: a South African instalment model tests a £250bn British gap

Launch Base Africa · Fintech · Wed 8 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the arbitrage is dormant credit-card headroom, and it is far larger abroad than at home — the same product, pointed at a market ten times the size. And the structural elegance is that Float monetises time, not risk: no underwriting, no new loan, revenue from merchants who see bigger baskets, which is why the FCA can only watch a boundary case it hasn't yet defined. That the backers include Remgro's Invenfin and Standard Bank makes this a Cape ecosystem export worth the partners' notice.

The binding constraint is the repayment window, not the credit limit
Launch Base Africa — “Britain's Founder Visa Alternative Brings SA Fintech Float to the UK”

Monday's Capitec item (Nic: up) described a retail flywheel — scale drives cost down, savings pass back as lower fees, data sharpens the offer — being aimed at South African business banking. Float is the same instinct wearing a payments-rail: use infrastructure that already exists (the card, the credit line, the loyalty programme) and sell the one thing the incumbent won't — here, the timing of repayment rather than the credit itself. It is a distinctly Southern-African read on a universal problem, and it is now being exported rather than imported, which is the direction of travel this page keeps looking for.

“These shoppers don't need more credit. They just need more time.”

The regulatory arbitrage is the fragile part and the interesting part. Float issues no new credit agreement, so it sits outside the consumer-credit rules the UK is tightening around Klarna, Clearpay and Zilch — a card-linked model in a market built for lender-led BNPL. That is a genuine structural edge and a genuine risk: the FCA has said it will keep boundary cases under review, and card issuers whose own instalment products Float competes with control the acceptance it needs. The forward question for us is whether a model forged against South Africa's thin-credit, high-rate conditions travels to a market where the constraint is behavioural rather than structural — and whether a Remgro-and-Standard-Bank-backed Cape company can hold a distribution edge abroad long enough for the rule-writers to catch up.

Nigeria · Governance · Ownership Analysis Thesis test
A Lagos labour court has dismissed a former OPay employee's claim to shares he says were owed under a stock-option scheme — a ruling that lands just as the Nigerian payments giant, having hired Citigroup, Deutsche Bank and JPMorgan in May 2026, prepares a US IPO targeting roughly a $4bn valuation. The case turns on how OPay's employee share plan was documented, vested and communicated — the unglamorous plumbing of who actually owns a fast-scaled company — and it surfaces exactly as external counsel and bankers stress-test the cap table for a New York listing. For a company that grew explosively on the back of Nigeria's cash-crunch years, the dispute is a reminder that equity administered casually on the way up becomes a legal and disclosure problem on the way to public markets.

Who really owns OPay? A staff-share dispute meets a $4bn IPO on the way to New York

Launch Base Africa · Governance · Fri 10 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the substance is organisational design, not litigation — ESOP structuring, vesting mechanics and the paper trail behind them are precisely what converts “we gave people equity” into a defensible cap table, and precisely what an IPO forces into the light. And the timing is the lesson: the same explosive growth that makes OPay listable is what left its ownership plumbing under-built, so the reckoning arrives at the least convenient moment. For anyone holding equity in fast-scaling private companies, it is a live test of whether the boring governance was done early.

Launch Base Africa — “Equity Dispute Puts OPay's Employee Share Scheme Under Scrutiny”

This threads to Monday's consolidation cluster from the opposite end. Gigbanc (Nic: up) is what happens when a company runs out of road at the bottom of the funnel; OPay is what the top of the funnel looks like — a survivor large enough to court Citi, Deutsche and JPMorgan for a $4bn New York float. But both stories are really about the same thing the page has been circling all month: ownership. Who owns the rail, who owns the licence, and here, who owns the shares. The court ruling went OPay's way, which is less interesting than what the case exposes — that the equity a company hands out during a hyper-growth scramble is a governance liability waiting for a liquidity event to make it matter.

“The equity you administer casually on the way up becomes the disclosure you litigate on the way to the exit.”

The read for a private-markets partnership is directly useful. Employee share schemes are a value-creation tool and a diligence landmine in equal measure; the difference is whether vesting, documentation and communication were treated as real corporate infrastructure or as an afterthought bolted on when talent had to be retained cheaply. OPay's timing — the dispute breaking as bankers ready an S-1-equivalent — is the cautionary detail: cap-table hygiene is cheap to do early and expensive to fix under a listing spotlight. The forward question we'd carry into any growth deal: does the ESOP survive contact with a Big-Four audit and a hostile ex-employee, or is it a story management tells that the paperwork doesn't back?

Africa · Venture Capital · Structure Briefing Longitudinal
Launch Base Africa's July deal tracker catalogues the under-the-radar rounds that sit beneath the mega-deals — and one stands out: Morocco's Ora Technologies closed a $2m Series A extension (cumulative $10m) funded entirely by domestic Moroccan private investors, said to be the largest tech Series A backed wholly by local capital. Around it: Kenya's Flowt building cash-flow-data lending for climate-smart SMEs; Rwanda's Eden Care licensing an AI claims-adjudication engine to cut the ~30% fraud in East African health insurance; Kenya's Fuzu turning a talent network into an AI data-operations supply chain. The macro frame is the H1 2026 barbell: $1.44bn raised (up 1.4% year on year) across just 146 disclosed deals, versus 252 in H1 2025 — roughly the same money in far fewer, larger cheques, with the middle of the market hollowed out.

The same money, half the deals: Africa's capital turns lumpy — and increasingly local

Launch Base Africa · Venture Capital · Fri 10 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the barbell is the mechanism behind Monday's Gigbanc wind-down (Nic: up) — capital now skips the middle, so sub-scale companies with heavy compliance costs can't bridge to profitability while scaled names raise bigger. And the quieter signal is composition: a landmark Series A funded entirely by domestic Moroccan investors, echoing the “local investors stage a partial comeback” data from early July, suggests the continent is starting to build the later-stage capital layer whose absence keeps forcing its best companies to sell early or shut.

Fewer companies, bigger cheques — the middle of the market is thinning
Launch Base Africa — “African Startup Deal Tracker — Newest Deals”

The barbell is now a month-long thread on this page: Gigbanc shutting for want of a Series A, Nigeria's startup graveyard becoming a bargain bin for acquirers, and now the raw arithmetic — the same $1.4bn spread across 42% fewer deals, pushing the average cheque up roughly 77%. Capital hasn't left Africa; it has concentrated. That is survivable for scaled names and lethal for the middle, and it is why the acquisition (Better Auth to Vercel, in today's lead) and the wind-down (Gigbanc) are two outcomes of the same funding structure.

“The money didn't leave. It concentrated — and the middle of the market is where the gap opened.”

The more durable signal is under the headline: who is writing the cheques. Ora Technologies raising a $10m Series A entirely from domestic Moroccan investors is not a rounding error — it is the first credible sign of the later-stage local capital layer whose absence has forced African companies to sell early or die. Read alongside the Ghanaian-pension and Moroccan-LP items this page has tracked, a pattern forms: the continent is slowly assembling its own growth capital, deal by deal, exactly where foreign VC retreated. The forward question for a firm like ours: as the middle hollows out and domestic capital rebuilds it unevenly, is the opportunity in the abandoned middle — the profitable, unfashionable companies that can't raise a fashionable round — rather than in the crowded, expensive top?

Pan-Africa · Embedded Finance · SME Credit Briefing Hyper-local
Renew Capital, the pan-African firm operating in 13 countries, has shortlisted 15 companies from 500+ applicants for its first embedded-finance programme, the Renew Venture Lab: EmFi Series. The premise: Africa's SMEs are ~90% of private-sector businesses but face a ~$330bn annual credit shortfall that formal banks have failed to close — so the next lenders will be the digital platforms that already sit on SMEs' sales, inventory and payment data. The 15 finalists span agri-marketplaces, pharmacy supply chains, B2B commerce and logistics across 10 countries (Ghana, Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, Morocco, Senegal, Zambia, Uganda, Ethiopia, Togo) — names like AgroCenta, Oze, Tradevu, Zendawa and Regxta. Co-CEO Matthew Davis: “The next generation of Africa's small business banks won't be banks. They'll be startups that already understand how SMEs operate, have their data and have earned their trust.”

“The next small-business banks won't be banks”: 15 platforms bet on the $330bn credit gap

Launch Base Africa · Embedded Finance · Wed 9 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the embedded-finance thesis stated plainly — distribution and proprietary data, not a banking licence, become the underwriting edge, letting platforms lend against cash-flow trails rather than collateral. And it is a direct counterpoint to Monday's Capitec item (Nic: up): incumbents are pushing down into SME banking from the top while data-rich platforms build up from inside the customer relationship, and both are chasing the same segment traditional banks priced out. The subsidised, donor-and-DFI-backed structure (Tamwilcom, Global Affairs Canada, Norad) is itself a data point on how thin private African growth capital still is.

Launch Base Africa — “Meet the 15 Startups Renew Capital Is Backing to Build Africa's Next Lenders”

Davis's line — the next small-business banks won't be banks — is the same structural claim Monday's Capitec story made from the incumbent side, and TechCabal's “age of lending to strangers is ending” made from the credit-risk side. The common thread is that the underwriting advantage is migrating from balance-sheet and collateral to distribution and data: whoever already sees an SME's daily cash flow — the agri-marketplace, the pharmacy supply chain, the logistics platform — can lend to it more cheaply and safely than a bank that meets it once at the counter. Fifteen companies across ten countries is a spread bet on that migration, and Flowt in today's deal-tracker item (cash-flow lending inside a venture studio) is the same idea funded directly.

“They'll be startups that already understand how SMEs operate, have their data and have earned their trust.”

Two cautions sit under the optimism. First, the programme is underwritten by development-finance and state money — Morocco's Tamwilcom, Global Affairs Canada, Norad — which tells you private capital still won't fund the ecosystem-building layer, echoing today's barbell item. Second, embedded lending works beautifully inside one market's data and regulation and breaks at the border: currency volatility, fragmented rules and credit models that don't travel are exactly why so few of these scale continentally. The forward question for us: is the durable value in the platforms that originate the loans, or in the infrastructure — scoring, capital, compliance — that lets fifteen different platforms lend without each rebuilding a bank? The picks-and-shovels layer under embedded finance may be the better-defended position.

Nigeria · Regulation · Digital Economy Briefing Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Nigeria has suspended new regulatory measures for digital platforms and online intermediaries until a single, harmonised framework exists. Rather than let three overlapping agencies — the Nigerian Communications Commission, NITDA and the Nigeria Data Protection Commission — each layer rules on the same companies, the Federal Ministry of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy has frozen the status quo and stood up a Joint Technical Coordination Committee, drawing in industry, civil society and academia, to draft one coherent governance regime. Minister Bosun Tijani framed regulatory coordination as essential for legal clarity, investment and consumer trust. It is a rare instance of a government choosing to pause its own rule-making to avoid contradiction rather than accumulate it.

Nigeria freezes its own digital rulebook rather than write it three times

Broadcast Media Africa · Regulation · Mon 13 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is regulatory architecture as competitive infrastructure — a market that resolves which agency governs what, before the rules bite, lowers the compliance drag that quietly taxes every platform operating across Nigeria's overlapping mandates. And it is the encouraging inverse of Monday's Ghana 5G item (Nic: up): where Ghana had to reverse a bad infrastructure-design choice after the fact, Nigeria is trying to prevent a bad regulatory-design choice before it's made. Whether a committee actually converges — rather than adding a fourth layer — is the open question.

Broadcast Media Africa — “Nigeria: Govt To Develop Unified Policy Framework For Digital Platform Regulations”

The regulation thread on this page keeps testing one proposition: that in African markets, spectrum and rule-making — not technology — set the pace at which value gets built. Ghana's 5G reversal (Monday, Nic: up) showed the cost of getting infrastructure design wrong; Nigeria's central-bank orders to split payments empires, and its microfinance-licence crackdown, showed a regulator moving fast and hard. This is the same government in a different mode — deliberately slowing itself to coordinate three agencies whose overlapping mandates on data, telecoms and IT had begun to contradict each other. For once the structural instinct is coordination over control, and it's worth marking when a state chooses that.

“Coordination, not control — and chosen before the coverage or the compliance is lost, not after.”

The read for operators and investors is that compliance overhead is a real, if invisible, line item in every African digital business, and it scales with the number of regulators claiming jurisdiction. A single framework, if it actually lands, is a genuine reduction in the cost of doing business across Nigeria's digital economy — the country's most valuable and most fought-over market. The scepticism is warranted: joint technical committees have a habit of producing a fourth document rather than replacing three, and “maintain the status quo while we harmonise” can quietly become permanent. The forward question we'd watch: does the committee ship a binding, simplifying framework on a clock — or does the pause become the policy?

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Make Claude argue against you — the standing sceptic on every thesis

Monday's tip was about setting standing instructions so the assistant starts every session already briefed. Today's is about what to make it do once it is: disagree with you on purpose. The default failure mode of any AI assistant — and of any smart colleague who wants to be liked — is agreeable elaboration: you float a thesis, it finds reasons you're right. That feels productive and quietly narrows your thinking. The fix is a single instruction you give after it has helped you build the case: “Now argue the other side as if your bonus depended on killing this deal.” The model has no ego to bruise and no relationship to protect, which makes it the cheapest red-team you will ever run. Use it the moment you notice you're enjoying the answer — that's usually the signal you've stopped stress-testing it.

A worked example

After it drafts your investment memo: “You are now the most sceptical member of our IC. Write the three-paragraph note that sinks this deal — the strongest bear case, the base rate that says most deals like this fail, and the one piece of diligence we're skipping because we already like the answer. Be specific, cite what would have to be true for the bull case, and don't hedge.” Then read its bear case before you read your own bull case again. If your thesis survives its own sceptic, you're not persuading yourself — you're deciding.
