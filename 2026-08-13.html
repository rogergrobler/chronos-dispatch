South Africa · Ecommerce · Structural Analysis Chart of the day
Shoprite's Sixty60, the group's on-demand grocery-delivery platform, generated R25.5bn ($1.6bn) in sales in the year ended June 2026 — up 34.5%, and growing nearly five times faster than Shoprite's core South African supermarket business, which rose 7.1%. The number sits inside the group's R270.8bn ($16.8bn) of total sales (up 7.2% from R252.7bn) and is already equivalent to about 9.4% of group sales, though Shoprite doesn't report it as a standalone segment. The platform runs off the group's roughly 3,000 stores — its Supermarkets RSA arm (84.5% of group sales) opened a net 262 stores in the year to reach 2,839 corporate outlets — using that physical footprint as ready-made fulfilment infrastructure rather than building standalone dark stores. Internal selling-price inflation was just 0.8% (vs 3.9% national food CPI), like-for-like sales rose 2.0%, and Checkers' brands grew 10% against 4.3% for Shoprite/Usave. Group HEPS from continuing operations is guided up 9.7%–14.7%; full results land 1 September.

Shoprite's Sixty60 is quietly a $1.6bn business growing 5x its stores — a physical network turned into a digital-commerce moat

TechCabal · Ecommerce · Phathisani Moyo · Wed 12 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a strong retail update. The one that matters is what a durable, cash-generative business looks like in a market that has stopped funding possibility (Issue 062, item 1) — Sixty60 didn't raise a growth round, it compounded 34.5% off assets Shoprite already owned, converting 3,000 stores into last-mile infrastructure a pure-play delivery startup could never afford to build. It is the exact inverse of yesterday's MTN lesson: here, owning the asset is the moat, not the liability. The catch is that the number is buried inside brand sales, so its true margin economics stay off-screen.

One unit is pulling the whole group's growth — and it's the one built on infrastructure the group already owned
TechCabal — “Shoprite's Sixty60 is now a $1.6 billion digital commerce business”

The structural read is that the most valuable digital businesses on the continent may not be the venture-funded apps at all — they may be the incumbents that already own the hard, unfashionable, capital-intensive infrastructure and quietly bolt software onto it. Sixty60 is a masterclass: a grocery-delivery operation that would be ruinously expensive to build from zero (real estate, inventory, cold chain, a national footprint) instead runs off 3,000 stores Shoprite already paid for, so every incremental delivery leans on sunk assets rather than fresh capital. That is why it can grow 34.5% while the group grows 7% — and why a pure-play startup burning venture money to build dark stores was always going to struggle against it. For a firm that buys durable, cash-generative businesses, this is the thesis in one data point: proprietary physical infrastructure plus a software layer is a moat that compounds, and it is exactly the kind of asset that gets more valuable when capital is scarce and competitors can't fund the build.

The startup had to raise money to build the network. Shoprite already owned it. In a market that has stopped funding possibility, the incumbent with the infrastructure wins the digital business.

The threads: to Issue 062's item 1 (the funding squeeze) — this is the answer to the question that edition posed: when capital rewards proof over promise, the winners are businesses that can grow without the next cheque, and Sixty60 grew a third larger on assets already on the balance sheet. To yesterday's item 5 (MTN/Iran) and this week's own-versus-orchestrate spine — the clean counter-case: MTN showed owning an asset can import a geopolitical shock, while Shoprite shows owning the right asset — a dense domestic store network — is precisely what makes the digital business defensible; ownership is a question of which asset, not whether. To the Chronos read on durable moats and capital allocation — a hard-to-replicate physical network with a high-growth software layer on top, funded from internal cash, is close to the platonic ideal of what the firm underwrites. The sceptic's ledger: Shoprite pointedly does not break out Sixty60's profitability, so a 34.5% top-line number tells us nothing about whether last-mile grocery delivery actually makes money at scale — it may be buying growth with margin; R25.5bn is impressive but still under 10% of group sales, so this is a promising engine, not yet the business; and grocery delivery is fiercely contested (Checkers Sixty60 vs Woolworths Dash, Pick n Pay asap!, Bolt/Uber), with thin economics everywhere in the world. The forward marker: the 1 September full results — whether Shoprite gives any colour on Sixty60's contribution margin, and whether the 34.5% growth is still accelerating or starting to mature as the base gets large.

Pan-Africa · Venture Capital · Structural Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
TechCabal's Francophone Weekly ran a rare data-driven “where would you deploy $100m in Africa today” analysis, cross-referencing the IMF, World Bank, AfDB, UNCTAD, GSMA, SBM Intelligence and AVCA. The fastest-growing 2026 economies (IMF): Ethiopia 9.2%, Guinea 8.7%, Uganda 7.5%, Rwanda 7.2%, Benin ~7.0%, Côte d'Ivoire 6.2% — Nigeria, the continent's third-largest economy, grows just 4.1% and misses the top ten. On the only regional private-equity return data ever published (EY/AVCA, 2007–15 exits vs MSCI EM): East Africa 2.0x, Southern Africa ex-SA 1.9x, North Africa 1.6x, South Africa and West Africa 1.5x — yet South Africa took 39% of exit volume (the only market with a deep local exchange and acquirer base). Francophone sub-Saharan Africa draws just 10–15% of African startup funding despite ~20% of the population. The piece's core claim: institutional quality, not market size, predicts investability — small well-run states (Mauritius, Rwanda, Botswana, Seychelles) consistently outperform their scale while resource giants (Nigeria, DRC, Angola) underperform theirs.

A rare data map of where Africa's money actually gets made — and the uncomfortable finding that governance beats size

TechCabal · Francophone Weekly · Lina Kacyem · Tue 11 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a country-picking guide. The one that matters is a rigorously-sourced argument for a thesis this firm already holds — that institutional quality, predictable regulation and functioning capital markets matter more than population or GDP rank, and that the smallest, best-governed states are being re-rated faster than their growth alone would justify. It is the natural companion to yesterday's funding-squeeze piece: if capital is now scarce and choosy, this is the map of where the discerning cheque should go. The honest limit: the only hard return data is a decade old, so the framework is directional, not a live scorecard.

East Africa returned the most per dollar; South Africa merely absorbed the most deals — volume and quality are not the same market
TechCabal — “A Francophone investor's guide to Africa's next growth markets”

The structural read is that the single most reliable predictor of whether capital gets a return in Africa is not how big or fast a market is, but how well it is run — and the data assembled here makes that case unusually hard to dismiss. The piece's three-tier framing is useful: a core allocation of institutionally deep, predictable markets (Morocco, Mauritius, Rwanda, Côte d'Ivoire, South Africa); a higher-growth, higher-execution-risk tier (Ethiopia, Senegal, Kenya, Egypt, Uganda), each carrying a specific identifiable fragility — a floating-since-2024 currency, an election-year FDI collapse, a debt restructuring; and a frontier tier (DRC, Guinea, Namibia, Mozambique) that only makes sense as a specific commodity thesis structured around governance gaps rather than in spite of them. The recurring names — Rwanda, Côte d'Ivoire, Mauritius, Morocco — are precisely the states that treated administrative reform and regulatory predictability as strategy, and they are being re-rated faster than their GDP growth alone would imply. That is the whole lesson: the market pays for the reduction of uncertainty, and small states that manufacture certainty out-earn large states that squander it.

Market size is not a substitute for institutional quality. The small, well-governed states keep out-earning the resource giants — and the market is starting to price the difference.

The threads: to Issue 062's item 1 (the funding squeeze) — if capital has turned scarce and evidence-driven, this is the discipline that should govern where it lands: not the biggest TAM, but the deepest institutions and the clearest exit path, which is why South Africa still absorbs 39% of exits despite mediocre relative returns (it's the only place you can reliably sell). To Issue 062's item 2 (Kenya's local ETF) — the same signal from the other side: East Africa's superior relative returns and its home-grown capital-markets deepening are two readings of one story about where quality is compounding. The sceptic's ledger: the only hard return data is the EY/AVCA 2007–15 study — a decade stale, a different capital cycle, and small samples per region — so the “East Africa 2.0x” headline should be read as suggestive, not bankable; ranking countries by IMF growth and World Bank governance indices is a well-worn exercise that rarely survives contact with an actual deal; and “institutional quality beats size” is comfortable orthodoxy that can quietly become an excuse to avoid the hard, large, messy markets (Nigeria, DRC) where genuine mispricing and scale actually live. The forward marker: whether the capital-markets deepening now visible in East Africa and the reform momentum in Côte d'Ivoire and Togo start producing a fresh, post-2020 exit dataset that either confirms or overturns the decade-old return map.

South Africa · Payments · Structural Briefing Structural design
The South African Reserve Bank (SARB) has, for the first time since 1996, withdrawn its recognition of the Payments Association of South Africa (PASA) as the primary management body for the national payment system — taking direct control of regulatory and oversight functions PASA has handled for nearly three decades. The remaining operational functions, including PayShap (SA's instant bank-to-bank rail) and real-time clearing, transition to a new entity, “PayInc,” by 2 September. Card payments, ATM withdrawals and PayShap transfers continue as normal through the handover. The stated aim: make the system more resilient to shocks, better able to absorb fintech innovation, and to let the central bank set standards and manage system-wide risk directly rather than through a self-regulatory middleman. SARB frames it as matching a global trend of central banks becoming more hands-on with the rails that move money.

South Africa's central bank takes the payment rail back into its own hands — 30 years of self-regulation ends

TechCabal Daily · Emmanuel Nwosu · Wed 12 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a governance reshuffle. The one that matters is the central bank asserting direct ownership of the country's core financial rail — the same instinct on display across the continent this week, where the state stops delegating and starts operating the infrastructure its economy runs on. It threads straight from Issue 062's Nigeria tax net: once the payment system is the place value and visibility concentrate, whoever controls it holds real power, and SARB has decided that should be SARB. The open question is execution risk — re-plumbing a 30-year-old settlement architecture is not a press release.

TechCabal Daily — “South Africa's central bank takes direct control of the national payments system”

The structural read is that the payment system has quietly become the most strategically important piece of financial infrastructure a modern state owns, and central banks are moving to control it directly rather than supervise it at arm's length. For 30 years SARB let an industry body (PASA) manage the rules by which banks cleared and settled — a sensible arrangement when payments were a plumbing utility. But payments are no longer plumbing: they are where fintech innovation, systemic risk, real-time data and monetary-policy transmission all converge, and a self-regulatory model staffed by the very banks being regulated looks increasingly ill-suited to that stakes. Pulling oversight in-house and spinning operations into a purpose-built entity (PayInc) is SARB positioning itself to set standards, absorb new entrants, and manage system-wide shocks on its own authority. It is the domestic bookend to this week's cross-border story (item 6): first assert full control of your own rail, then decide which foreign rails you're willing to bridge it to.

Payments stopped being plumbing and became power. After 30 years of letting the industry regulate itself, SARB wants its hands directly on the rail.

The threads: to Issue 062's item 4 (Nigeria's digital tax net) and item 3 (SA's ML border) — the same doctrine, one institution over: the state instrumenting itself directly into the infrastructure where value and data concentrate, whether to tax it, screen it, or now to run it. To today's item 6 (BRICS payment rails) — the two are a matched pair: SARB centralises domestic control in the same fortnight it contemplates plugging that rail into a cross-border network, and you cannot responsibly do the second without the first. To today's item 5 (CBN's crypto sandbox) — two central banks in one edition asserting that the perimeter of the financial system is theirs to draw and operate. To the Chronos read on structural design and regulatory risk — when the rail's governance changes hands, so does the risk profile of every payments and fintech business that runs on it, which is worth tracking for both the resilience upside and the transition-risk downside. The sceptic's ledger: central banks are not famously nimble, and moving a 30-year-old, bank-run settlement architecture into a new state-controlled entity by an early-September deadline is exactly the kind of migration that produces outages and unintended fragility; concentrating oversight in the central bank removes an industry check and raises questions about who now regulates the regulator; and “more resilient and innovation-ready” is the standard justification for every centralisation, which sometimes delivers the opposite. The forward marker: whether the PayInc handover completes cleanly by 2 September without disrupting PayShap or clearing — the first real test of whether SARB can operate the rail as well as it can regulate it.

Kenya · Internet · Regulation Analysis Hyper-local
From 14 August, the Communications Authority of Kenya (CAK) requires every cyber cafe operator to register each customer — name, ID number and the exact times they were online — and to keep detailed session logs for at least three years. The regulator frames it as closing an “identity gap”: if a cybercrime is committed from a shared computer, there should be a paper trail to the person in the chair. It dropped its more contentious 2024 proposals (mandatory CCTV, browsing-history tracking) but operators must still install filters to block illegal sites and get approval before reselling bulk internet. Non-compliance draws fines of at least KES 500,000 ($3,860) or 0.2% of annual turnover. Cyber cafes remain important in Kenya — one of Africa's most expensive data markets — where they subsidise access for those priced out of mobile data. The move is part of Kenya's National Cybersecurity Strategy 2022–2027, marking a shift from “access at all costs” to “access with accountability.”

Kenya turns the corner cyber cafe into a data collector — the state's appetite to see and attribute reaches the humblest rail

TechCabal Daily · Emmanuel Nwosu · Wed 12 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a niche cyber-cafe rule. The one that matters is how far down the stack the digitising state's demand for legibility now reaches — not the payment rail or the border this time, but the shared PC on a corner street, which must now log every user by name and ID or face a crippling fine. It is the same “access with accountability” instinct visible in this week's payment and crypto items, applied to the internet itself. The tension is real and local: in one of Africa's priciest data markets, the cafes that bridge the digital divide are the ones being asked to police it.

TechCabal Daily — “Kenya says cyber cafes must now collect customer data or pay fines”

The structural read is that the digitising African state is extending its demand for identity and attribution all the way to the edge of the network, and the cyber cafe is a revealing place to watch it because it sits at the intersection of access and anonymity. Kenya's logic is a security logic: shared, anonymous computers are a gap in the identity fabric the state is otherwise weaving (Huduma Namba, SIM registration, a national cybersecurity strategy), and closing that gap means conscripting the operator as a record-keeper with a three-year retention duty. That the regulator dropped CCTV and browsing-history tracking shows some restraint, but the direction is unmistakable and continental: the same week SARB takes the payment rail and Nigeria supervises the crypto rail, Kenya reaches for the last unmonitored on-ramp to the internet. The cost lands on small operators in a market where data is already expensive — the businesses bridging the divide are being asked to shoulder the compliance of policing it.

First the border, then the payment rail, now the cyber cafe. The state's map of what it can see keeps expanding — and the cost of accountability lands on the smallest operators.

The threads: to Issue 062's item 3 (SA's ML border) and item 4 (Nigeria's tax net) — the same appetite for legibility and attribution, now reaching the humblest rail; read together, the three show a continental pattern of states insisting that economic and online activity carry a traceable identity. To today's item 3 (SARB) and item 5 (CBN sandbox) — “access with accountability” is the internet-access version of the perimeter-drawing doctrine running through payments and crypto this edition. To the Chronos read on regulatory and structural risk — a state steadily raising the identity-and-compliance burden across every digital touchpoint changes the operating calculus for any consumer-internet or access business in the market, and is worth tracking as a leading indicator of where the surveillance-versus-inclusion line is being drawn. The sceptic's ledger: mandatory identity logging at shared-access points is a genuine privacy and civil-liberties concern in a country with a contested data-protection record, and the three-year retention rule creates honeypots of personal data with little clarity on who secures them; the security rationale (traceable cybercrime) is real but the same infrastructure is trivially repurposed for surveillance; and a KES 500,000 fine is existential for a small operator, so the practical effect may simply be to shutter marginal cafes and widen the divide the state says it wants to bridge. The forward marker: whether the rule meaningfully aids cybercrime enforcement without hollowing out the cyber-cafe layer — and whether Kenya's data-protection regime proves able to secure the identity records it is now compelling thousands of small businesses to keep.

Nigeria · Crypto · Regulation Briefing Longitudinal thread
The Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) has opened a regulatory sandbox to virtual-asset operators, fintechs and financial institutions — Cohort 2 applications run 12–31 August. It has two tracks: a Virtual Asset Service Provider (VASP) track for stablecoin, payment/settlement, custody and wallet firms, and a Data-Enabled Financial Services (non-VASP) track for firms using permission-based data sharing to improve payments, credit and inclusion. The CBN will oversee virtual assets used for payments (including stablecoins); the SEC handles security-like digital assets. It follows President Tinubu's 18 July executive order creating a Virtual Asset Council (CBN chairs; the Nigeria Revenue Service and SEC are vice-chairs), and builds on a March pilot with Flutterwave, Paystack and Juicyway. Context: Nigerians transacted about $92.1bn in crypto between July 2024 and June 2025 (Chainalysis) — Africa's largest market — and the NRS released a virtual-asset tax framework on 3 August. The sandbox is explicitly not a licence.

Having started taxing crypto, Nigeria now builds the supervised on-ramp — the state draws the perimeter, then invites capital inside it

TechCabal · Cryptocurrency · Emmanuel Nwosu · Wed 12 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a regulatory sandbox. The one that matters is the completion of a deliberate sequence — the same fortnight Nigeria published a crypto tax framework and stood up a Virtual Asset Council, its central bank opens the supervised channel through which stablecoin and payment firms can operate inside the rules. It is Issue 062's Kenya-ETF logic in a different asset class: first the state bounds the market, then it builds the vehicle that legitimises domestic participation. For Africa's largest crypto market, formalisation is the story — the risk is that supervision hardens into gatekeeping.

TechCabal — “Nigeria's central bank opens regulatory sandbox to virtual asset firms”

The structural read is that Nigeria is doing something coherent and rarely seen this cleanly: sequencing the full institutionalisation of a market that grew up entirely outside the state's control. In roughly three weeks it has published a tax framework for virtual assets, formalised a cross-agency Virtual Asset Council under an executive order, and now opened a central-bank sandbox that gives stablecoin, payment and custody firms a supervised path to operate. Read as a sequence, it is textbook perimeter-drawing: tax it so the state captures value, coordinate the agencies so oversight doesn't fragment, then build the on-ramp so legitimate operators come inside rather than route around. The prize is real — a $92bn market is too big to leave ungoverned, and formalisation could lower the risk premium on Nigerian fintech and pull stablecoin rails into the regulated economy. The danger is equally real: a sandbox is a gate, and gates can be used to admit incumbents and exclude the disruptive small firms that made Nigeria's crypto market vibrant in the first place.

Tax it, coordinate the agencies, then open the supervised door. Nigeria is institutionalising a $92bn market in three weeks — the question is whether the door is a welcome or a filter.

The threads: to Issue 062's item 4 (Nigeria's digital tax net) and its watchlist note on the deferred crypto-tax story — this is the direct next beat: having wired itself to see and tax the payment trail, the state now builds the channel to supervise the virtual-asset rails feeding it; tax and sandbox are two halves of one formalisation. To Issue 062's item 2 (Kenya's ETF) and Issue 061's Kenya crypto-licensing item — the same continental doctrine, repeated: the state draws a clear perimeter, then the vehicles and supervised channels appear to bring capital onshore under its rules. To today's item 3 (SARB) — two central banks in one edition asserting that the shape and supervision of the financial system is theirs to define. To the Chronos read on regulatory risk and structural design — formalisation cuts both ways for any fintech or digital-asset business: it lowers the existential regulatory tail risk while raising the compliance cost and potentially entrenching incumbents. The sceptic's ledger: a sandbox is explicitly not a licence, and African regulatory sandboxes have a long history of admitting a handful of firms and then stalling, so this may be optics ahead of substance; layering a CBN sandbox on top of an SEC incubation programme and an NRS tax regime risks exactly the multi-agency friction the executive order was meant to remove; and formalising a market often means taming it — the compliance burden could push the nimble, permissionless activity that made Nigeria the continent's crypto leader either offshore or underground. The forward marker: who actually gets admitted when the window closes on 31 August — a broad cohort of genuine builders, or a short list of well-capitalised incumbents — and whether the three new regimes (tax, council, sandbox) operate as one coherent system or three overlapping toll-gates.

South Africa · Payments · Geopolitics Analysis Tests a thesis
The BRICS bloc — Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa and newer members — is discussing whether to link their fast payment systems and sovereign CBDCs for cross-border settlement. Reserve Bank of India governor Sanjay Malhotra said members are exploring how to connect their real-time rails and potentially use central-bank digital currencies to make international payments faster and cheaper. It is early-stage, and a clear blocker is that most members have no live CBDC (only Nigeria's eNaira, Jamaica's Jam-Dex and the Bahamas' Sand Dollar have gone live). South Africa is not starting from scratch: the SARB and BankservAfrica already run a 60-second SA–Zambia cross-border corridor, with more SADC links rolling out, and SARB governor Lesetja Kganyago has consistently argued that interoperable national payment systems are more practical than a common BRICS currency. A functioning network could cut costs for African exporters, fintechs and remittance providers — and reduce reliance on the dollar-centred correspondent-banking system.

BRICS floats bridging its payment rails so money can skip the dollar — South Africa's plumbing could join a bigger network

TechCabal Daily · Emmanuel Nwosu · Wed 12 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a central-bankers' talking shop. The one that matters is the slow construction of payment infrastructure designed to route around the dollar — interoperable national rails, possibly settled in CBDCs, that would let a Johannesburg–Mumbai payment skip correspondent banking entirely. It is the external complement to today's SARB item: having asserted control of its own rail, South Africa weighs which foreign rails to bridge. The thesis it tests is de-dollarisation-by-plumbing rather than by grand currency — and the honest read is that ambition here still runs well ahead of deployment.

TechCabal Daily — “BRICS may be building the world's next payment shortcut”

The structural read is that the most credible form of de-dollarisation is not a new reserve currency but boring interoperability — linking the real-time payment systems that emerging markets have each built domestically, so cross-border value can move between them without passing through the slow, expensive, dollar-denominated correspondent-banking layer. Kganyago's position is the tell: he has repeatedly said a common BRICS currency is impractical, but interoperable national rails are not — and South Africa has already proved the concept with a 60-second SA–Zambia corridor and further SADC links. The CBDC angle is what makes the BRICS framing newsworthy (regulators may prefer centrally-issued, monitorable digital currencies to private stablecoins), but it is also the weakest link, because almost none of the members have a live CBDC to connect. For Africa specifically, the prize is concrete: cheaper trade settlement and remittances, and less exposure to dollar liquidity and sanctions plumbing — which is precisely why it is as much a geopolitical project as a technical one.

The realistic path around the dollar isn't a new currency — it's interoperable plumbing. South Africa has already built a 60-second corridor; BRICS is asking how far the pipes can reach.

The threads: to today's item 3 (SARB takes the domestic rail) — the matched pair of the edition: a central bank asserts full control of its own payment system in the same fortnight it weighs bridging that system into a cross-border network; domestic control is the precondition for credible external interoperability. To Issue 062's item 4 (the payment rail as strategic asset) and item 6 (Nigeria's data-sovereignty push) — the same recognition that the rails money and data run on are geopolitical infrastructure, and that jurisdiction over them is a strategic question, not a procurement one. To the Chronos read on structural design and optionality — a maturing set of African and BRICS cross-border rails is a slow-burn structural shift worth tracking for its effect on trade-finance costs, remittance economics and dollar dependence across the firm's markets. The sceptic's ledger: BRICS payment ambitions are long on communiqué and short on deployment — most members lack a live CBDC, and linking heterogeneous national systems across very different legal and monetary regimes is a multi-year technical and political slog; “skip the dollar” headlines routinely overstate near-term reality, since the dollar's network effects in trade and reserves are not dislodged by a settlement corridor; and CBDCs carry their own surveillance and adoption problems that could slow rather than speed the project. The forward marker: whether SARB's proven SADC corridors expand into genuinely multilateral BRICS interoperability with real volume — or whether, as before, the currency-bloc rhetoric outpaces the far more mundane, and more valuable, work of connecting the pipes.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Feed Claude all the documents at once — the value is in the cross-reference no single file contains

Most people paste one document and ask about it. The bigger lift comes from dropping several related files into a single conversation and asking Claude to reason across them — because the insight you actually want usually lives in the gaps and contradictions between documents, not inside any one of them. Give it the board pack, last quarter's minutes, and the financial model together, then ask: “Where does the board pack contradict the model? What did management promise last quarter that this quarter's numbers don't deliver? What's in the minutes that never made it into the pack?” Claude holds all of them in view at once and surfaces the discrepancy a human reader would only catch by flipping back and forth for an hour. The skill is not summarising a document — it's making the model triangulate between them. It turns a pile of files into a single, reconciled picture, and it is exactly the move that catches the thing everyone else missed.

A worked example

Before an IC meeting, drop in the screening paper, the target's last two sets of management accounts, and the competitor's latest results. Then ask: “Cross-reference these. Where does our thesis rely on an assumption the accounts don't support? Where is the competitor already doing what we're betting the target will do? List every place the three documents disagree.” You'll get a reconciliation, not a summary — and the disagreements are usually the agenda for the meeting.
