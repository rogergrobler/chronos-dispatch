Nigeria · Payments · Cross-border Analysis Wouldn't-have-seen-otherwise
Nigerian fintech Payaza has expanded into Australia and New Zealand after securing full regulatory approvals in both — in New Zealand as an Authorised Money Service Business, in Australia as an Independent Remittance Dealer, Digital Currency Exchange Provider and Virtual Asset Service Provider. The point is the framing CEO Seyi Ebenezer puts on it: rather than routing transactions through correspondent banks, Payaza can now move money through its own infrastructure across the Africa–Asia–Oceania corridors — and, in its own words, is betting that "owning more of that infrastructure, not just connecting to it" is the edge. It caps a busy stretch: ₦6.25 trillion (~$4.17bn) processed in H1 2026, a new commerce platform (Shopaza), and fresh investment-grade credit ratings from Augusto & Co., GCR and DataPro.

Payaza takes the "own the rail" playbook off the continent — to Australia and New Zealand

Techpoint Africa · Victoria Fakiya · Tue 14 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is Friday's Paystack lesson — compete on the rail, not the method — carried across oceans. A Nigerian fintech is not expanding into another African market; it is buying the licences to own remittance and virtual-asset rails in two OECD economies, on the thesis that the durable position is the pipe, wherever the pipe runs. And it reframes what "African fintech expansion" means: the frontier is no longer the next African country but the diaspora-and-trade corridors off the continent, where African businesses already move money and the incumbent rails are slow and dear.

A Lagos fintech buys the pipes in Sydney and Auckland — the rail thesis crosses the equator
Techpoint Africa — “Payaza expands to Australia and New Zealand”

Read this straight against Friday's Paystack item and the escalation is the point. Paystack's move was to stop competing on the payment method and own the collection-and-settlement rail at home; Payaza's is the same instinct with the map redrawn — go and acquire the actual regulatory licences to operate remittance, FX and virtual-asset rails in Australia and New Zealand, so African-linked money moving through those corridors runs on infrastructure Payaza owns rather than correspondent banks it rents. The tell is the CEO's framing: the edge is "owning more of that infrastructure, not just connecting to it." That is the whole thesis of this page, said by an operator, applied trans-continentally.

“Payaza is betting that owning more of that infrastructure, not just connecting to it, will give it an edge in the increasingly competitive global payments market.”

For a firm that assesses African financial-services operators, the read cuts two ways. The bull case is structural: cross-border is where the fees still live — correspondent banking is slow and expensive, African trade-and-diaspora corridors are thick and growing under AfCFTA, and a fintech that holds the licences on both ends of a corridor captures the settlement economics rather than surrendering them. The ₦6.25tn H1 volume and the new investment-grade ratings say this is a real balance sheet, not a press release. The scepticism is equally concrete: licences in four OECD regimes are a compliance burden as much as a moat, Payaza is now competing on incumbents' home turf (Wise, banks, local remitters) rather than in markets where it has structural advantage, and "owning the rail" only pays if volume actually routes onto it — a licence is permission to build, not proof of flow. The forward question we'd carry into any African-fintech case: when an operator says it wants to own the infrastructure end-to-end, is it buying a durable settlement moat, or a stack of regulatory obligations in markets where it has no distribution edge — and which of those is on offer in the deal in front of us?

South Africa · Media · M&A Briefing Structural design
French media group Canal+ has completed its $3bn (R55bn) acquisition of MultiChoice — the South African pay-TV company behind DStv, GOtv and Showmax — after nearly two years of negotiation, taking full ownership of a business with more than 40 million subscribers across ~50 African markets. Two details make it a Chronos item rather than a media headline. First, because South African law limits foreign ownership of broadcasting licences, Canal+ had to restructure MultiChoice's local broadcasting arm before the deal could close — the identical ownership obstacle Amazon routed around a week ago, solved this time by corporate surgery rather than a rented partner. Second, the logic is pure consolidation-for-scale: as streaming compresses traditional pay-TV, Canal+ is buying reach (10,000+ hours of African content a year, dozens of languages) to compete with Netflix, Disney+ and Prime rather than trying to grow into it organically.

Canal+ takes full control of MultiChoice — and restructures SA ownership to clear the door

Techpoint Africa · Victoria Fakiya · Sat 11 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the same South African foreign-ownership rule that shaped Friday's lead, seen from the M&A side — where Amazon rented a licensed operator to avoid restructuring, Canal+ paid $3bn and did the restructuring, because it wanted the whole company, not access to it. Two entrants, one rulebook, two designs. And it is a clean marker of where African media value is heading: consolidation into pan-continental scale, the same instinct running through African banking and telecoms — when organic growth stalls against global platforms, buy the reach.

Techpoint Africa — “DStv owner is now fully owned by Canal+”

Set this beside Friday's Amazon item and the diptych writes itself. Both are foreign giants entering South Africa through the same 30%-style ownership gate; Amazon rented a licensed local operator (Herotel) to avoid touching it, Canal+ spent two years and $3bn buying MultiChoice outright and restructuring the broadcasting arm to satisfy it. The lesson is not that one approach is right — it is that in a regulated market the ownership rule is a fixed feature of the terrain, and the entrant's whole strategy bends around how it chooses to clear it. Rent access if you only need distribution; restructure and buy if you need control of the asset itself.

“Because South African law limits foreign ownership of broadcasting licences, the companies had to restructure parts of MultiChoice's local broadcasting business before the deal could be completed.”

The second thread is consolidation, and it rhymes with the Absa item from Friday and the Prosus item below. African media, like African banking, is being pulled toward scale: Canal+ is betting that 40 million subscribers, a combined content library and lower equipment costs can defend against Netflix and Disney+ in English-speaking markets where DStv stalled. The bull case is real — scale, content and distribution are the only credible moat against global streamers, and Canal+ now has all three in one entity. The scepticism is equally real: pay-TV is a structurally declining format, integration across ~50 markets and multiple regulators is hard, and "buy the reach" only works if the reach retains its subscribers as viewing shifts online. For our purposes the durable read is the ownership-design lesson: any thesis that turns on entering or consolidating a regulated African market has to model the ownership rule as a first-order variable — it decides whether the play is a rental, a restructuring, or a wall.

Nigeria · Regulation · FX Analysis Thesis-test
The Central Bank of Nigeria has directed banks and authorised dealers to report retail foreign-exchange transactions in far greater detail — capturing who receives the dollars, what they are used for, and where the funds ultimately go. It is not a restriction on access; it is a demand for visibility. The CBN wants to curb round-tripping, improve FX-rule compliance and detect suspicious transactions, and the directive slots into a wider build: since 2023 it has collapsed multiple exchange-rate windows, tightened oversight of Bureaux de Change, added AI-powered anti-money-laundering transaction monitoring (March 2026), and set out a Payments System Vision 2028 to modernise the financial plumbing. The bet is explicit — restoring confidence in the FX market depends not only on supply but on oversight, and better data leads to better policy.

Nigeria's central bank moves to trace every retail dollar through the pipes

Techpoint Africa · Victoria Fakiya · Fri 17 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is a direct probe of a thesis this page has been building — that African regulation is moving from vetting who is allowed in, to governing conduct, to now tracing the flows themselves. Friday's Kenya item said a licence governs the door, not the conduct; the CBN's move is the next escalation, reaching past both to the dollar itself. And it is a marker of how thick the compliance-and-data layer is becoming beneath every African operator: AI-powered monitoring, granular reporting, a 2028 payments-modernisation roadmap — infrastructure the state is building under the market, whether the market wants it or not.

Techpoint Africa — “Nigeria's central bank to track every retail dollar”

Run this along the longitudinal thread and the escalation is the whole point. Issue 045 celebrated licensing conjuring a governed lending market out of a vacuum; Issue 046 showed licensing governs the door but not the conduct behind it; today the CBN reaches past both — not who lends, not how they behave, but where every retail dollar physically goes. The regulatory frontier in African finance is moving down the stack: entry → conduct → flow-level surveillance, each layer requiring more data and more infrastructure than the last. The CBN is quite openly rebuilding the plumbing beneath the payments layer — AI transaction monitoring, granular FX reporting, Payments System Vision 2028 — on the thesis that visibility, not just supply, is what restores a currency's credibility.

“Around the world, central banks are using richer transaction data to combat financial crime, improve policymaking and monitor market activity in real time.”

For any African financial-services case the read is double-edged, which is why it is a thesis-test rather than a cheer. The bull side: a state that can see its FX flows in detail is a state slowly rebuilding a functioning market — the compliance layer is a precondition for the confidence that lets capital return, and operators with genuinely clean books and strong reporting infrastructure are advantaged as the requirements tighten. The bear side is just as concrete: every new reporting demand is a cost and a friction, surveillance can shade into capital control, and a central bank that wants to trace every dollar can as easily throttle legitimate flow as catch illicit flow — Nigeria's FX history is not reassuring on the difference. The investable gap, as with the Kenya conduct story, may sit precisely in the layer the rule exposes but does not itself provide: the compliance, monitoring and reporting infrastructure every regulated operator now has to run. The caution worth holding: "the regulator wants more data" is a thesis about the direction of travel, not a dated event — the risk is that the data demand becomes an access constraint dressed as a transparency one.

South Africa · Capital · M&A Briefing Longitudinal
Prosus — the Naspers-controlled technology investor, and one of the most influential capital allocators touching the African tech landscape — has sold its remaining 4% stake in Delivery Hero for about €602m ($703m), ending an investment begun in 2019. The timing is the story: Delivery Hero's shares have climbed on speculation that Uber is weighing a takeover, so Prosus is selling the last of the position into strength, locking the gain and side-stepping the uncertainty of a large merger process. It fits a deliberate rebalancing — having completed its Just Eat Takeaway acquisition earlier this year, Prosus is concentrating on positions where it can play a strategic role, and exiting those where it cannot.

Prosus sells the last of Delivery Hero into an Uber rumour — the discipline of knowing when to leave

Techpoint Africa · Victoria Fakiya · Fri 17 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the exact mirror of Friday's Absa item, and the pairing is the point. Absa looked at a compounding asset it understood and bought more; Prosus looked at a position that had stopped being strategic and sold the last of it into a rumour-driven high. Same discipline — own more of what compounds and matters, own none of what doesn't — pointed in opposite directions. And it is a live read on a capital allocator that shapes the African tech landscape through Naspers: Prosus is signalling concentration over sprawl, strategic control over passive minority stakes.

Techpoint Africa — “Prosus sells final Delivery Hero stake”

Put the two side by side and you have a compact lesson in allocation discipline. Friday's Absa was the "buy more of the compounder you understand better than any outside buyer" move; Prosus here is the "sell the last of the position that no longer earns its place, and do it into strength" move. Neither is about the asset being good or bad in the abstract — both are about strategic fit and marginal return on capital. Prosus first invested in Delivery Hero in 2019, spent years trimming, and having completed its Just Eat Takeaway deal now has a controlled platform in the category; the residual 4% minority stake bought it neither control nor strategic optionality, so when an Uber bid inflated the price, the disciplined move was to exit completely rather than ride someone else's merger.

“For investors, the move suggests Prosus is locking in gains while avoiding the uncertainty that often surrounds large merger talks.”

Why it matters to us beyond the tape: Prosus, through Naspers, is one of the reference capital allocators in the African-linked tech world, and how it behaves is a signal about the discipline the best allocators are applying right now — concentrate where you can be strategic, exit where you are merely a passenger, and use others' M&A enthusiasm as your liquidity. It is also the honest counterweight to the Absa item: "more of a good thing" and "out of a non-strategic thing" are the same decision rule read from both ends. The forward question we'd carry into any portfolio review: which of our positions are strategic compounders worth adding to (the Absa case), and which are passive minorities we hold out of habit that a moment of market enthusiasm would let us exit cleanly (the Prosus case) — and are we as willing to sell the second as to buy the first? The caution: selling into a takeover rumour is easy to celebrate if the bid never comes and looks premature if it does at a premium — discipline and hindsight are not the same thing.

Ghana · Telecoms · Infrastructure Analysis Structural design
Ghana has abandoned its plan to let a single company build the country's 5G network. The government revoked the exclusive licence held by Next Gen Infraco (NGIC) — the appointed wholesale-network builder, partnered with Reliance's Radisys — and is preparing to auction 5G licences to operators, with MTN Ghana and Telecel Ghana already confirmed to bid, an auction expected within weeks. The trigger was delivery: under the single-wholesaler model, only 49 of a targeted 1,200 5G sites had been deployed by March 2026. Ghana is one of the few major African telecom markets still without commercial 5G — GSMA Intelligence puts its potential population coverage near 7% by end-2026 if it launches now, against roughly 22% in Nigeria, 38% in Kenya and 60%-plus in South Africa. The state's new bet: competing owners who build and run their own rails deploy faster than one appointed landlord.

Ghana scraps its single-owner 5G rail and lets MTN and Telecel build their own

Techpoint Africa · Victoria Fakiya · Fri 10 Jul 2026 · developing

Two lines of read: this is Friday's ntel question — who should own the passive rail — answered from the state's side, and answered against the appointed-monopoly model. Ghana tried the tidy version (one wholesale network everyone rents) and got 49 sites out of 1,200; it is now betting that competing operators who own their rails outright will move faster than one landlord with no rival. And it is a rare, clean natural experiment in infrastructure design: shared-utility rail versus competitively-owned rails, with a hard deployment number showing the first model stalling.

Ghana's tidy shared rail stalled at 4% of its site target — so it is handing the build to competing owners
Techpoint Africa — “Ghana opens 5G rollout to MTN and Telecel, ends exclusive model”

Friday's ntel item asked whether the durable position is owning the passive rail or being the scaled consolidator everyone rents from. Ghana just ran the state-level version of the same question and reversed its own answer. The previous government backed a single wholesale 5G network — NGIC, partnered with Reliance's Radisys — precisely to lower infrastructure cost and stop MTN extending its dominance. The tidy theory met an untidy number: 49 of 1,200 targeted sites by March 2026. Faced with that, the regulator concluded that a shared rail with no competitive pressure under-builds, and that competing operators who own their own infrastructure will deploy faster to win customers. So it is auctioning licences and letting MTN and Telecel build in parallel.

“Officials now believe allowing operators to build and run their own 5G networks will accelerate deployment, improve service quality, and encourage more investment.”

The read for any African infrastructure thesis is the trade-off this lays bare, because it is the trade-off we underwrite constantly. A single shared rail is capital-efficient and, in theory, keeps prices down — but it concentrates execution risk in one appointed builder and removes the competitive spur that actually gets sites in the ground. Competitively-owned rails duplicate capex and can entrench the dominant operator (MTN starts ahead), but they align ownership with incentive: you build fast because your rival is building too. Ghana's coverage gap — ~7% against Kenya's 38% and South Africa's 60%-plus — is the cost of having bet on the elegant model and stalled. The forward question we'd carry into any digital-infrastructure case, on the continent or in a portfolio company's build decision: does this asset get deployed faster as a shared monopoly rail or as competitively-owned parallel rails — and is the plan in front of us optimising for capital efficiency or for the competitive pressure that actually ships the network? The caution: this is a developing story — the auction is weeks away, and a competitive scramble can as easily entrench MTN's lead as break it.

Kenya · Telecoms · Governance Briefing Longitudinal
On 31 July, Safaricom shareholders will vote on constitutional amendments that would give Vodafone Kenya — now controlled by South Africa's Vodacom — the right to nominate Safaricom's chief executive for as long as it remains majority shareholder (the board still approves the appointment), plus board-representation rules scaled to ownership. It is the first real test of a new ownership structure: on 30 June, Vodacom completed a ~R35bn ($2.1bn) purchase taking its Safaricom stake from 35% to about 55%, buying out the Kenyan government's 15% and an effective stake from Vodafone Group. The government retains 20%; the rest trades on the Nairobi exchange. Safaricom is not a normal telco — it runs M-Pesa, one of Africa's most valuable payment rails — so the question of who controls its leadership is a question of who controls that rail.

Vodacom took 55% of Safaricom — now it asks for the right to name the CEO

Techpoint Africa · Victoria Fakiya · Fri 10 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this threads Friday's consolidation story forward one step — where Absa bought more equity in its Kenyan bank, Vodacom bought control of Safaricom and is now converting that control into the concrete right to choose who runs it. Ownership is being turned into governance. And it is a live case study in what majority control of a strategic African rail actually buys: not just the dividends, but the leadership, the strategy, and by extension the direction of M-Pesa — set against a Kenyan public and government uneasy about a national asset passing to foreign control.

Techpoint Africa — “Safaricom's new ownership era faces first test”

Friday's Absa item was about a parent buying more of a compounding African subsidiary; this is the next chapter of the same story, in the same region, one layer up. Vodacom has gone from 35% to 55% of Safaricom by buying out the Kenyan government and part of Vodafone Group, and is now asking shareholders to convert that majority into the explicit right to nominate the CEO. That is the difference between owning the cash flows and owning the company: control is worth paying for precisely because it lets you choose the leadership and therefore the strategy. And the strategy here is not incidental — Safaricom runs M-Pesa, the payment rail half of East Africa moves money on, so whoever names the CEO influences the most important financial-infrastructure asset in the region.

“Supporters argue Vodacom's increased control could attract more investment and spur regional growth, while some worry about how decisions on leadership and strategy will change.”

For a firm holding African financial-services and infrastructure assets, the read is about the anatomy of control and its frictions. The consolidation logic is clean — a committed majority owner with deep capital and a pan-African footprint can invest and integrate in ways a fragmented register cannot, which is the same bull case as Absa and Canal+ this week. But Safaricom adds the variable those two lack: it is a national asset with a government still holding 20% and a public that debated whether Kenya gave up too much, which means the "right to name the CEO" is as much a political question as a governance one. The forward question we'd carry into any deal involving a strategic, nationally-sensitive African asset: control on paper is not the same as control in practice — how much of the value depends on governance rights that a government, a regulator or public sentiment can still contest? The caution: majority ownership of a national champion buys the upside and the political exposure in the same transaction, and the 31 July vote is where the second half of that bargain starts to show.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Stop starting from scratch — give a recurring task its own Project

Friday's tip was to hand the model the whole document stack at once. Today's is about not rebuilding the context every time: for anything you do more than once — a company you track, a board you sit on, a fund you report to — create a Project (a persistent workspace) and load it once with the standing material: the mandate, the last few updates, the people, the way you like the output structured. Then every new question starts from that shared base instead of a blank page. Most people paste the same background into a fresh chat each week and wonder why the answers drift; the point of a Project is that the model already knows the account before you ask the question, so you spend your prompt on the new thing, not on re-explaining the old.

A worked example

Set up one Project per portfolio company. Drop in the last three board packs, the investment thesis, the KPI definitions you care about, and a note on how you want briefings laid out. Then, before each board call, all you ask is: “Given everything in this Project, what has moved since the last pack, what are the three numbers I should interrogate, and what did management not address?” You get a briefing grounded in the whole history — not a cold summary of one document — and you never paste the background in again.
