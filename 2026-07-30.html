Pan-Africa · Credit ratings · M&A Analysis Chart of the day
S&P Global has agreed to acquire a majority stake in Agusto & Co., one of Africa's oldest and largest domestic credit-rating agencies, with operations in Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana and Rwanda. Terms were undisclosed; the deal is expected to close in the second half of 2026, and Agusto will keep issuing its own ratings and methodologies. The move mirrors Moody's, which bought 51% of South Africa's Global Credit Ratings (GCR) in 2022 and took full control in 2024. It lands amid intensifying criticism of the "big three" (S&P, Moody's, Fitch): African policymakers argue their methodologies miss local realities and inflate borrowing costs. President Tinubu renewed his call in February (in the FT) for a home-grown African rating agency, noting only three African countries hold investment-grade ratings and citing a 2023 UNDP estimate that rating inefficiencies cost Africa about $75bn a year. The backdrop is real demand: African governments and corporates are turning to domestic debt markets, and Dangote Refinery just completed a $2.5bn private placement, 3.7x oversubscribed.

S&P buys control of Agusto — the global agencies are moving onshore to own Africa's credit verdict

Businessday NG · Economy · Chinwe Michael · Wed 29 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the story is not one acquisition but a pattern — after Moody's took GCR, S&P taking Agusto means two of the big three now own a controlling local presence in African ratings, buying the layer that prices every domestic bond rather than renting judgement from New York. And the timing is the tell: it arrives exactly as Tinubu argues Africa should build its own agency to escape a $75bn "premium," so the same week frames both the problem (foreign agencies price African risk) and the incumbents' answer (buy the local agency instead of letting a rival be built).

Two of the big three now own a controlling African agency — and the "Africa premium" is the prize they are pricing
Businessday NG — “S&P Global's Agusto deal signals race for Africa's credit market”

The structural read is that ratings are infrastructure, and the global agencies have decided the cheapest way to own the African version is to buy the incumbent rather than build or defend from a distance. Agusto is more than three decades old and rates banks, corporates, insurers and structured deals across four markets; S&P is not buying revenue so much as a distribution network and a local information advantage that a New York desk relying on consultants cannot replicate. That is the same "own the rail every transaction crosses" logic that ran through last week's page — Vodacom over M-PESA, Zedcrest over Leatherback — applied to the rail that prices debt. Whoever owns the trusted local verdict sits upstream of every domestic bond issue on the continent.

A credit rating is a verdict on who can be trusted with money. Two of the three firms that issue Africa's verdicts now own the local court.

The threads: to today's Unitel and yesterday's Airtel items (item 2, Issue 054 item 2) — deeper domestic debt and equity markets are exactly what a stronger local ratings layer is meant to unlock, so this is the plumbing beneath the listings. To Issue 054's ATLAS Umoja "digital sovereignty" card (item 4): Tinubu's call for an African-owned agency is the financial-sovereignty version of the same argument — own the judgement layer, or keep paying a premium set by others — and S&P's move is the incumbents pre-empting exactly that. The sceptic's ledger: an independent domestic agency now majority-owned by a big-three global is a governance paradox — the thing Africa is asked to trust as "local" now answers to New York, and "Agusto keeps its own methodologies" is a promise, not a structure; the $75bn UNDP figure is a modelled counterfactual, not a measured cost; and better local ratings raise transparency but do nothing about the macro fundamentals that actually drive the premium. The forward marker: whether Tinubu's continental agency gets real funding now that the two obvious acquisition targets (GCR, Agusto) have been taken off the board — or whether the onshoring wave quietly ends the "African alternative" before it launches.

Angola · Capital markets · Cyber / operational risk Briefing Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Unitel, Angola's largest telecoms operator with more than 21m customers, detected a cyberattack at 02:20 local time on Tuesday 28 July that disrupted voice, mobile data and internet nationwide — roughly one day before its shares were due to begin trading on the Angola Debt and Securities Exchange (BODIVA). The company declined to detail the nature of the attack or attribution and did not say when services would be fully restored. The listing followed an oversubscribed IPO in which the government sold a 15% stake (7.5m shares) at 40,040 kwanzas — the top of the range — raising about 300bn kwanzas ($327–329m) from more than 11,000 investors, oversubscribed by roughly 21%. Despite the attack, neither BODIVA nor Angola's Capital Markets Commission announced a delay, and Unitel listed on Wednesday 29 July, becoming the first non-financial company on the exchange and a milestone in President Lourenço's PROPRIV privatisation programme.

Angola lists its national telco at home — oversubscribed, and hit by a cyberattack hours before the bell

Developing Telecoms · Operators · Manny Pham · Wed 29 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the headline is the cyberattack, but the more important fact is that it did not stop the listing — a frontier exchange most investors have never priced absorbed a $329m sale, oversubscribed by a fifth, with 11,000 buyers, the day after its flagship issuer went dark. That is the counter-image to yesterday's Airtel item: where Airtel fled a deep-but-timid London, Angola manufactured demand at home. The sting in the tail is operational, not financial — a company whose core asset is uptime revealed, on its debut, that it can be switched off by someone it can't yet name.

Developing Telecoms — “Angola's Unitel suffers cyberattack ahead of stock market debut”

Two things sit inside one event, and they pull in opposite directions. The capital-markets signal is genuinely encouraging for anyone who believes African assets should be financed on African exchanges: BODIVA took its first non-financial listing, priced at the top of the range, and cleared with heavy retail participation — evidence that domestic demand exists when the state is willing to sell a recognisable name into it. That is precisely the "deepen local capital markets" thesis that makes today's S&P–Agusto item (item 1) matter, and the mirror of yesterday's Airtel-to-London card (Issue 054 item 2): the question of where an African champion can be priced got a home-market answer this week, and the answer was "here, and oversubscribed."

A telco's product is uptime. Unitel's debut proved it can raise a third of a billion dollars and be switched off by a stranger in the same 24 hours.

The operational-risk signal cuts the other way, and it is the one a private-equity reader should not wave past. A dominant telco is critical national infrastructure; that it could be disrupted nationwide, at will, on the eve of the most scrutinised day in its history, is a governance and resilience flag that belongs in any diligence on African connectivity or fintech assets riding those rails. The threads: to Issue 054's SARS card (item 6) and today's Bingo item (item 6) — the same digital-integration that delivers efficiency and enforcement also concentrates single points of failure, and the more of the economy that runs through one operator's stack, the larger the blast radius when it goes down. The sceptic's ledger: attribution is unknown, so this could be criminal, hacktivist or state-adjacent, and the response differs sharply by which; "oversubscribed by 21%" on a government-set price in a captive domestic market is not the same demand signal as a competitive book-build; and a milestone first listing is a thin base from which to call BODIVA a functioning market. The forward marker: whether the attack surfaces in Unitel's post-listing disclosures and pricing — and whether it chills or, perversely, accelerates the PROPRIV pipeline behind it.

Lesotho · Mobile money · Financial inclusion Briefing Longitudinal thread
VCL Financial Services, the operator of M-PESA in Lesotho, has launched Tsetela (Sesotho for "to invest"), a mobile-based investment product built with asset manager STANLIB Lesotho and unveiled in Maseru on 24 July. Eligible individual M-PESA customers and Mokhatlo group-savings members can invest directly from their wallets, earn daily interest, check balances over USSD (dial *200#) and request withdrawals. Individuals can start from M50 (about $3) and group-savings members from M300 — against traditional channels that STANLIB says can require M5,000 minimums plus a bank account. STANLIB is licensed by the Central Bank of Lesotho; investor funds are segregated, operations are annually audited and an independent trustee oversees compliance. Vodacom Lesotho framed it as moving digital finance "beyond basic access towards meaningful usage," with plans to widen the portfolio range as the product matures.

M-PESA in Lesotho goes from moving money to managing it — regulated investing from $3, over USSD

TechAfrica News · Fintech · Fri 24 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is yesterday's Vodacom story taken one rung up the value chain — the group that now earns 22% of revenue from financial services isn't just processing payments, it's beginning to gather and manage savings, which is where the durable, higher-margin relationship in banking actually lives. And the mechanism matters more than the market's size: a M50 minimum delivered over USSD, on a wallet already trusted in rural Lesotho, is a distribution advantage no traditional asset manager can match — STANLIB brings the licence and the audit, Vodacom brings the 22m-wallet reach.

TechAfrica News — “Vodacom Lesotho launches Tsetela investment service on M-Pesa”

The reason this clears the bar is the thread, and what it says about where telco-fintech goes next. Yesterday's page established that Vodacom has become a financial-services company; Tsetela shows the direction of travel from here — from transactions (fee income on flow) toward intermediation (managing the customer's balance). Savings and investment are the stickiest, highest-lifetime-value products in retail finance, and the operator that already sits in the customer's pocket, over a channel that works on any handset, has a structurally cheaper path to them than any bank branch or app. The STANLIB partnership is the template: the licensed asset manager supplies the regulated wrapper and the trustee; the telco supplies the distribution and the trust. It is the "own the whole flow" instinct again, but the flow being captured is now the customer's stored wealth, not just their spending.

The payment was the wedge. The savings balance is the business — and the telco that owns the wallet is the cheapest distributor of it on the continent.

The threads: straight to Issue 054's Vodacom lead (item 1) — the 22%-of-revenue financial-services shift is exactly this, extended from Kenya's M-PESA to Lesotho's, and from payments into savings. To Issue 053's payments-participation essay: Tsetela is a participation play at the wealth layer — not who can transact, but who can invest — answered with a $3 minimum. The sceptic's ledger: Lesotho is tiny, so this is a template to watch rather than a needle-mover, and daily-interest cash-management products carry thin margins that only work at M-PESA scale; "regulated" is doing reassurance work, but placing investment risk in front of first-time, risk-averse savers over USSD is a mis-selling and suitability question regulators across the region will eventually have to price; and STANLIB, not Vodacom, holds the licence, so the economics split in ways the press release doesn't disclose. The forward marker: whether Vodacom rolls the STANLIB-style savings wrapper across its larger M-PESA markets (Tanzania, DRC, Kenya) — that, not Lesotho, is where "telco becomes asset manager" would actually move the Vision 2030 maths.

Africa · Startups · Gender & capital Briefing Longitudinal thread
The third edition of Disrupt Africa's "Diversity Dividend: Exploring Gender Equality in the African Tech Ecosystem" (with Madica, Thinkroom and Jumpstarter) finds representation slowly rising but funding going backwards. Of more than 3,000 startups sampled, 641 — 19.2% — have at least one female co-founder, up from 17.3% in 2024 and 14.6% in 2023; female-CEO-led startups rose to 12.1% from 11.1% and 9.6%. But capital has diverged: the share of funded startups with a female co-founder fell from 26.3% (2023) to 18.5% (2024) to 16.9% (2025), and female-CEO-led funded startups fell from 15.3% to 12.5% to 9.6%. In the first five months of 2026, 11 of 60 funded startups (18.3%) had a female co-founder and five (8.3%) a female CEO. Disrupt Africa's Gabriella Mulligan called the funding figures "a concern"; Thinkroom's Catherine Young called the gap "commercially irrational."

African women are founding more companies and getting a smaller slice of the money — the lines have crossed

Disrupt Africa · Research · Tom Jackson · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the striking finding isn't the slow rise in representation, it's the crossover — in 2023 female-co-founded startups were over-represented among the funded (26% of cheques vs 15% of companies); by 2025 they were under-represented (17% of cheques vs 19% of companies). As total funding thins and concentrates, the allocation is tightening around a narrower profile of founder. And the framing to keep is Thinkroom's: this is presented not as a fairness problem but a returns problem — "diversity is not lowering the bar, it is widening the field so excellence is less likely to be missed."

More women founding, a thinner slice of the funding — as capital concentrates, it narrows the founder it backs
Disrupt Africa — “Small steps towards greater gender diversity in African startups, says new report”

The reason this is worth a card for an investor, not just an ESG note, is that it is a clean data point on how capital behaves when it gets scarce — and the answer is that it narrows the profile it will back. Read alongside the fortnight's funding thread (Issue 053–054: total capital holding but deal count and the small-cheque band collapsing), this report supplies the distributional detail: the concentration isn't neutral. As the number of cheques falls, the ones that disappear first are disproportionately those written to founders who already sat furthest from the networks that allocate capital. Representation kept climbing because founding a company is something a founder controls; funding fell because who gets funded is something the allocator controls — and allocators, under pressure, revert to pattern-matching.

Representation is what founders decide. Funding is what allocators decide. When money tightens, the second number is the one that tells you what the market actually believes.

The threads: to Issue 054's Fincart card (item 5) and the whole funding-concentration thread — this is the same "base is thinning" story resolved to a finer grain, showing the thinning is uneven. To the "commercially irrational" framing from Thinkroom: if the diverging line represents mispriced founders rather than lower-quality ones, it is, in principle, an edge — a pool of companies the market is systematically under-backing. The sceptic's ledger: the sample is self-selected and small (60 funded startups in five months of 2026 is a thin base for a trend), and "female co-founder" spans a wide range of actual roles and control; representation gains that don't reach the CEO line (12.1%) may overstate real shifts in power; and the funding gap has plausible composition effects — sector mix, stage mix, cheque size — that a headline percentage hides. The forward marker: whether 2026 full-year data confirms the crossover as a trend or a wobble — and whether any fund treats the gap as an opportunity to price rather than a statistic to lament.

Madagascar · Critical minerals · Geopolitics Briefing Tests a thesis
The US is backing the roughly $150m Ampasindava rare-earths project in Madagascar as part of a State Department strategy to loosen China's grip on critical-minerals supply chains, a spokesperson told Reuters, describing the aim as countering "opaque, predatory investments from our adversaries." London-listed Harena Rare Earths said the US International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) committed up to $4.48m for pilot-plant work, lab testing and environmental programmes — modest, but a potential door to larger backing. The ionic-clay deposit is rich in neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium and terbium — the elements behind permanent magnets used in EVs, wind turbines, electronics and defence systems (fighter jets, precision-guided missiles). Harena targets ~4,000 t/yr of rare-earth oxides (including 1,700 t of high-value magnet materials), is seeking an exploitation permit, aims to begin production by mid-2028, and is weighing US/European refining partners (MP Materials, USA Rare Earths, Solvay). China dominates global rare-earth mining and processing and has used export controls to press its advantage.

The US bets on a Madagascar rare-earths project — Africa becomes contested ground for the inputs of the AI economy

Mining Weekly / Reuters · Rare Earths · Tue 28 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the dollar figure is small, but the signal isn't — the US development-finance arm planting a flag in African rare earths marks a shift in a sector Chinese capital has owned (copper, cobalt, lithium), and it reframes Africa as strategic ground in the great-power contest over the physical inputs of electrification, defence and AI hardware. And it tests a thesis worth holding: as software and models commoditise, does durable advantage migrate back down to the things that can't be cloned — the deposits, the processing, the magnets — where geography, permits and capital, not code, set the moat?

Mining Weekly / Reuters — “US backs Madagascar rare earths project in push to loosen China's supply chain grip”

The strategic read is that this is the hard-asset counterpoint to the whole AI-software story the Dispatch has been tracking. Everything above the metal — models, language layers, enterprise deployments — is getting cheaper and more contestable; the magnets that turn electricity into motion in an EV, a turbine or a missile are the opposite, gated by geology, processing know-how (which China dominates far more than mining) and a decade of permitting and capital. Washington committing even $4.48m through the DFC is less about the sum than the posture: the US treats African critical minerals as a theatre it can no longer cede, and Harena's willingness to route processing through MP Materials or Solvay signals that the West wants the refining, not just the ore — because processing, not extraction, is where China's real chokehold sits.

The models are getting cheaper by the month. The magnets that move the physical world are gated by geology, chemistry and a decade of permits — that asymmetry is the whole game.

The threads: to Issue 054's ATLAS Umoja "digital sovereignty" card (item 4) and today's S&P–Agusto item (item 1) — three variants of the same 2026 theme, the contest to own a strategic layer: the language models, the credit verdict, and now the minerals. Sovereignty is the through-line, and Africa is where all three are being fought over at once. The sceptic's ledger: $4.48m of pilot funding is a foot in the door, not a supply chain — the project needs an exploitation permit, ~$150m of capital and until mid-2028 to produce anything, and rare-earth developments have a long history of slipping; the "counter-China" framing can inflate a junior miner's story well beyond its fundamentals; and Madagascar carries real political, environmental and governance risk that a State Department press line glosses. The forward marker: whether the DFC follows the pilot with construction-scale finance and a firm Western refining offtake — the point at which "flag-planting" becomes an actual alternative to Chinese processing — versus another announced project that never clears its permit.

Kenya · Electric mobility · Embedded finance Analysis Organisational design
California-based Bingo Technologies has opened pre-orders in Kenya for the Bingo E2, an EV built for Nairobi's ride-hailing and delivery drivers (a market of 500,000+ daily rides). Its headline feature is a dual-battery system — a fixed 31 kWh pack plus four swappable batteries (13 kWh) that can be exchanged in under two minutes — for a combined ~440 km range, with running costs targeted under $0.02/km. But the more telling product is the software: Bingo is launching Cloud Fleet OS, letting fleet owners anywhere buy an E2, assign it to a vetted local operator, and track vehicle status, operator performance and lease revenue in real time (7,500+ devices already connected). The car runs an AI system, MAYA, that handles remote immobilisation when a driver stops paying, automated payment enforcement and maintenance tracking. Founder/CEO Daniel Huang (who founded phone-accessory maker Mophie) is backed by Trucks VC, Delta 40 and family offices; the E2 is priced around KES 1.8m, shipping Q4 2026.

A US startup sells Nairobi drivers an EV — but the real product is the AI that repossesses it if they miss a payment

Techweez · Automotive · Caleb Sama · Tue 28 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the vehicle is the wedge; the business is the stack around it — Bingo bundles the asset, the battery-swap network, an embedded lease and an AI enforcement layer into one owned system, so it captures the driver's financing, energy and uptime, not just a one-off sale. And the enforcement mechanism is the part to sit with: MAYA can remotely disable the car for non-payment, which is exactly what makes lending against a moving asset to a thin-file driver underwritable — and exactly the kind of automated control that turns a mobility product into a question about who holds the kill-switch on someone's livelihood.

Techweez — “Bingo unveils E2 electric vehicle with swappable batteries and 440 km range”

The organisational-design read is that Bingo is not really an EV company; it is a fleet-financing and enforcement platform that happens to ship a car. Cloud Fleet OS is the giveaway — a global owner buys the vehicle, a vetted local operator drives it, and Bingo sits in the middle owning the ledger: the lease, the battery-swap energy sale, the telematics, the maintenance and the repayment. That is the "own the whole flow" instinct the Dispatch has tracked through Vodacom and Zedcrest, executed on a physical, income-producing asset. The unlock is MAYA: lending against a car driven all day by a first-time borrower is only underwritable if you can guarantee recovery, and remote immobilisation converts an unsecured-feeling loan into a secured one. This is the same architecture as SARS and Absa from yesterday's page — integrate the data, let an algorithm make the enforcement decision — now pointed at a household's means of earning.

The battery swaps in two minutes; the loan enforces itself in real time. Bingo isn't selling a car — it's selling a repayment guarantee with wheels.

The threads: to Issue 054's Absa and SARS AI items (items 3 and 6) — automated enforcement moving from the bank back-office and the tax authority into a privately-owned physical asset, with far less oversight than either public institution faces. To Issue 053's people-and-participation threads: this both widens participation (a driver who could never buy a car now can) and deepens dependence (that access comes with a lender who can switch the asset off). The sceptic's ledger: "AI" here is doing marketing work over what is, at core, telematics plus a remote kill-switch and a lease book — useful, but not novel; battery-swap economics live or die on swap-station density that doesn't exist yet in Nairobi, so the 2-minute promise is aspirational at launch; the range, cost and 7,500-device figures are all company-supplied and unaudited; and the repossession model carries real consumer-protection and reputational risk in a market with active fintech-lending scrutiny (see Issue 053's Kenyan court items). The forward marker: whether Bingo actually ships and builds the swap network in Q4 — and whether an African regulator treats "remote immobilisation for non-payment" as legitimate security or as a consumer-protection problem, the same question now circling digital lenders.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Get the bad draft out first — use Claude to break the blank page, then spend your time editing, not starting

The most expensive part of most writing is not the writing — it is the staring. A board note, an LP update, a difficult reply you've been circling for two days: the cost is the activation energy of the first sentence. The move is to stop treating the first draft as something you have to produce and start treating it as something you react to. Give Claude the raw material — the bullet points, the context, the tone, the length — and ask explicitly for a deliberately rough first pass: "Don't polish this. Get a complete, ugly draft on the page so I have something to fix." A finished-looking draft invites you to tinker at the sentence level; an openly rough one invites you to think at the argument level, which is where your actual judgement adds value. You will almost always find that reacting to a wrong draft — "no, the real point is X, and the second half is throat-clearing" — surfaces what you think faster than a blank page ever does. The draft is scaffolding, not output; its job is to be replaced.

A worked example

Stuck on a portfolio-company note you've been avoiding: "Here are six messy facts and my rough gut view. Write a deliberately rough 250-word first draft of a note to the board — don't make it good, just make it complete, so I can tell you what's wrong with it." Read it once, and you'll react instantly: the opening buries the lede, paragraph three is the real argument, the recommendation is too soft. Those three reactions — which you couldn't summon against a blank page — are your actual note. You wrote it by editing, not by starting.
