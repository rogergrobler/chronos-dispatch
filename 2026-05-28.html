Africa & EM · SARB Monetary Policy Today 15:00 SAST
The SARB MPC delivers its rate decision this afternoon — the first live test of the new 3% inflation target since April CPI surged to 4.0% (from 3.1% in March) on Middle East-driven fuel pass-through. Three respected SA macro houses now see a pre-emptive 25bp hike to prime 11.50%. The structural story under the headline: Kganyago is trading short-term household pain for credibility on the lower target, in a year where every African central bank's anti-inflation posture is being repriced by foreign LPs.

SARB rate decision today — first big test of the new 3% target

Given Majola · The Star (Independent Media Property) · Tue 26 May 2026

The MPC announces at ~15:00 SAST. April CPI of 4.0% is the highest reading in 19 months and breaches the upper bound of the 3% ± 1pp tolerance band. Investec, REMAX and Future Forex all flag a pre-emptive 25bp hike — prime moves to 11.50%, adding roughly R165/month to a R1m bond, R330 to R2m, R495 to R3m. Counter-argument from Seeff: this is a Middle East oil shock plus an annual Eskom hike, both short-term, and a hike now would compound household stress without anchoring a real second-round risk. Kganyago has said publicly the committee must "keep options open" with inflation expectations still above target. The decision will frame the H2 path for credit-extension growth across Capitec, FirstRand, Standard Bank — and read directly into Chronos's discussions with Singapore and GCC LPs on emerging-market central bank credibility.

The Star — Given Majola

The bigger pattern here is the cost of credibility. SARB's new 3% target — half a percent below the old 4.5% midpoint — was always going to be tested early. The Middle East oil shock has given it that test on the schedule it didn't choose. The fuel-pass-through that pushed April CPI to 4.0% is, on the technicals, a one-off pulse. The hawkish case for hiking is that even one-off pulses become permanent if the central bank tolerates them, because expectations re-anchor higher. The dovish case is that the SA household is already so credit-strained that another 25bp pre-empts an inflation risk that wouldn't have materialised, and pays the price in growth.

For Chronos and the wider portfolio, the read is asymmetric. A hike confirms that the new target is a real constraint — which strengthens the long-term credibility story we tell LPs about SA macro discipline, but tightens credit conditions for the next two quarters. A hold says the target is flexible — which loosens credit short-term but invites LP scepticism that the SARB has the spine for the new regime.

"By signaling a potential, defensive interest rate hike, the SARB reinforces the mechanics of the emerging-market carry trade. Maintaining a robust yield differential between South African interest rates and those of advanced economies — particularly a volatile US Dollar — is essential." — Harry Scherzer, CEO of Future Forex

The longitudinal thread: Issue 008's Howard Marks "calibrated uncertainty" piece warned about central banks losing the discipline to defend new targets under political pressure. Issue 009's SARB stablecoin sandbox showed SARB extending the regulatory perimeter on credibility. Today's decision is the live test of whether the inflation-target half of that credibility holds when the price is household pain. Watch the wording of the statement — particularly any reference to the timeline for returning inflation to 3% — more than the headline number.

For the rain board specifically: a hike compresses consumer disposable income at the moment rain is pricing its mid-tier 5G plan. For Optasia's airtime-credit book: hike feeds straight into default risk on the marginal user. For TymeBank: a hike improves NIM on the funded book but slows the rate of new account-funding. Each of these reads will firm up by 18:00 SAST tonight.

Africa & EM · SA Banking Structural
Standard Bank closed Friday 22 May at a R517bn market cap, overtaking Capitec (R511bn) and FirstRand (R503bn) to become the most valuable bank in Africa. The reorder is not random — it's been compounding since Standard Bank's Capital Markets Day on 26 March, where management committed to a continent-wide pan-African operating model rather than the SA-anchored federation structure. The first hyper-local structural read in the SA banking hierarchy in nearly a decade. For comparison: FirstRand traded at a 35% premium to Standard Bank as recently as 2023.

Standard Bank at R517bn — the SA Big Four hierarchy reorders

Adriaan Kruger · Moneyweb · Mon 25 May 2026
SA Big Four market cap — the Feb-to-May reorder, Standard Bank up 17.4% over the period

At Friday's close, Standard Bank's R517bn market cap edged ahead of Capitec (R511bn) and FirstRand (R503bn) — the first time it has held the top spot since 2017. The compounding move dates to the 26 March Capital Markets Day, when CEO Sim Tshabalala committed to a pan-African operating model that explicitly cross-subsidises the SA franchise with African earnings rather than the prior SA-anchored federation model. The market read: SA bank valuations now have to price in continental optionality rather than purely domestic credit growth. The Big Four pecking order is also losing its meaning — Capitec, the disruptor, and FirstRand, the previous leader, now sit within R14bn of each other, all within 3% of Standard Bank's number.

Moneyweb — Adriaan Kruger

The compositional story is more important than the headline. For the last decade, the SA banking valuation hierarchy has been a proxy for two debates: (1) "is Capitec going to keep eating the consumer bank market" and (2) "is FirstRand going to keep extracting the highest ROE in the system from its diversified federation". Both debates produced premia: Capitec for disruption, FirstRand for capital discipline. The reorder this month suggests both premia are now considered priced — and the new premium is being assigned to continental scale rather than domestic operating discipline.

Standard Bank's own commentary frames this honestly. The Capital Markets Day made the pan-African pivot explicit, with management committing to capital-allocation rules that explicitly favour higher-growth African markets over a marginal incremental rand of SA-focused capital. The recent Equity-Group / KCB / NCBA / Ecobank competitive dynamics in East and West Africa give Standard Bank a clearer relative-positioning story than it has had in years.

"Standard Bank's complete outperformance in the last two months can be traced to its Capital Markets Day on 26 March; since then, the stock has been on a tear." — Moneyweb, 25 May 2026

For Chronos: this is the structural backdrop against which the TymeBank international expansion and Optasia's pan-African telco partnerships now sit. The market is paying for continental scale; we hold positions in the smaller, more agile category of operators that can deliver that scale faster than a Big Four bank. The TymeBank-Optasia-Crossfin triangle is the read-across — three operators each with credible pan-African positioning, none yet repriced for the new market premium that Standard Bank's repricing just confirmed.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 002's TymeBank FT placement positioned TymeBank as the international scale story. Issue 004's Rank Capital piece reinforced the African fintech scale theme. Issue 009's $887M H1 VC chart showed the capital flowing to fewer, larger operators. Issue 010's IHS Towers piece showed the consolidation in telecoms infrastructure. Today's piece confirms the same dynamic in banking: scale is being repriced, and the SA banking hierarchy is no longer about who serves the SA consumer best — it's about who delivers continental scale to the continent's investors.

Africa & EM · AI Sovereignty
Rest of World's Ananya Bhattacharya published a deeply reported piece on 21 May arguing that Africa's $60bn AI Sovereignty plan still runs through 12,000 Nvidia GPUs, Microsoft data centre partnerships, and Google Cloud credits. The continent that wants AI sovereignty is the same continent with less than 1% of global data centre capacity. South Africa's draft national AI policy was withdrawn in April after the AI tools used to help write it generated fake citations. This is the structural backdrop for every AI-deployment story we underwrite on the continent.

Africa's $60bn AI Sovereignty plan still runs through 12,000 Nvidia GPUs

Ananya Bhattacharya · Rest of World · Thu 21 May 2026

Africa's four biggest tech economies — South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria, Egypt — have each drafted AI strategies admitting they depend too heavily on Google, Microsoft, Nvidia and Meta for infrastructure and want more control over the terms. Africans comprise 18% of the world's population but less than 1% of global data centre capacity (WEF). The top five African markets combined have less compute than France had in 2024 (McKinsey). Cassava Tech, founded by Strive Masiyiwa, launched Africa's first AI factory in South Africa with Nvidia in March, with 12,000 GPUs to follow across Egypt, Nigeria, Kenya and Morocco. Microsoft's $1bn data centre with G42 Kenya has stalled after the government held back on the compute purchase commitments demanded. Ghana, Nigeria and Zambia have rejected US-linked health data-sharing agreements that would move citizens' data offshore. The April 2026 South African draft AI policy was withdrawn after AI tools generated fake citations during its drafting. The framing matters: this is dependency through the front door, and the next two years of African AI deployment economics are going to be a negotiation over "meaningful control" rather than "complete sovereignty".

Rest of World — Ananya Bhattacharya

The piece's most disciplined framing is from Hilda Barasa at the Tony Blair Institute in Nairobi: African AI sovereignty needs a regional approach because no single country has the workload to justify building alone. The honest version: continental sovereignty is achievable; national sovereignty is not. That has direct implications for any deal Chronos underwrites where the value-creation thesis is "AI on African data". The infrastructure beneath that thesis is, today, Cassava + Nvidia in Johannesburg, or Microsoft data centres in Nairobi (when they actually get built), or G42 Egypt. The "data stays in country, processing happens abroad" segmented architecture that Adeola Bojuwoye at the Digital Impact Alliance describes is the realistic interim — and the cost-of-egress mechanics on that architecture are what determines whether an Africa-AI-deployment story has unit economics or not.

The Anthropic-Rwanda MOU framing in the piece is sharp. Ayantola Alayange at the Global Center on AI Governance puts it bluntly: "Anthropic is creating a very nice, low-hanging fruit way for somebody to absorb the cost of adoption for them. Just imagine the number of people that will be forced to use that technology." The read is not anti-Anthropic — it's a discipline on how to think about US AI labs landing in African public-sector deployments. The training-data flywheel runs one way. The capability building runs the other. Whether the two flywheels balance depends on contract terms most observers cannot see.

"It's not just about having control, but having meaningful control." — Tay PeiChin, Tony Blair Institute, on African nations outsourcing data-centre management to third parties

For Chronos and the wider AI-on-Africa underwriting frame: this piece is the structural denominator. Every AI-deployment story we look at runs on top of an infrastructure stack that is, today, 90% US-owned. That doesn't kill the thesis — most of the world's software stack is US-owned and that hasn't stopped local operators building defensible businesses on top of it. But it does mean the moat in any African AI deal needs to live in the data, the distribution, or the local regulatory permission — not in the compute layer or the model layer.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 005's agentic-workflows case studies. Issue 008's Andrew Ng open-source piece. Issue 010's Ben Evans AI-job-exposure essay. Today's Bhattacharya piece adds the geopolitical layer to the same emerging picture: AI deployment in Africa is a real category, but the underlying physical infrastructure is a different sovereignty story than the policy documents make it sound. The Spock heuristic: when underwriting an Africa-AI deal, ask which of the four layers (compute, model, data, distribution) the moat lives in. Anything except data or distribution today is a Big Tech option that can be revoked.

Africa & EM · Consumer Credit RG-relevant
M-KOPA published its 2026 Ghana Impact Report on Wednesday 20 May. The headline number — $82m of credit unlocked across 550,000 customers — is one Roger flagged as relevant on Issue 010 (M-KOPA operates heavily in Kenya, now Ghana scaling). The substantive read is what the smartphone has become: 67% of insured customers got health insurance for the first time, 44% accessed a formal financial product for the first time, 54% reported higher income after purchase, 76% reported a measurable improvement in quality of life. The smartphone as a financial-inclusion bundle, packaged inside a 12-month instalment.

M-KOPA Ghana — $82m credit, 550k customers, smartphone-as-platform

Victoria Fakiya · Techpoint Africa Digest · Thu 21 May 2026

M-KOPA's Ghana Impact Report covers the period from its 2021 entry through to end-March 2026. Cumulative credit unlocked: GHS 1.2bn ($82m), across 550,000 customers, distributed through 3,000+ agents across all 16 regions of Ghana. The "More than a Phone" bundle (launched January 2025) attaches health insurance via Turaco, data plans, and device protection to every instalment. The result: 67% of insured customers got health insurance for the first time; 44% accessed any formal financial product for the first time; 36% had never owned a smartphone before; 43% of women specifically chose M-KOPA for the bundled health cover. The economic translation: 54% of customers report higher income after purchase; 76% report improved quality of life. GSMA estimates an entry-level smartphone costs up to 95% of a low-income earner's monthly wage in sub-Saharan Africa. M-KOPA's daily micro-payment model is the unit economics that breaks that constraint. Africa-wide, M-KOPA has now disbursed $2bn+ in credit since 2011 and reached profitability in 2024 with annual revenue of $416m.

Techpoint Africa Digest — Victoria Fakiya

The Ghana numbers re-prove what Kenya already proved: M-KOPA is not, by 2026, a hardware financing company. It is a financial services platform with a smartphone as its acquisition channel. The hardware is the entry point; the insurance, credit, data, and digital-banking products attached to the daily payment are the LTV. The 67% first-time-health-insurance figure tells you most of the story — without M-KOPA, those people would not have insurance, full stop. The 36% first-time-smartphone figure is the other half: this is structurally additive demand, not displacement of an existing channel.

For Roger's M-KOPA tracking specifically: the Ghana data is a useful triangulation point for the Kenya thesis. Kenya is M-KOPA's most mature market; Ghana is roughly 2.5 years behind. The same playbook works in both — direct-to-low-income, daily payments, services-attached-to-device, sales-network-as-distribution-moat. The fact that the Ghana playbook is now generating numbers comparable to Kenya at the same age says the operating model is portable and not Kenya-specific. Standard Bank Group is the largest debt lender to M-KOPA ($200m+ sustainability-linked facility, Lendable as verification agent) — the read-across to today's Standard Bank reorder piece is real: this is what continental scale looks like at the operating level.

"54% of customers say they earn more money after purchasing the device... 76% reported an improvement in their overall quality of life." — M-KOPA Ghana Impact Report, 20 May 2026

The strategic question for Chronos is the read-across to portfolio. Optasia's airtime-credit-as-distribution-channel model is the closest analogue. Crossfin's payment-rail-into-merchant-distribution model is a related architecture. The pattern across all three: the underlying product (credit, payments, identity) gets distributed through an everyday consumer-touchpoint (airtime top-up, payment terminal, smartphone purchase) at a per-transaction cost that legacy distribution cannot match. M-KOPA's Ghana economics ($150 per customer, 76% reporting QoL improvement, 67% first-time insurance) are an existence proof for the model that we are betting on in adjacent categories.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 008's $1.5T African mobile-money chart established the size of the category. Issue 009's African fintech VC chart showed where the capital is flowing. Issue 010's Salvador Anglada piece committed Optasia to "trust is infrastructure" doctrine. Today's M-KOPA piece is the operational data that backs all three frames simultaneously. The category is at scale, the capital is flowing, and the operating doctrine works.

AI Frontier · Simon Willison
Simon Willison published an essay yesterday (27 May) arguing that April 2026 is the inflection point at which Anthropic and OpenAI both found product-market fit — measured by the enterprise pricing change, the SpaceX inference deal, and the Uber and Microsoft budget-overrun stories. The discipline of this piece is that it does the maths: Willison runs his actual API-token consumption and shows that $2,180 worth of tokens are being delivered to him for $200. That gap is what's now closing. Worth reading before any board conversation about portco AI-spend budgets.

Simon Willison: Anthropic and OpenAI have found product-market fit — April 2026 is the inflection

Simon Willison · simonwillison.net · Wed 27 May 2026

Willison's argument has four legs. (1) Both Anthropic and OpenAI quietly moved enterprise customers from "Claude seats include enough usage for a typical workday" to "$20/seat/month plus API pricing for usage" — Anthropic in November 2025, OpenAI on 2 April 2026. The previous extreme discounts are gone. (2) Both labs released new frontier models (Opus 4.7 on 16 April, GPT-5.5 on 23 April) at 1.4-2x the previous API price. (3) SpaceX's S-1 disclosed an Anthropic cloud services agreement worth $1.25bn/month through May 2029 — for inference, not training — implying the inference budgets are extraordinary. (4) Stories of Uber blowing its full-year AI budget by Q1 2026 (mostly Claude Code) and Microsoft cancelling Claude Code licences are not failure stories — they are exactly what product-market fit feels like, with customers "sucking air through their teeth and then saying yes". Anthropic is rumoured to be heading for its first profitable quarter at $10.9bn Q2 revenue. The November 2025 model wave plus April 2026 enterprise pricing are the inflection.

Simon Willison — Product-market fit

Willison's analytical discipline is what makes this piece useful. He's not predicting the bubble or denying it; he's doing the arithmetic on his own usage, on the SpaceX disclosure, and on the labs' job-listing patterns (32.6% of OpenAI's open roles are now enterprise sales-and-support; 26.9% at Anthropic). The conclusion is observable rather than speculative: the labs have shifted from "burn capital to acquire users" to "extract revenue from a category that has, finally, started to pay for itself".

The read for portcos and the wider Chronos AI-deployment frame: the era of "AI tools are basically free" is over. Anyone building product economics on the assumption that LLM costs will keep falling needs to repeat the maths on April-2026 pricing rather than 2025 pricing. Opus 4.7 is 1.4x the price of Opus 4.6. GPT-5.5 is 2x the price of GPT-5.4. Enterprise no longer gets the extreme discount. The token-spend curve in any seriously-AI-deployed portco is going to look a lot like the Uber curve — budgeted in 2025, blown in 2026.

"The best advice I ever heard on pricing a product was that your customer should suck air through their teeth and then say yes. Uber's budget overrun and Microsoft's seat cancellations look like that effect playing out in practice." — Simon Willison, 27 May 2026

For Chronos board-meeting hygiene: every portfolio company with an AI deployment thesis needs to be reporting two new metrics in 2026: monthly LLM-token spend and unit economics per AI-assisted output. Without those two, the board is flying blind. Willison's $2,180-of-tokens-for-$200 data point is the gap that closed in April; portcos still operating on the assumption that the gap is open are running a risk that won't be visible until the renewal cycle.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 005's agentic-workflows case studies showed AI landing in production. Issue 007's Anthropic Economic Index quantified the workforce penetration. Issue 008's Andrew Ng open-source piece argued for the cost-control alternative. Today's Willison piece closes the loop: production-scale AI deployment has just been repriced, and the repricing is now in the budget conversations of every operator that's been paying attention. The boards should ask the question this week, not next quarter.

PwC announced its expanded Claude deployment one week earlier (14 May). Deloitte and EY are next. KPMG rolls Claude to 276,000 employees — the Big Four AI race lands

Anthropic press release · 19 May 2026

Anthropic and KPMG announced a strategic alliance on 19 May 2026 to integrate Claude across KPMG's 276,000-person global workforce. The deployment covers audit, tax, advisory, and consulting workflows. KPMG is also building industry-specific Claude-based assistants for its clients. The deal lands one week after Anthropic's analogous PwC announcement (14 May) — PwC is deploying Claude to "build technology, execute deals, and reinvent enterprise functions" for clients across the firm. Both deployments use the new Claude Managed Agents capabilities released on 26 May (self-hosted sandboxes, MCP tunnels, enterprise security controls). Anthropic's open job-listings show 105 of 390 roles are enterprise sales / forward-deployed engineers, supporting the scale of these rollouts. Concurrent context: SpaceX-Anthropic $1.25bn/month compute deal through May 2029, signed in May 2026.

Anthropic — KPMG strategic alliance

The KPMG and PwC announcements within seven days of each other are not coincidence. They are the Big Four professional-services category committing — at the strategic level, not the experimentation level — to a specific AI vendor as the spine of the next-decade delivery model. KPMG's framing makes this explicit: this is not a tooling deal; it is a strategic alliance covering audit, tax, advisory and consulting simultaneously. Once an audit firm runs its analytical work through a specific AI vendor's stack, the working papers, the precedent libraries, and the institutional knowledge graph all accrete around that vendor. The switching cost is structural and increases month-on-month.

For Chronos portfolio companies with professional-services advisors: the practical implication is that the advisor's IC memos, due-diligence frameworks, valuation approaches, audit working papers, and tax structuring are now being produced with Claude as the underlying analytical engine. The quality, speed, and consistency of advisor output will all change — likely up. The cost may also change — likely down for high-volume routine work, possibly up for bespoke advisory where the advisor charges for the AI-augmented depth. Any portco renegotiating an advisor contract in the next twelve months should ask explicitly what the AI-driven deflation is going to be and where it accrues.

"Anthropic forms $200 million partnership with the Gates Foundation... KPMG integrates Claude across its core business and workforce of more than 276,000 in strategic alliance." — Anthropic newsroom, 14-19 May 2026

The competitive read: this is the AI vendor war moving from "model performance" to "enterprise lock-in". Anthropic has won the Big Four moment so far — KPMG and PwC public, Deloitte and EY widely rumoured. OpenAI has won the consumer-app moment. The two moments are different markets; the next moment is the operator-stack moment, where a frontier-model vendor wins inside the actual operating workflow of a regulated industry. Banks, insurers, telcos, and large healthcare operators are where that next round gets played out. For Chronos AI-deployment underwriting, the heuristic: which frontier model has the operator-stack lock-in advantage in the specific vertical you are underwriting?

The longitudinal thread: Issue 006's Sutter Health / R1 RCM piece showed production AI deployment in healthcare. Issue 007's SA banks AT1 raise showed the capital being raised to deploy AI in African banks. Today's KPMG announcement is the same dynamic at the advisor-services level. The category is consolidating around the labs that can offer end-to-end enterprise infrastructure — model + sandbox + MCP + audit trail + security — rather than just a frontier model. Pure model-quality competition is becoming the floor, not the differentiator.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Force Claude into a 2×2

The most underused output format with Claude is the 2×2 matrix. Most partners default to asking for a list, a summary, or a recommendation — all of which Claude is happy to provide, all of which read as bullet-point hedging once you actually try to use them in a meeting. The 2×2 is different: it forces the model to commit to two named dimensions, place specific entities in specific quadrants, and justify each placement. The constraint is the analytical work. A good 2×2 is trivially shareable in a partner discussion, much faster to react to than a paragraph of hedged conclusions, and surfaces the trade-off the prose version is hiding. Two dimensions in. Four named quadrants out. Specific things in specific boxes, with one-sentence reasons.

Three 2×2 prompts to keep in the back pocket

"(1) 'Plot the top 20 African fintech operators on a 2×2 with axes data-asset depth and distribution-network depth. Name the quadrants. Tell me which quadrant gets disrupted first by stablecoin rails.' (2) 'Plot the top 30 listed companies in our pipeline on a 2×2 of cost-side AI integration vs revenue-side AI integration. Name the quadrants. Tell me which quadrant Chronos should be hunting in for the rest of 2026.' (3) 'For each of the 11 Chronos portcos, place them on a 2×2 of trust-as-infrastructure (Anglada's framing) vs speed-of-execution. Name the quadrants. Tell me where the most underrated operator sits.'"
