Global · AI · Labour Analysis World's-best-thinkers
In Import AI 464, Jack Clark — Anthropic co-founder, and the newsletter the partners read for the macro read on AI — flags a July update to the Remote Labor Index (RLI), a benchmark from the Center for AI Safety and Scale that tests whether AI agents can complete real, paid freelance projects end to end: 3D and CAD models, architectural floor plans, advertisement videos, graphic design, data analysis. The headline number: the frontier success rate has risen from 2.5% at the index's launch in October 2025 to 16.1% in July 2026 — “more than quadrupled in under eight months.” The three newest models tested score 6.3% (GPT-5.5), 8.3% (Opus 4.8) and 16.1% (Fable 5). These are not chat answers or code snippets; they are whole priced deliverables a client paid a human to produce. Clark's own bet, stated plainly: AI is expanding its economically relevant capabilities faster than humans are expanding their comparative advantage against it.

AI now finishes one freelance job in six — up from one in forty last October

Import AI 464 · Jack Clark · Sun 6 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the number to hold onto as the abstraction under every “AI and jobs” conversation — not a survey of what firms say they'll do, but a measured completion rate on actual priced work, and it is compounding on a steep curve. And it is the honest frame for our own portfolio question: the value at risk is not the loan book or the licence but the hours — the design, drafting, analysis and back-office labour that sits inside every company we own, priced today as if a person must do it. Sixteen per cent is small; the slope is not, and the slope is the story.

One paid project in six, finished end to end — and the curve is eight months old
Import AI 464 — Jack Clark, “AI systems are getting better at pricey online work tasks”

This is the longitudinal payoff of a number this page first ran in Issue 040, when the same index sat around 6% and the framing was “AI's freelance completion rate quadruples.” It has now quadrupled again off a higher base. The reason to keep returning to RLI rather than to the louder benchmarks is that it measures the thing that actually converts to money: not a model's exam score, but whether it can deliver a whole job a client would otherwise have hired a person to do — the ring re-rendered in a new cut, the 60-second animated advert cut to a voiceover, the cadastral plan turned into a dimensioned floor plan. Sixteen per cent completion is not a labour market event yet. A curve that goes from 2.5% to 16% in eight months is the leading edge of one.

“The frontier has more than quadrupled in under eight months — a concrete signal of how quickly economically capable AI agents are advancing.”

Clark states the wager without hedging: he is betting AI expands its economically relevant capabilities faster than humans expand their comparative advantage against it. Read against yesterday's down-votes, the point is not the deep-tech but the arithmetic underneath our own book. Every portfolio company prices a large block of internal hours — design, drafting, analysis, support, reconciliation — as work only a person can do. RLI is the meter on how fast that assumption decays. The forward question for us is not “which AI company do we buy” but “in which of the operators we already own does a 16-and-rising completion rate quietly re-rate the cost base first” — and whether the ones that capture that saving are the ones with proprietary data and distribution, or the ones that merely rent the model like everyone else. The counter-case worth holding: RLI measures self-contained freelance briefs, not the messy, context-heavy, relationship-bound work most jobs actually are — and the gap between a clean brief and a real mandate is exactly where human comparative advantage may prove stickier than the slope implies.

Egypt · Manufacturing · Industrial Policy Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
OPPO, the Chinese handset maker, has opened its fifth Egyptian retail store, in Benha — a small event that sits on top of a large one. Since a 2023 factory in 10th of Ramadan City, Egypt has pushed global OEMs to build locally rather than import: Samsung, Xiaomi, Vivo and Nokia all now assemble handsets there, giving Egypt one of Africa's deepest smartphone-manufacturing bases. The country produced 10 million phones in 2025, up from 3 million two years earlier, and is targeting 15 million this year. OPPO is already Egypt's third-largest smartphone brand behind Samsung and Xiaomi, with nearly one in five users; the Benha store is the retail half of a make-and-sell strategy — the phones on its shelves come from an Egyptian factory, not a shipping container. Cairo's ambition is to be a regional electronics-manufacturing hub with export destinations, not merely a market where foreign brands sell.

Egypt now makes the phones it sells — and wants to make 15 million of them this year

TechCabal Daily · Smartphones · Tue 14 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is industrial policy actually working — a state that spent years dangling incentives has moved four of the five largest handset brands from importing to assembling, tripling domestic output in two years, which is the kind of manufacturing base almost no other African economy has built at scale. And the structural point for us is import substitution as a moat: local production sidesteps FX bottlenecks and import queues, responds faster to demand, and earns government incentives — a durable cost-and-supply edge that has nothing to do with software and everything to do with where a thing is physically made.

A two-year tripling most of the continent has not managed to build
TechCabal Daily — “OPPO opens its fifth store in Egypt” (14 Jul 2026)

This page keeps circling the question of where value is physically located — who owns the rail, the licence, the identity layer. Egypt's answer is unfashionably concrete: own the factory. While the continent's headline story is software and fintech, Cairo has quietly assembled the deepest handset-manufacturing base in Africa by making local assembly the price of access to a large, protected consumer market. OPPO's fifth store is trivial on its own; it matters as the visible tip of a policy that has moved Samsung, Xiaomi, Vivo, Nokia and OPPO from importing to assembling, and tripled output to 10 million units in two years. That is a different model of development from the venture-funded one this page usually tracks — slower, heavier, and far harder for a competitor to clone.

“The phones on its shelves come from an Egyptian factory, not a shipping container — a model few African countries have built at scale.”

For a firm that thinks in moats, the interesting property is that manufacturing localisation is defensible in exactly the ways software increasingly isn't. A factory sidesteps the FX and import bottlenecks that throttle every African importer, earns state incentives a newcomer can't easily match, and builds a supplier ecosystem — components, logistics, trained labour — that compounds and can't be spun up overnight. The caution is equally structural: assembly is low-margin, tariff-dependent and politically contingent, and “made in Egypt” can mean screwing together imported kits rather than genuine value-add. The forward question we'd carry: as import-substitution industrial policy spreads — and it is spreading, from handsets to EVs to pharma — is the durable investment in the branded assemblers, or in the picks-and-shovels underneath them: the local component suppliers, the industrial-park landlords, the logistics and trade-finance rails that every one of these factories has to rent?

Kenya · Regulation · Liability Analysis Hyper-local
Kenya's High Court has upheld a ruling that both Diamond Trust Bank (DTB) and Safaricom — the bank and the telco behind M-PESA — are jointly liable for KES 4.4m ($34,000) stolen from a customer, Mercy Wairimu Kariuki, after a SIM-swap fraud she had reported and believed resolved. Justice Asenath Ongeri split liability 60:40 (Safaricom KES 2.63m, DTB KES 1.79m), finding each owed the customer a concurrent and independent duty of care: Safaricom for allowing the swap after a warning, DTB for waving through a burst of abnormal transfers structured to stay just under its KES 2m daily limit across a weekend reset. The court rejected both defences — that a correct PIN absolved the bank, and that the SIM swap was an intervening act breaking the chain: “A bank cannot hide behind a customer's PIN… the banking system operates on an automated 24/7 basis.” The ruling sets a precedent future Kenyan courts can cite.

A Kenyan court makes banks and telcos share the bill for SIM-swap fraud

TechCabal · Legislation · Sun 13 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this quietly re-prices fraud risk across Kenya's mobile-money economy — “the PIN was entered” and “it happened over a weekend” are no longer defences, so both banks and telcos now carry an independent, non-delegable duty to catch foreseeable fraud even when it originates in someone else's system. And it dissolves the convenient fiction that operators and lenders sit in separate regulatory lanes: the court treated the swap and the withdrawals as one chain, which raises the compliance and monitoring bar — and the cost base — for every player touching mobile money.

TechCabal — “Kenyan court holds banks, telcos liable over $34,000 SIM swap fraud”

Yesterday's Nigeria item (Nic: up) showed a government coordinating platform rules before they bite; this is the same market getting its guardrails the other way — a judge writing the rule after the loss, and making it stick for everyone who comes next. The substance is not the courtroom drama the partners waved off in the OPay card; it is a structural shift in where liability sits. By holding that DTB and Safaricom each owed Kariuki a concurrent duty, and refusing to let either point at the other's failure, Justice Ongeri collapsed the “not my layer” defence that banks and telcos have leaned on for a decade. Transaction-limit compliance is no longer a shield; a system that can wave through an obvious fraud pattern “without human review is no longer a defence in itself.”

“A bank cannot hide behind a customer's PIN when it is presented with transactions so glaringly out of the ordinary that a reasonable banker would have been put on inquiry.”

For anyone holding financial-services or telco exposure in Africa, this is a real, if unglamorous, cost signal. A precedent that assigns shared, independent liability for SIM-swap losses pushes both sides to invest in fraud detection — behavioural monitoring, tighter SIM-replacement controls, human-in-the-loop review of anomalous bursts — and raises the price of getting security wrong across an entire market, not just for the two named defendants. That is bad for cost bases and good for the vendors who sell fraud and identity infrastructure into them. The forward question we'd carry: as courts across the continent start treating banks and telcos as jointly responsible for the mobile-money rail they share, does the durable value accrue to the operators absorbing the new liability, or to the fraud-detection and digital-identity layer they will now all be forced to buy?

Kenya · Telecoms · Capital Briefing Longitudinal
Yas — the pan-African telecoms arm of Madagascar-based AXIAN Telecom, which bought Zuku's parent Wananchi Group for $63m in November 2025 — has raised €270m ($307m) from the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development to rebuild the Kenyan fixed-internet market Zuku once led. EBRD is committing €170m ($193m) for Kenya and Senegal operations, with a further €100m ($113m) earmarked for future acquisitions. In Kenya the money modernises the inherited fibre network; in Senegal it funds 4G, 5G and fibre rollout. The facility includes a Kenyan-shilling tranche — the first EBRD local-currency financing in sub-Saharan Africa — and follows a $160m AfDB facility approved in 2025. Yas serves 43.8 million subscribers across 11 markets and generated $1.69bn of revenue in 2025. Zuku's fixed-internet share had fallen from over 40% in 2016 to 12.7% by mid-2025 as Safaricom (now 34.3%) and cheaper rivals took the market.

Yas raises $307m — on the first shilling-denominated EBRD loan in sub-Saharan Africa — to win back the market Zuku lost

TechCabal Daily · Telecoms · Tue 14 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the debt side of the funding shift the partners marked up yesterday — a nine-figure, development-bank-led facility into asset-heavy fibre and mobile infrastructure, exactly the “blended finance into infrastructure-style businesses” pattern the H1 data flagged, and the opposite of the venture-equity retreat. And the quiet first is the shilling tranche: an EBRD local-currency loan in sub-Saharan Africa removes the FX mismatch that has sunk many an infrastructure play, and may be the more repeatable innovation than the headline number.

TechCabal Daily — “Yas secures $307 million investment from EBRD” (14 Jul 2026)

Yesterday's barbell item (Nic: up) described capital concentrating into fewer, larger cheques and turning toward asset-heavy, debt-funded infrastructure. Yas is that thesis in one deal: not a Series A into a software startup but €270m of mostly development-bank money into fibre and spectrum, backing a consolidator rebuilding a market incumbents let slip. It is also a study in how African infrastructure actually gets financed now — AXIAN stacking an AfDB facility, an EBRD facility and an acquisition war-chest to turn a declining asset (Wananchi/Zuku) into a credible challenger to Safaricom. The equity venture story gets the headlines; the debt-and-DFI story is quietly doing the heavy building.

“A well-capitalised Yas is a different competitor from the one that lost ground for a decade.”

The detail worth holding is the shilling tranche. Local-currency financing sounds technical, but FX mismatch — dollar debt against local-currency revenue — is one of the most common ways African infrastructure deals blow up when the currency moves. An EBRD facility denominated in Kenyan shillings, the first of its kind in sub-Saharan Africa, is the kind of structural plumbing that, if it becomes a template, lowers the risk premium on a whole category of deals. The scepticism is the execution: capital is necessary but not sufficient, Zuku lost the market on service and speed as much as money, and $307m against Safaricom's scale rebuilds infrastructure without guaranteeing customers come back. The forward question for us: in African telecoms and infrastructure, is the returns edge in the operators raising these facilities, or in the layer that structures and de-risks them — the local-currency debt, the DFI co-financing, the tower-and-fibre assets that outlast whichever brand rents them?

Nigeria · Embedded Finance · Payments Briefing Longitudinal
Klump, a Nigerian buy-now-pay-later startup, has embedded its instalment technology inside Jumia's checkout — letting shoppers on one of Africa's largest marketplaces compare financing offers from multiple banks (First Bank, Renmoney, Credit Direct, Wema Bank) without leaving the page. The structural choice is the story: Klump does not lend and holds no credit risk. It provides the rails that connect borrowers to partner lenders, who underwrite, fund and carry the risk. Shoppers pick a product, choose “Pay with Klump,” select a lender, pass a credit check and pay a 20–30% deposit, with the balance over six to twelve months; loans run from ₦1,000 ($0.73) to ₦2.6m ($1,898). CEO Celestine Omin: the bet is that “owning checkout placement across Nigeria's biggest marketplaces matters more than owning a loan book.” Klump now powers instalments for two of Nigeria's largest e-commerce platforms.

Klump slides multi-bank instalments into Jumia's checkout — and refuses to own the loan

TechCabal · Ecommerce · Fri 11 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the exact “more time, not more credit” thread Nic marked up on Float yesterday, now shipping at scale inside Africa's biggest marketplace — instalments at the point of purchase, against existing bank relationships rather than a new lender. And the design is the thesis the partners also backed in Renew: distribution over balance sheet. By owning the checkout placement and letting partner banks carry the risk and set the price, Klump is a neutral credit-rail, not a lender — deliberately ceding approval rates and margin to keep the position that compounds.

TechCabal — “Klump brings multi-bank instalment payments to Jumia's online checkout”

Two threads the partners upvoted yesterday meet in this one product. Float carried the “more time, not more credit” logic into a £250bn British gap; Renew argued the next small-business banks won't be banks but data-rich platforms. Klump is both claims as a live integration: instalments embedded at the moment of purchase, and a founder who has decided the durable asset is the checkout, not the credit book. “Owning checkout placement… matters more than owning a loan book” is the whole strategy in a sentence — Klump takes no credit risk, sets no prices, and lets First Bank, Renmoney, Credit Direct and Wema compete inside its rail while it keeps the one thing that's hard to dislodge: the placement in front of high-intent buyers.

“Owning checkout placement across Nigeria's biggest marketplaces matters more than owning a loan book.”

The elegance is also the vulnerability, and it's worth naming both. As neutral infrastructure, Klump is capital-light, avoids the balance-sheet risk that sank a generation of BNPL lenders, and gets more defensible with every marketplace it wires in — it now sits inside two of Nigeria's largest. But ceding underwriting to partner banks means ceding approval rates, terms and the customer's actual experience to lenders it doesn't control; if the banks tighten or price badly, Klump owns the checkout but not the conversion. And its position depends on marketplaces not building the rail themselves — Jumia already ran CredPal and Easybuy before adding Klump. The forward question we'd carry from Float to Renew to here: in embedded consumer credit, does the value settle with the platform that owns the placement, the banks that own the risk, or the marketplace that owns the customer and can switch rails at will?

South Africa · Media · Business Models Briefing Wouldn't have seen otherwise
eMedia, the South African broadcaster behind streaming service eVOD, has launched Openview Stream — a free, ad-supported streaming service (FAST: Free Ad-Supported Streaming TV) rolling out across Kenya, Ghana, Rwanda, Zambia, Botswana, Namibia and Mauritius. Viewers pay nothing; advertisers fund it, and audiences “pay” with attention. The telling omission: South Africa isn't on the launch list. eMedia keeps eVOD — which crossed 2.2m users in February, up 22% year on year, with 9,000+ hours of content — focused on its home market, and uses Openview Stream as the vehicle to expand across the rest of Africa. The backdrop is a structural shift: subscription fatigue is real, pay-TV subscribers are declining (hurting MultiChoice's DStv), and the FAST model — Netflix without the subscription — is gaining traction globally as households tire of stacking monthly fees.

A South African broadcaster bets Africa would rather watch an advert than pay a subscription

TechCabal Daily · Streaming · Tue 14 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is a deliberate wager on ability-to-pay — in markets where a stack of $10 subscriptions is out of reach, the binding constraint isn't content, it's the wallet, so eMedia removes the paywall and monetises attention instead. And it's a smart segmentation play: keep the paid product (eVOD) at home where subscription works, and lead with free-ad-supported everywhere else, matching the monetisation model to each market's income rather than forcing one price across the continent.

TechCabal Daily — “South Africa's eMedia launches free streaming service” (14 Jul 2026)

Two of this week's items rhyme with this one, and neither is about media. Float and Klump both monetise something other than the obvious thing — time and placement rather than credit; eMedia monetises attention rather than a subscription. The common structural move is to look at a product priced one way in a rich market and re-price it for a market where the constraint is different. For streaming in most of Africa, the constraint is not appetite for content — it is the number of $10-a-month debits a household can absorb, which is roughly one, if that. FAST removes the paywall entirely and sends the bill to advertisers, and eMedia's decision to hold eVOD back in South Africa while leading with free-ad-supported everywhere else is a clean admission that the right business model is a function of the market's income, not the product.

“You'll pay with your attention instead of your wallet — the trade-off, and also the business model.”

For a partnership that thinks about consumer businesses across income tiers, the read is that ad-funded, zero-price models may be the honest way into low-ARPU markets that subscription simply can't reach — the same logic that made free-with-ads radio and broadcast TV mass-market in the first place, now rebuilt for streaming. The caution is that FAST economics are brutal: advertising CPMs in these markets are thin, the model needs enormous scale and watch-time to work, and it competes with free-and-pirated alternatives that carry no cost base at all. The forward question we'd carry into any consumer-media or attention-economy view: in African markets where the wallet is the ceiling, is the durable business the one that owns the content, the one that owns the ad-serving and measurement rail underneath the free tier, or the one that owns the distribution — the set-top box, the bundled data, the default app on the cheap handset Egypt is now busy manufacturing?

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Force the 2×2 — make Claude turn a tangle into two axes

Yesterday's tip was the standing sceptic — make the model argue the other side. Today's is a thinking tool rather than a check: when a decision or a market has gone fuzzy in your head, ask Claude to force it onto a 2×2. The discipline of a two-by-two is that it makes you name the two variables that actually matter and throw the rest away — and an AI is unusually good at proposing candidate axes, sorting a messy list of options into the four quadrants, and then telling you which quadrant is crowded and which is empty. The value isn't the pretty grid; it's the argument you have with the model about which two axes are the real ones. Half the time the first pair is wrong, and finding that out is the point.

A worked example

Paste in a shortlist of six possible investments or six competitors and ask: “Propose the two axes that best separate these — not the obvious ones, the ones that actually predict who wins. Plot all six on that 2×2, tell me which quadrant is over-crowded and which is empty, and name what a company in the empty quadrant would have to look like. Then propose a different pair of axes that would tell a different story, and say which framing you trust more and why.” You'll usually learn more from the second grid than the first.
