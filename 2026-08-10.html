Pan-Africa · Mobility · Infrastructure Briefing Chart of the day
Electric-mobility company Spiro ended 2025 with about 22,000 electric motorcycles and more than 600 battery-swap stations in Rwanda — its single largest market across six African countries — against roughly 5,000 in Nigeria, Africa's most populous nation and one of its biggest motorcycle markets. Rwanda (population ~13m) therefore hosts more than four times as many Spiro bikes as Nigeria (population >200m). Its other footprints: Uganda ~16,000, Kenya ~14,000, Togo and Benin ~8,000 each. Group CEO Anant Badjatya attributes Rwanda's lead to a 2023 market entry (a year ahead of Nigeria), Kigali's import-duty relief and coordinated EV policy, and years of swap-network build-out — now moving to “Mega Stations” serving 2,000+ riders a day. Nigeria, he says, is being built differently: local assembly, battery recycling and industrial capacity first, deployment later. Spiro plans to reach ~20 African markets, with Cameroon and Tanzania scaling and Ethiopia, Malawi, Mali and the DRC next.

Spiro's biggest EV market is Rwanda, not Nigeria — policy and infrastructure, not population, decide where the fleet scales

TechCabal · Features · Kenn Abuya · Wed 6 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is one company's deployment map. The one that matters is the variable it exposes — market size is not the driver. A 13-million-person country with duty relief, coordinated urban policy and a dense swap network out-deploys a 200-million-person one four to one, because battery-swapping is an infrastructure business and infrastructure follows policy and rider economics, not headcount. It is the same lesson running under Moove (Issue 060): the durable value in African mobility is the physical layer — and that layer scales where the state has already cleared the road.

Source: Spiro Sustainability Report 2025 via TechCabal (6 Aug 2026) · the largest market is the best-governed one, not the most populous
TechCabal — “Why Spiro deployed 22,000 electric motorcycles in Rwanda but only 5,000 in Nigeria”

The structural read is that a battery-swap network is infrastructure, and infrastructure compounds where three things line up: an early start, a government that prices the transition in (duty relief, fleet mandates, coordinated urban policy), and rider economics that hold. Rwanda had all three by 2023, so Spiro could compress years of build-out into a short runway and is now adding capacity, not coverage. Nigeria has the demand and the scale but not yet the aligned policy, so Spiro is deliberately front-loading the unglamorous industrial base — local assembly, battery recycling, second-life cells — before it floods the streets with bikes. The investable point is that in asset-heavy African mobility, the binding constraint is rarely the size of the addressable market; it is the density of the physical network you can profitably stand up, and that density is a function of policy.

A country a fifteenth of Nigeria's size runs four times the fleet. In an infrastructure business, the market you can build beats the market you can imagine.

The threads: to Issue 060's item 2 (Moove) — both argue the defensible layer in African mobility is the physical plumbing (fleets, chargers, swap stations), and today's Spiro data adds the qualifier Moove's raise left implicit: that plumbing only scales where the policy environment lets it, which is why site selection, not TAM, is the real skill. To Issue 060's item 3 — the Egypt chart flagged a single $327m Spiro raise skewing H1 funding; here is where that capital actually goes, into steel and swap stations rather than software. To the Chronos read on infrastructure and organisational design — it favours businesses whose moat is a hard-to-replicate physical network built alongside a supportive regulator, and warns against reading a large population as a proxy for a large deployable market. The sceptic's ledger: this is one company's self-reported sustainability data, and Badjatya has every reason to frame a thin Nigerian footprint as strategy rather than struggle; Rwanda is a small, unusually well-governed, top-down market whose model may not transplant to Lagos's scale and chaos; and battery-swap economics remain capital-intensive and unproven at true continental scale. The forward marker: whether Nigeria's “industrial base first” sequencing actually converts into Rwanda-like density — or whether the four-to-one gap is telling us that without aligned policy, the largest market stays the smallest fleet.

Pan-Africa · Banking · Credit Analysis Tests a thesis
Africa's banks were built on one assumption — that the best borrower earns a regular salary — on a continent where ~83% of the workforce is informal. That leaves a $300bn consumer-and-SME financing gap and millions of creditworthy people invisible to formal credit. Lenders are now supplementing the payslip with alternative data: mobile-money flows, telecom activity, POS histories and other digital footprints. Notto, a licensed alternative credit bureau operating in South Africa, Zambia and Zimbabwe, says it has analysed 1bn+ transaction records, built scores for 8m+ consumers and generated assessments for 5m mobile-money users. Before its models, only 1–2% of those five million had formal credit access; within about a year, lending to them expanded almost tenfold while non-performing loans stayed below 4%. Access Bank's cross-border head says lenders are partnering with mobile-network operators to read cash flows “even when they're informal.”

African banks stop asking for a payslip and start reading the data trail — and the previously-invisible turn out to repay

TechCabal · Features · Phathisani Moyo · Wed 6 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is financial inclusion by better plumbing. The one that matters is where creditworthiness now lives — not in a formal-employment record but in the proprietary behavioural exhaust (wallet deposits, till receipts, airtime patterns) that Africa's informal majority already generates. Whoever holds that data can underwrite the people the payslip model can't see, at bad-debt rates under 4%. It is the AI-progress thesis in its most concrete African form: the durable asset is the data that lets a model price risk others simply cannot.

Tenfold more lending, bad debt under 4% — the constraint was never the borrowers' quality, only the lenders' data
TechCabal — “Millions lack credit histories. African banks are changing how they lend”

The structural read is that credit is a data problem wearing an income-verification costume. The payslip was only ever a proxy for “will this person pay me back,” and it is a poor proxy on a continent where most economic activity never touches a formal salary. Alternative-data underwriting swaps the proxy for the behaviour itself — the regularity of wallet top-ups, the rhythm of till receipts, the consistency of airtime spend — and Notto's result (lending up ~10x, NPLs under 4%) says the informal majority was never uncreditworthy; it was unmeasured. The prize accrues to whoever controls the data layer: the mobile-money operator, the POS network, the licensed bureau that can turn a billion raw transactions into a score a bank will act on. That is a moat made of proprietary, compounding data, which is exactly the asset the Chronos AI-progress test is built to find.

The payslip was a proxy. The data is the thing itself. Whoever holds the transaction trail gets to underwrite the people the old model was blind to.

The threads: to Issue 060's item 6 (Cloud9) — the same instinct from the other side: Cloud9 buys the surface where merchants transact so it can layer a bank on the data; here the banks reach that data through the bureau and the mobile operator, and both point to proprietary transaction data as the real credit engine. To Issue 060's item 1 (Safaricom) — when the regulator caps the visible per-transaction fee, this is where the margin migrates: into the lending book and the data that powers it, which a regulator cannot legislate down as easily as a tariff. To the Chronos AI-progress and moat framework — a lender whose edge is a hard-to-replicate behavioural dataset, validated by sub-4% loss rates, is precisely the “does the AI story show up in the numbers” case, and it is more defensible than a thin consumer app. The sceptic's ledger: the standout figures (8m scored, ~10x lending, <4% NPL) are self-reported by the bureau that profits from the narrative, and a year is far too short a window to see a full credit cycle — alternative-data books have looked pristine before a downturn exposes them; scoring people on their telco and wallet exhaust raises real data-privacy and consent questions that African regulators are only starting to frame; and “we won't introduce risk” is the most dangerous sentence in lending. The forward marker: whether these thin-file books hold their sub-4% loss rates through a genuine stress — an FX shock, a spike in unemployment — because alternative-data lending is a thesis about proprietary data until a bad year turns it into a thesis about underwriting discipline.

South Africa · Civic Tech · Platforms Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
South Africa's Independent Electoral Commission (IEC) has taken voter registration onto WhatsApp — the country's most-used messaging app — ahead of the November local-government elections, letting eligible citizens register or update details via OTP authentication, ID-document upload, address verification and voting-station confirmation. The premise is plain: meet young people on the platform they already live on. It appears to be working — during the final registration drive, ~500,000 registration transactions came from people aged 16–29, and 46% of all new registrations were by voters under 29. Over the final weekend the IEC logged 1.7m registration-related transactions, including 291,016 first-time registrations, with the online portal alone handling 238,000. The commission — already experimenting with digital voter services — says its platforms ran “at full capacity” as the deadline closed.

South Africa registers Gen Z voters inside WhatsApp — the state meets citizens on the rail they already use

TechCabal · Analysis · Phathisani Moyo · Fri 7 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is an electoral-admin convenience. The one that matters is a state institution reaching its hardest-to-reach constituency by going to the platform they already inhabit rather than asking them to queue at a government office — and getting nearly half a million under-30 registrations for it. It is the civic-sector version of the week's spine: distribution beats persuasion, and the cheapest way to reach a population is the rail it already rides. The unsolved half — registration is not turnout — is exactly the limit the thesis keeps running into.

TechCabal — “South Africa is betting on WhatsApp to bring Gen Z back to the ballot box”

The structural read is that adoption is a distribution problem, and the IEC solved it the way the best consumer companies do — by removing friction at the exact point where the user already is. WhatsApp is where South Africans transact, argue and organise; putting OTP-verified registration inside it turned a trip to a government office into a two-minute chat, and the under-30 numbers followed. That is a genuinely useful proof point for anyone building on the continent: the messaging layer is becoming the default interface for services the state and the market both need to deliver — identity, payments, commerce, now civic registration — and the integrity controls (OTP, ID upload, address check) are mature enough to carry it. The honest caveat is baked into the story: lowering the cost of registering does not lower the cost of caring, and turnout depends on whether anyone gives these voters a reason to show up.

Don't build a destination and wait. Put the service inside the app people never close — the electoral commission just proved it works for democracy, not only commerce.

The threads: to Issue 060's item 6 (Cloud9/Chpter) — the same rail, the opposite user: Cloud9 bought WhatsApp/Instagram commerce tools to sell merchants a bank; the IEC uses the identical channel to enrol citizens, and together they show WhatsApp hardening into Africa's default civic-and-commercial interface. To today's item 2 (alternative-data credit) — both are “meet the majority where they already are,” one for a loan, one for a ballot, and both convert an existing behavioural footprint into access. To the Chronos read on platforms and organisational design — it favours businesses positioned on the rails users already inhabit rather than those spending to drag users somewhere new, and it treats messaging-layer distribution as a structural advantage. The sceptic's ledger: registration transactions are a vanity-adjacent metric — 500,000 sign-ups mean little if turnout stays depressed, and the article is candid that easier registration does nothing about political disillusionment; routing national identity and voter data through a Meta-owned platform concentrates civic infrastructure in a foreign private rail with its own outage, privacy and policy risks; and “full capacity” at a deadline can as easily describe a bottleneck as a triumph. The forward marker: November's actual youth turnout — if the under-30 share of the roll translates into under-30 ballots, WhatsApp civic registration becomes a template the whole continent copies; if it doesn't, it is a lesson that the last mile of participation was never the sign-up form.

Pan-Africa · Telecoms · Infrastructure Briefing Longitudinal thread
IHS Towers has completed the sale of its Latin American operations — IHS Brazil and IHS Colombia, ~9,000 tower sites — to Macquarie Asset Management (~$477bn AUM), closing on 7 August 2026 under an agreement struck in February. The divestment leaves IHS focused entirely on Africa, where it runs 28,000+ towers across Nigeria, South Africa, Côte d'Ivoire, Cameroon and Zambia. It also aligns the portfolio with MTN Group's plan to take IHS private — MTN said the sale “aligns with the intention… to acquire only IHS's African assets.” MTN announced the take-private in February; IHS shareholders approved it on 5 August, clearing a key condition. JP Morgan advised IHS. The broader acquisition still needs regulatory approvals across several markets before MTN gains full ownership of one of the continent's largest independent tower portfolios.

IHS sells its last Latin-American towers and narrows to Africa — the MTN take-private advances, one divestment at a time

TechCabal · News · Frank Eleanya · Fri 7 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a regional asset sale. The one that matters is a thesis being executed, not merely announced — IHS shedding its non-African masts to Macquarie so MTN can absorb a clean, Africa-only tower book, reversing the decade in which operators sold their towers to look asset-light. When a listed tower company can't hold the valuation its anchor tenant thinks is cheap, the tenant re-integrates the steel and takes it private, and the portfolio gets tidied for the deal first. This is Issue 060's own-the-infrastructure call moving from vote to completion.

TechCabal — “IHS exits Latin America as MTN takeover moves closer”

The structural read is that the independent-tower model — a listed, specialist owner of passive infrastructure, leased back to the operators — is being unwound at its largest African instance, and the mechanics are instructive. Before you take a company private you make it legible: IHS is selling the Latin-American sprawl to a big infrastructure-fund buyer (Macquarie, exactly the kind of patient capital that wants steady tower cash flows) and leaving MTN a focused, single-continent asset it understands and anchors. For MTN the logic is control and captured economics — own the towers, keep the lease fees it was paying away, and remove the public market's daily discount on an asset it considers strategic — at the cost of re-loading capital-heavy infrastructure onto its own balance sheet. The sale of the LatAm book is the quiet, necessary step that makes the headline take-private cleaner.

First you make the company legible, then you take it private. IHS is selling the sprawl so MTN can own the steel that matters — Africa only.

The threads: to Issue 060's item 5 — that was the shareholder vote; this is the portfolio being surgically prepared around it, and the two together show a re-integration thesis moving from “approved in principle” to “assembled in practice.” To Issue 060's item 3 (Egypt's DPI hunt) — here is the disciplined strategic exit Cairo's funds are straining to manufacture: a real infrastructure buyer, a completed cash sale, no reliance on a frozen IPO window. To Issue 060's item 2 and today's Moove/Spiro/Medwaka thread — MTN sits firmly on the “own the physical layer” side of the week's own-versus-orchestrate axis, betting the tower is the durable asset. To the Chronos connectivity book — a tower re-integration rewrites who pays whom for coverage and matters for any exposure to African passive infrastructure or to MTN's leverage. The sceptic's ledger: taking a listed asset private removes a transparent price and concentrates a strategically vital, capital-heavy business inside a single operator — the opposite of the diversification the tower-co model was sold on; the deal still hinges on multi-market regulatory approvals that can reshape or delay it; and re-loading tower capex onto MTN's balance sheet is precisely the weight equity investors once paid it to shed, so the market's verdict on the leverage is not yet in. The forward marker: whether other African operators follow MTN back toward ownership or defend the independent-tower model — that answer decides whether this is one company's balance-sheet play or the start of the continent's tower industry re-integrating.

Kenya · Digital Assets · Regulation Briefing Longitudinal thread
Global and Africa-focused crypto firms — Binance, Luno, Yellow Card, VALR, Kotani Pay and Roqqu — are lining up to apply for virtual-asset licences in Kenya after the country gazetted its VASP rules on 24 July. Kenya recorded ~$19bn in crypto inflows between July 2024 and June 2025 (Chainalysis), more than double the prior year's ~$7bn. The framework puts exchanges, wallets and payment processors under the Capital Markets Authority and Central Bank of Kenya, with 10 licence categories and hard paid-up-capital floors: wallets KES150m (~$1.16m), exchanges KES100m (~$772k), stablecoin issuers KES300m (~$2.3m). Tax shifts from a proposed 3% transfer levy to a 10% excise on VASP fees, with transaction reporting to the tax commissioner. It puts Kenya among the few African states — with South Africa, Mauritius and Botswana — running a dedicated crypto regime, and contrasts with Nigeria's tax-first approach (a 1.5% stamp duty, no comparable licensing).

Binance, Luno and Yellow Card queue for Kenya's first crypto licences — the state draws the perimeter and capital lines up inside it

TechCabal · Cryptocurrency · Emmanuel Nwosu · Fri 7 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is exchanges filing paperwork. The one that matters is a state competing to be the jurisdiction crypto chooses — Kenya trades regulatory clarity (a clean perimeter, capital floors, a fee-based tax) for institutional capital and an early-mover industry, and the biggest names queue up rather than route around it. Set against Nigeria taxing first and licensing never, it is a live experiment in whether rules-first or revenue-first wins the rail. The same Nairobi that capped M-PESA's fees last week is now pricing entry to the next rail before it scales.

TechCabal — “Binance, Luno, Yellow Card, others seek Kenyan crypto licences under new rules”

The structural read is that regulation, done early and clearly, is an acquisition channel for capital rather than a brake on it. Kenya has one of Africa's largest crypto markets ($19bn of inflows, doubling year on year) and, until 24 July, no rulebook — a combination that keeps institutional money on the sidelines because it cannot underwrite legal uncertainty. By defining the perimeter, setting capital adequacy and formalising stablecoin and payment activity, Kenya converts an ambiguous market into a bankable one, and the queue of applicants (a two-year journey for Binance, a three-to-four-week filing plan for Yellow Card) is the proof that clarity pulls capital in. The design choice to tax the fee at 10% rather than the transaction is the tell: Nairobi is taxing the operator's margin, not the user's trade, which is friendlier to volume and consistent with a jurisdiction that wants the activity onshore where it can see it.

Nigeria taxes the trade and skips the licence; Kenya licenses the operator and taxes the fee. One is chasing revenue, the other is competing to be chosen.

The threads: to today's item 1 (Safaricom, Issue 060) — the same Central Bank of Kenya that capped the merchant fee on the mobile-money rail is now drawing the perimeter around the crypto rail, extending a coherent doctrine: as a rail matters, the state prices and bounds it, and does so before it becomes ungovernably systemic. To Issue 060's item 4 (PalmPay/OPay) — those were companies choosing their jurisdiction; this is a jurisdiction choosing to be chosen, the supply side of the same market for regulatory venue. To the running Kenya-regulation thread (the July VASP gazette, the earlier appeals-rights provision) and to Luno specifically (its 2026 restructuring and cuts) — the platforms racing to license are the ones betting on institutional, B2B, regulated rails rather than retail speculation. To the Chronos read on regulatory-arbitrage and structural design — it treats a clear, early regime as a durable competitive advantage for the market that writes it, and a risk for the ones that don't. The sceptic's ledger: “intends to apply” is not “has been licensed,” and implementation — how the CMA and CBK actually run the process — will decide whether Kenya becomes a hub or erects a moat only incumbents can clear; the capital floors (up to $2.3m for stablecoin issuers) plus a 10% fee excise could just as easily price out the local innovators the regime claims to want, handing the market to global majors; and a rules-first regime is only as good as its enforcement, which is unproven. The forward marker: the first licences actually granted — who clears the bar and who quietly withdraws will tell us whether Kenya has built a competitive digital-asset hub or a compliance wall, and whether rules-first genuinely beats Nigeria's revenue-first on the same continent.

Nigeria · Healthtech · Organisational Design Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Medwaka, a Nigerian emergency-response startup founded by Rest Essence, Dr Muyiwa Oluwatobi, Dr Glennise Ayuk and Abakar Mahamat, set out in 2024 to build a “911 for Nigeria” — a mobile app connecting people in medical emergencies to nearby hospitals, ambulances and first responders. It hit a wall the founders didn't control: ambulance infrastructure. Buying a fleet was beyond an early-stage startup, and relying on hospitals' own ambulances meant delays and inconsistent availability. So by “Day 1000” the team made a deliberate pivot — stop trying to run an independent 911 system it couldn't resource, and orchestrate the assets that already exist instead: private hospitals, HMOs, governments, emergency agencies, NGOs and community bodies. Today Medwaka runs a team of 20, has trained 11 first responders (recruited partly from local motorbike riders who reach patients before an ambulance can) and facilitated 550+ emergency requests through its platform and partner network.

Medwaka stopped trying to own the ambulances and started orchestrating the ones that exist — a pivot from asset to coordination layer

TechCabal · Day 1-1000 · Opeyemi Kareem · Sat 8 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a small healthtech founder-journey. The one that matters is a clean organisational-design lesson — when the capital-heavy asset (the ambulance fleet) is out of reach, the durable, buildable business is the coordination layer that routes demand across assets others already own. Medwaka chose to be the software and the network, not the fleet. It is the exact mirror of the week's other bets: where Moove and Spiro own the physical layer, Medwaka proves the other valid answer — orchestrate it — and it is the more capital-efficient path when you don't control the steel.

TechCabal — “How Medwaka rebuilt itself into an emergency response platform”

The structural read is a decision every asset-light platform eventually faces: build the expensive physical layer yourself, or become the intelligence that coordinates the physical layers others already run. Medwaka tried the first, discovered that ambulances are a balance-sheet problem it couldn't solve, and pivoted to the second — a coordination network stitching together private hospitals, HMOs, emergency agencies and neighbourhood motorbike responders, with the app as the routing brain. The move is quietly sophisticated: it trades the illusion of end-to-end control for a capital-efficient position on top of infrastructure that already exists, and it reframes the company from “we run emergencies” to “we make everyone else's emergency response faster.” The unglamorous truth of the “Day 1-1000” story is that the strategy pivot was forced by a resource constraint — which is how most durable organisational designs are actually discovered.

You don't have to own the ambulance to own the moment help arrives. Sometimes the buildable business is the layer that conducts the assets you'll never afford.

The threads: to Issue 060's item 2 (Moove) and today's item 1 (Spiro) — the sharpest own-versus-orchestrate contrast in the week: Moove and Spiro raise large sums precisely to own fleets and swap networks, while Medwaka, without that capital, proves the coordination layer is a legitimate and cheaper answer to the same “who controls the physical asset” question. To Issue 060's item 6 (Cloud9) and today's items 2 and 3 — all four are versions of “plug into what already exists rather than build it from zero,” whether the existing thing is a merchant base, a data trail, a messaging app or an ambulance. To the Chronos read on organisational and structural design — it prizes exactly this kind of honest constraint-driven pivot, and treats a capital-efficient coordination layer over incumbent assets as a defensible position when the asset itself is uneconomic to own. The sceptic's ledger: this is a very early, sub-scale company — 20 people, 11 responders, 550-odd requests — and a coordination layer with no control over the underlying ambulances inherits every delay and gap in the partners' fleets, which is the exact failure it was built to fix; “pivot to partnerships” can be a polite description of a startup that couldn't raise the capital its original vision required; and in emergency care, a routing app that can't guarantee an asset shows up is a liability as much as a service. The forward marker: whether the coordination model actually cuts response times at scale — if orchestrating existing assets provably gets help there faster and cheaper than owning a fleet would, Medwaka has found a real structural edge; if not, it is a smaller, safer business that quietly abandoned the problem worth solving.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Save your best prompts as templates — stop rewriting the same instruction from memory every time

The prompts that get the best work out of Claude are rarely one-liners — they carry your standards baked in: “lead with the conclusion, UK English, support with data, flag anything off-thesis, no corporate fluff.” Most people rebuild that scaffolding from memory each time, slightly differently, and get slightly different output. The fix is to treat a good prompt as a reusable asset: the first time you craft one that produces exactly the register and structure you want — a board-update summariser, a deal-memo critique, a “turn this transcript into actions” pass — save it verbatim somewhere you can paste from. Over a few weeks you accumulate a small library of proven instructions, and the quality stops depending on whether you remembered to specify the format that morning. The discipline is simple: when a prompt works unusually well, don't just admire the output — keep the prompt.

A worked example

Keep a single note called “Prompts that work.” When Claude nails a portfolio-update summary, paste the exact instruction you used under a heading: “Board pack → one-page update.” Add the ones that earn their place — “Red-team this thesis,” “Rewrite in my voice, no banned phrases,” “Extract every number and its source.” Next time, you paste the proven version and tweak one line, instead of reconstructing it from scratch and hoping. The library is the compounding asset; each good prompt you save is one you never have to re-derive.
