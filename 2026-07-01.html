Nigeria · Fintech · Structural Longitudinal
In its Monday Follow the Money column, TechCabal set out a structural shift: Nigeria’s largest fintechs are moving into banking through microfinance-bank (MFB) licences. In January, Paystack (now owned by The Stack Group) acquired Ladder MFB; in April, Flutterwave secured a national MFB licence via its acquisition of open-banking startup Mono; in May, Sycamore said it plans a deposit base above ₦40bn ($29.1m) after buying an MFB. An MFB licence lets a fintech take deposits, lend, and earn interest income — but a microfinance bank is not a commercial bank. The Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) requires savings to be ≥60% of the funding base, a loan-to-deposit ratio near 80%, net loans ≥60% of assets, ~80% of lending in microloans (max ₦1m for national and state MFBs), 100% provisioning on classified loans, a capital-adequacy ratio ≥10%, ≥20% liquid assets, treasury bills capped at 5–10%, ~80% of gross income from interest, and opex below 15% of assets. FX and international transfers are prohibited. National MFBs need ₦5bn ($3.62m) of paid-up capital — against ₦500bn for an international commercial bank. Flutterwave CEO Olugbenga Agboola: “$40 billion has gone through our platform… not one cent was retained. With this new phase… money now stays in our platform. Margins get better.” Moniepoint processed ₦412tn ($297bn) in 2025 and financed more than ₦1tn ($724m) in loans.

Nigeria’s fintechs built payments — now they’re becoming banks, on the regulator’s terms

TechCabal (Temitayo Jaiyeola) · Follow the Money · Mon 29 Jun 2026

The pivot everyone can see — Paystack, Flutterwave, Moniepoint, Sycamore and Kuda swapping light-touch payments licences for microfinance-bank licences — hides the more interesting part: what the licence forces them to become. Payments firms earn when money moves; banks earn when money sits. But the CBN’s rulebook is so prescriptive it reads like an operating model: four-fifths of deposits must be lent, four-fifths of that in capped microloans, four-fifths of income must come from interest, opex must stay below 15%, and foreign exchange is off-limits. The deposit base is the prize; the rulebook is the price. Tuesday’s lesson, restated: owning the layer is profitable, but the sovereign writes the terms on which you own it.

A microfinance-bank licence hands a fintech a deposit base — and a rulebook that tells it, almost line by line, what it must become
TechCabal — “Nigeria’s fintechs built payments. Now they’re becoming banks.”

Tuesday closed on Optasia — a company that genuinely owns a lending layer and is now living through the part of the thesis where the state takes an interest in who controls it. Today the rest of the Nigerian fintech field walks toward the same regulator on purpose. The MFB licence is the door into deposit economics, and every one of them wants through it.

“$40 billion has gone through our platform… not one cent was retained. With this new phase of life, money now stays in our platform. Margins get better.”

The economics are genuinely better: a deposit base is permanent funding, and the same naira can be lent, repaid and lent again, compounding interest income in a way a per-transaction fee never does. But the CBN has priced the upgrade carefully. The guidelines don’t merely permit deposit-taking; they dictate its use — 80% lent out, most of it in microloans under ₦1m, 100% provisioning on bad loans, opex capped, treasury holdings capped, no FX. This is a regulator that has decided a microfinance bank is a development instrument, not a balance sheet to be optimised.

For Chronos this is worth holding on three counts. First, it is a clean read on the value of a licence: the market keeps pricing the deposit base as pure upside, but the CBN’s rulebook is a set of binding constraints on ROE — the interesting diligence question on any of these names is not “how big is the deposit book” but “what return can it earn under an 80% loan-to-deposit floor and a 15% opex ceiling.” Second, it sharpens the through-line from Optasia: in African financial services the decisive actor is rarely the competitor and almost always the regulator, and here the regulator is effectively the product manager — deciding what the product must do, to whom, and at what margin. Third, the read-across to our own book: the same instinct that made a payments company want a banking licence is the one worth interrogating whenever a portfolio business proposes to move up the stack — the new layer comes with a new supervisor, and the supervisor’s objectives may not be the shareholder’s. The thread to carry forward: name the layer a business wants to own, then read, line by line, the terms on which the state will let it.

Africa · China · Monetary Sovereignty Longitudinal
On 26 June, China’s central bank, the People’s Bank of China, named two lenders to run yuan clearing across Africa: Standard Bank, the continent’s biggest bank by assets, and ICBC, the world’s biggest. Together they will operate the “Renminbi Clearing Bank of Africa” — the first yuan clearing hub built for an entire continent (most exist for a single city such as London or Singapore) and the first jointly run by two commercial banks. It covers 19 countries and lets African firms pay Chinese suppliers directly in yuan instead of buying US dollars first. It follows Standard Bank’s November 2025 admission to CIPS, China’s SWIFT alternative — the first African lender to join — through which it processed ~$500m in trade transactions in four months. China-Africa trade hit a record $348bn in 2025 (+18%); China is now ~20% of Africa’s trade with the world, up from 5% two decades ago (Afreximbank), and on 1 May Beijing scrapped tariffs on goods from 53 African countries. ICBC has owned ~20% of Standard Bank since 2007. Ecobank (35 markets) is in advanced talks with Bank of China. The yuan is not freely convertible. The deeper shift is in government debt: Kenya converted $3.5bn of Chinese loans into yuan in late 2025 (~$215m/yr saved); Ethiopia is in talks; Zambia is accepting yuan for mining royalties. A third player is moving too — the UAE is signing currency deals with Egypt, Ethiopia, Kenya and Nigeria — as AGOA is renewed only to end-2026.

Beijing hands Standard Bank the keys to Africa’s yuan — a quiet crack in the dollar’s grip

Ecofin Agency (Idriss Linge) · Finance · Mon 29 Jun 2026

Tuesday’s Kenya item revealed a “de-dollarisation” that was really a debt restructuring; today the plumbing beneath that story gets switched on for a whole continent. A continent-wide yuan clearing hub is a modest thing on the surface — faster, cheaper payments for importers — and a significant one underneath: the first real alternative to routing African-Chinese trade through the dollar. But the “African win” is tangled: the winning bank is one-fifth Chinese-owned and holds the role on licence, Beijing could grant the same status to a rival tomorrow, and the yuan stays capped as long as China keeps its capital controls. The signal to watch is not this appointment but the second one.

Ecofin Agency — “China hands Standard Bank the keys to the yuan in Africa”

Tuesday, AidData taught us to read the terms rather than the framing, and to notice who holds the accurate version of a record everyone else misreads. Today’s item is the same lesson one layer down: the debt swap Kenya dressed up as “de-dollarisation” needs rails to run on, and Beijing has just built them — a settlement system that quietly ties African trade and, more importantly, African public debt to the renminbi.

“The clearest sign to watch is the day a second African bank wins the same clearing status Standard Bank just got. That is when an exception becomes a system.”

The reporting is admirably unsentimental about the “African champion” narrative. Standard Bank is one-fifth owned by ICBC, holds the clearing role on licence, and already faces Ecobank moving toward Bank of China. The advantage is real but rented — a head start, not a moat.

For Chronos this matters as a lens on the macro backdrop for everything we hold on the continent, on three counts. First, cost and rails: if a growing share of African trade and sovereign debt migrates to yuan, the currency composition of African balance sheets — and the FX risk embedded in local funding — starts to shift under the reported numbers, exactly as Tuesday’s AidData piece warned. Second, the multipolar point: the contest is usually framed as Washington versus Beijing, but with the UAE cutting its own currency deals and AGOA expiring, African governments are gaining optionality and leverage — good for them, more complex for anyone underwriting long-duration African assets. Third, the discipline: the article’s tell — watch for the second clearing bank — is precisely the kind of leading indicator worth wiring into how we track the de-dollarisation thread from here. The thread from Tuesday to today: the debt swap was the trailer; the clearing hub is the feature.

South Africa · AI · Markets Chart
Natale Labia — partner and chief economist of a global investment firm, writing in his personal capacity — argues that AI is inverting how technology booms usually work. Past booms broadened as adoption spread; AI, the longer it runs, is concentrating gains among a narrow set of winners in a zero-sum dynamic. A country’s equity performance now tracks its place in the AI “stack”: the US and China at the top (foundational models); Taiwan and South Korea in hardware; Japan and Israel adjacent; secondary suppliers Malaysia, Singapore, Vietnam and Thailand; and then everyone else — most of Europe (bar the Netherlands and ASML), and India, now seen as on the wrong side of the disruption trade (the Nifty 50 is down almost 10% over a year while the Kospi is up 174%). AI-related companies are now ~45% of the S&P 500’s market cap and ~62% of its performance since mid-2024 (Barclays), versus ~30% weighting and ~60% of gains at the dot-com peak. South Africa sits squarely in the last group: the JSE has been pedestrian, its only partial exception copper miners as a secondary data-centre play. Labia notes early cracks — the Magnificent 7 foundering since mid-May while the index held, a semiconductor “chip wreck” (Samsung and SK Hynix crashing, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index sideways since June), and leadership rotating into banks, industrials, staples, insurers and pharma. He frames the post-pandemic years as a relay of speculative manias — crypto, NFTs, meme stocks, precious metals, the Mag 7, semis — each running out of steam, and expects AI, like the railroads and the dot-com boom, to deliver real structural change only after the frenzy breaks.

Winner takes all in the AI economy — and South Africa wasn’t dealt a hand

Daily Maverick (Natale Labia) · Opinionista · Tue 30 Jun 2026

The world’s markets have spent a year sorting themselves by position in the AI stack, and South Africa isn’t on the ladder. Labia’s argument is sharper than “AI is a bubble”: this boom is zero-sum, sucking capital out of everything that isn’t AI (the “SaaSpocalypse,” the hollowing of media and recruiting) rather than broadening as past booms did. The JSE has no AI beta beyond copper miners, which is a real problem for a market that badly needs a growth narrative. His hedge is historical: railroads and dot-com both collapsed before their real structural gains arrived — and the first cracks (a foundering Mag 7, a semiconductor “chip wreck”) may already be here.

A year of returns maps almost perfectly onto the AI “stack” — and South Africa’s market has no rung on the ladder
Daily Maverick — “Winner takes all in the AI economy, and SA wasn’t invited”

Tuesday, Bilello watched an AI equity bubble mutate into a credit bubble and warned that the strain would show in the internals. Today Labia, reading the same market from Johannesburg, finds the internals already cracking — the Mag 7 stalling, a semiconductor “chip wreck,” leadership fleeing into unglamorous banks and staples — and adds the part that lands closest to home: the country Chronos is built in has no seat at this table at all.

“For countries such as South Africa, which lacks the industrial base and energy infrastructure needed for AI investment, the boom might as well be on another planet.”

The uncomfortable read for a Stellenbosch-based, Africa-weighted book is twofold. The discount rate on everything we own is increasingly set by a trade we cannot join — and our home equity market offers no offsetting exposure to it. When 62% of the world’s largest index’s gains come from AI and the JSE’s only proxy is copper miners, “diversified into South Africa” and “diversified away from AI” become the same sentence.

For Chronos three things are worth holding. First, Labia’s zero-sum claim is the one to test, not assume: if AI capital is genuinely being drained from other sectors rather than added on top, then the non-AI businesses we back globally face a capital headwind that has nothing to do with their own execution — a real risk to weigh, but also, for a patient buyer, the source of the mispricing. Second, the fragility signal rhymes with Bilello: violently narrow leadership and a semis complex already wobbling are the classic late-stage tells, and a book with no AI beta is, perversely, less exposed to the unwind than the crowded trade is. Third, the structural consolation — railroads and dot-com both delivered their real gains after the speculative capital was washed out — is exactly the window a firm like ours is built to exploit, provided we have the balance-sheet patience to be buying when the manias that Labia lists finally run their course. The thread to carry: we don’t need a view on Nvidia to need a view on what an AI unwind does to the cost of capital in the one market we can’t diversify out of.

DR Congo · Macro · FX Wouldn't have seen otherwise
The IMF cleared the Democratic Republic of Congo’s program review on 26 June, praising a sharp disinflation: inflation averaged 17.7% in 2024 and was down to 2.5% by end-April 2026, with an appreciating Congolese franc (rising sharply from October 2025) doing most of the work by pulling down import prices. The same currency did damage elsewhere. Broad money shrank 9.6% in 2025 (the IMF had projected ~4% growth); net foreign assets fell 12.9%; domestic credit rose just 1.2% against a 7.7% forecast. The Central Bank of the Congo held its policy rate at 25% until October 2025, then cut to 13.5% in April 2026 (S&P Global). Exports of goods and services fell from 45.9% of GDP in 2024 to 39.8% in 2025. Kinshasa banned cobalt exports in February 2025, then switched to quotas capping shipments at 96,600 tonnes for 2026–27 (about half of 2024 volumes); cobalt has jumped ~160% from its early-2025 low to more than $56,000 a tonne, but industry sources say less than half the quota actually shipped, held up by logistics. Copper fell 17% year-on-year over the first nine months of 2025 after a seismic incident at the giant Kamoa-Kakula mine. The government raised a $1.25bn debut Eurobond in April (yields of 8.75% and 9.5%, four times oversubscribed) on debt worth only ~20% of GDP. The IMF projects end-2026 inflation back at 6.9%.

DR Congo beat inflation with a strong franc — and is now strangling the exporters meant to save it

Ecofin Agency (Idriss Linge) · Finance · Tue 30 Jun 2026

A textbook illustration of the cost hidden behind a “good” macro headline. The strong franc that the IMF applauded for crushing inflation to 2.5% is the same franc that shrank the money supply nearly 10%, left domestic credit essentially flat, and cut the local-currency value of every non-mining exporter’s receipts — the precise firms Congo needs to diversify away from cobalt and copper. Two forces are compressing export value at once: the currency, and the state’s own decision to throttle its most famous export with cobalt quotas. Price stability and market access, bought with the growth of the private sector meant to sustain them.

Ecofin Agency — “DR Congo’s Strong Franc Beat Inflation. Now It Is Squeezing Exporters and Borrowers.”

This is Tuesday’s AidData discipline — read the terms, not the framing — applied to a central-bank success story. The IMF’s praise is real and the disinflation is real; the question the headline skips is who paid for it. The answer is the small and mid-sized firms living on bank credit that barely grew, and the non-mining exporters whose dollar receipts a strong franc quietly shrinks.

“A disinflation built on a strong currency unwinds when the currency does.”

The bind is structural, not accidental. About 40% of Congo’s public revenue comes from the dollar-priced mining sector, so the state carries a hedge that its exporters and borrowers do not — and the IMF is asking it to use the exchange rate as a shock absorber and diversify exports at the same time, two goals that fight each other when the currency is this strong.

For Chronos the relevance is a lens, not a trade, on two counts. First, it is a reminder that African macro “wins” deserve the same forensic reading we give a portfolio company’s recap: a disinflation resting on a strong currency and a 25%-then-13.5% policy rate is a tightening in disguise, and the credit squeeze it produces is exactly the environment in which good local businesses get starved of funding regardless of their own quality. Second, the reversal risk is the part to hold: the IMF itself expects inflation back near 7% by year-end, which means the currency strength underpinning today’s stability is not a permanent feature but a phase — and the firms betting on cheap imports and a strong franc are the ones most exposed when it turns. Worth reading as a compact case study in how a celebrated stabilisation can hollow out the private sector it is meant to protect.

Asia · Fintech · AI Deployment AI for the portfolio
Airwallex, the Australia-headquartered cross-border payments fintech, has raised a $320m Series H at an $11bn valuation (led by Addition, the fund of former Tiger Global executive Lee Fixel; with Baillie Gifford, Hummingbird, QED Investors, T. Rowe Price, Hedosophia, Haun Ventures and Amex Ventures). More notable than the money is the product. The company launched T0, an AI-powered “full financial department” for startups — bookkeeping, forecasting, taxes, compliance and reporting, pitched as giving founders “CFO-grade books with compliance built in and no migration required.” It also launched Airi, described as its first agentic wallet, letting companies sell products and services to customers via AI agents (with permission controls, spend limits and multi-currency options to follow). CEO and co-founder Jack Zhang says “AI is the greatest entrepreneurship unlock we’ve ever seen” and that solo founders can now run companies. Airwallex is delaying a loosely planned 2026 IPO, telling the Australian Financial Review that its AI spending makes its financials ill-suited to public markets. It says it has been profitable since 2023, with $1.3bn ARR (up 74% year-on-year) and $287bn in annualised transaction volume (up more than 120%).

Airwallex bets an AI finance department is worth more than an IPO — and raises at $11bn to prove it

Asia Tech Review (Jon Russell) · Fri 26 Jun 2026

Read this one not for Airwallex the company but for what its new product implies about a cost curve. T0 turns the finance function — bookkeeping, tax, compliance, reporting — into an AI-run service a founder switches on, no CFO and no migration required. If that works even partially, the back-office headcount assumptions baked into every growth model bend. There are two tells worth noting alongside the launch: a profitable fintech is choosing AI capex over an IPO it could plausibly do, and it is doing so while claiming 74% ARR growth. This is the “AI deployment” story from the operator’s side, not the chip-maker’s.

Asia Tech Review — “Airwallex dives deep into AI services and is now worth $11 billion”

Tuesday, Tala showed what happens when a company becomes infrastructure: fewer people, more centralised. Airwallex is the next turn of that screw — the finance department itself repackaged as software. Where Tala moved the value-adding work somewhere cheaper, T0 proposes to remove a chunk of it entirely. For a portfolio of operating companies, that is the more consequential move.

“AI is the greatest entrepreneurship unlock we’ve ever seen.” — Jack Zhang, Airwallex CEO

The claim deserves the sceptic’s eye we’d apply to any founder selling his own tool: T0 is a launch, not a track record, Airwallex carries well-documented China-linkage questions, and “CFO-grade books, no migration” is a promise the accounting will have to keep. But the direction is the point, and it is portfolio-relevant even if this specific product disappoints.

For Chronos three reads. First, the operating one: if AI-run finance functions become real, the SG&A lines in our models — and in those of the businesses we back, from TymeBank to rain to Optasia — are more compressible than the base cases assume, which cuts both ways: better margins for the disciplined, and a competitive floor that drops for everyone. Second, the capital-allocation tell: a profitable, fast-growing fintech is choosing to spend on AI rather than list, explicitly because the spend spoils the near-term financials — the same equity-to-something rotation Bilello flagged on Tuesday, except undertaken voluntarily and framed as offence, not distress. Third, the diligence prompt: when a portfolio company tells us AI will lower its cost to serve, T0 is the concrete thing to benchmark the claim against — a shipped product with real numbers behind it, rather than a slide. Read it as a data point on where the finance-function cost curve is heading, and hold the founder’s enthusiasm at arm’s length.

Africa · Capital Markets · Structural Wouldn't have seen otherwise
In an Ecofin Agency interview, Yezdan Akcacakir — Director and Head of the Energy Sector at the Africa Finance Corporation (AFC) — sets out the thesis that Africa is “not capital-poor, it is capital-trapped.” She puts investable African domestic capital — pensions, insurance, sovereign wealth funds, banks and reserves — at ~$4.4 trillion, with pension and insurance assets together topping $1 trillion for the first time in 2025. The worked example is Poro Power: the first dual-currency, project-finance green bond in Côte d’Ivoire and the WAEMU zone — a €65m bi-currency facility (AFC anchoring the €43m euro tranche as co-arranger; regional banks and asset managers fully subscribing the €22m XOF tranche) financing a 66MW solar plant, at financial close in February 2026, with commercial operation targeted for April 2027. Context she cites: Official Development Assistance fell 23.1% in 2025 (the largest annual drop on record), forcing a re-anchoring on domestic capital; Nigeria’s InfraCredit (local-currency credit enhancement) is being replicated via Dhamana Guarantee in Kenya; pension regulators in Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, Namibia and Botswana are widening infrastructure mandates. On the geopolitics: Gulf capital (Saudi Arabia’s Vision Invest into AFC’s ARISE platforms) is becoming a structural, “multipolar” source of patient capital. AFC has also moved into technology venture — a $100m commitment anchoring Lightrock Africa II and Future Africa Fund III — and closed a $2bn syndicated loan, its largest ever. By 2050, Africa will have ~2.5 billion people.

“Africa isn’t capital-poor, it’s capital-trapped”: the AFC’s case for the $4.4-trillion pool nobody mobilises

Ecofin Agency (interview by Idriss Linge) · Finance · Tue 30 Jun 2026

The week’s most useful reframing for a firm that deploys capital in Africa. Akcacakir’s claim is that the binding constraint is not the amount of African capital — ~$4.4 trillion sits in pensions, insurers, sovereign funds and banks — but the machinery to channel it into African assets, which has never existed at scale. Poro Power is her proof of concept: a credit-enhanced, dual-currency structure that finally pulled West African pension and bank liquidity out of low-yield sovereign paper and into an actual power plant. Layer on collapsing aid, a multipolar arrival of Gulf capital, and a DFI now anchoring African tech VC, and the shape of a genuinely different African capital market starts to appear.

Everything else in today’s edition — fintechs chasing deposits, Beijing supplying settlement rails, a strong franc choking local credit — is a symptom of the same disease this interview names.

Ecofin Agency — “Africa Is Not Capital Poor, It Is Capital Trapped”

If there is one piece in today’s edition to read in full, it is this. The whole week has circled a single question — who owns the terms, the rails, the record — and Akcacakir reframes it as a capital question: African savings that cannot find African assets. The number ($4.4 trillion) is arresting, but the mechanism is the substance: a development-finance institution using its balance sheet as a credit wrapper so that domestic pension and bank money can take infrastructure risk it was structurally unable to price on its own.

“Africa is not capital-poor; it is capital-trapped.”

Read her with the appropriate discount — this is an AFC executive making AFC’s case, the $4.4 trillion is a theoretical pool not a committed one, and the regulatory unlocks she credits are gradual and reversible. But the structural claims are checkable and they matter to us directly.

For Chronos three threads. First, the InfraCredit / Dhamana credit-enhancement model is the most transferable idea in the piece: turning African infrastructure into an investment-grade asset class for local institutions is precisely the kind of structuring that widens the buyer base for anything we might later sell, and worth understanding deal by deal. Second, the multipolar-capital point — Gulf money entering platforms as patient equity while Western aid retreats and Chinese lending narrows — is the same three-sided contest we saw in today’s yuan item, now viewed from the equity side; it changes who our co-investors and eventual buyers are likely to be this decade. Third, the tell worth marking: a hard-infrastructure DFI is now anchoring African technology venture funds (Lightrock, Future Africa), which says the institutional definition of “infrastructure” is expanding to include the digital layer — and that patient, structural capital is moving toward exactly the intersection of technology and Africa where Chronos operates. The thread that ties the week together: owning the terms is one thing, but the deeper prize is building the machinery that lets African capital finance African assets — and that machinery is, slowly, being assembled.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Save your best prompts — stop rewriting the same instruction every week

Most of us re-describe the same task to the model over and over: “summarise this deal, lead with the conclusion, three risks, no fluff.” The unglamorous productivity unlock is to write that instruction once, properly, and keep it — in a note, a doc, wherever you’ll find it — then paste it whenever the task recurs. A good saved prompt is a small piece of institutional memory: it encodes what “done well” looks like for a job you do often, so the quality doesn’t depend on how carefully you happened to phrase it that morning. The partners who get the most from these tools aren’t writing cleverer prompts each time — they’re reusing three or four that already work.

A worked example

Keep a running “prompt drawer” — a single note with your five most-used instructions, each with a one-line title. For a portfolio read: “You are my analyst. Summarise the attached in ≤200 words: lead with the one conclusion that matters, then the three things that would change my mind, then the single number I should check myself. UK English, no preamble, no recommendations — I’ll decide.” Paste it, attach the document, done. When a result is especially good, don’t just use it — stop and fold whatever made it good back into the saved version. Over a few weeks the drawer becomes the most valuable AI asset you own, because it’s your taste, written down.
