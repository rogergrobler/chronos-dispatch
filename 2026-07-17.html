South Africa · Connectivity · Strategy Analysis Wouldn't-have-seen-otherwise
Amazon Leo (formerly Project Kuiper) will launch satellite broadband in South Africa in 2027 — not by applying for its own licence, but by partnering with Herotel, one of the country's largest fixed-internet operators, which already holds the electronic-communications licence and will handle installation, support and sales under a new brand, evry. The contrast is the whole story: Starlink has been unable to launch commercially in South Africa after refusing to meet the 30% historically-disadvantaged-ownership requirement. Amazon looked at the identical rulebook and chose to enter through the existing ecosystem instead of trying to change it. Veteran analyst Arthur Goldstuck of World Wide Worx: “Amazon has really shown the way foreign operators should operate in South Africa — rather than literally dropping into the country, they are entering through the existing ecosystem,” strengthening operators already on the ground the way AWS sits behind other companies rather than competing with them.

Amazon found the formula for entering South Africa that Starlink refused

TechCabal · Phathisani Moyo · Thu 16 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is yesterday's “a licence is not a business” lesson inverted — where the licence is not abundant but deliberately closed, the winning move is not to fight the rule for years (Starlink's approach, still stuck) but to rent the balance-sheet-and-permit of someone who already cleared it. And it is the cleanest recent example of the AWS pattern applied to a regulated market: don't be the retail brand the customer buys, be the infrastructure layer sitting behind whoever they already buy from — Herotel's 350,000 customers across 550-plus towns become Amazon's distribution without Amazon building a single storefront.

TechCabal — “Amazon found the formula for entering South Africa that Starlink refused”

Two global giants, one rulebook, opposite strategies. Starlink treats South Africa's 30% local-ownership requirement as an obstacle to be litigated and lobbied away, and has been shut out of commercial launch as a result. Amazon treats it as a fact of the terrain and routes around it — supplying the satellites while a licensed local partner owns the customer relationship, the regulatory exposure and the last mile. The lesson generalises well beyond satellites: in markets where the permit is the scarce asset, the fastest route in is not to acquire the permit but to acquire access to someone who holds it, and to be the layer they can't replicate rather than the brand they compete with.

“Amazon is avoiding the battle altogether by strengthening the offerings of operators already on the ground. If Amazon Leo succeeds, its biggest advantage won't be its satellites — it'll be the decision to work with local partners rather than challenge the rules.”

For a firm that assesses African market entrants for a living, the read carries two ways. It reframes what a “moat” is in a regulated market: the licensed local operator — unglamorous, sub-scale, often overlooked — turns out to be the chokepoint that even Amazon has to rent, which is an argument for owning the boring permitted rails rather than the flashy consumer brand on top. And it reframes the risk in any “global platform will disrupt this market” thesis: the disruptor may not arrive as a competitor to the incumbents at all, but as a supplier that quietly makes itself indispensable underneath them. The counter-case worth holding: the partner who holds the licence also holds the leverage, and Goldstuck notes Starlink has used exactly this local-partner model elsewhere — so the arrangement that looks like Amazon's masterstroke today could become Herotel's pricing power tomorrow, if the satellite layer ever commoditises faster than the distribution layer does.

Nigeria · Telecoms · Reinvention Briefing Structural design
ntel — formerly NITEL, the state monopoly, acquired by the NATCOM consortium for $252m in 2015 and now backed through its turnaround by creditor AMCON — relaunched this week as a digital-infrastructure company rather than a mobile operator. Its bet: make more money leasing the towers, fibre, power and real estate behind the network than from selling the phone calls that run over them. It has reorganised into three units — Beam (the residual telecoms and a fixed-wireless product), Titan (commercialising 600-plus towers, 3,500-plus km of fibre and its power assets), and Eden (real estate in Lagos, Abuja and Port Harcourt). The move tracks a global shift — as WhatsApp, Zoom and FaceTime hollow out call revenue, operators monetise infrastructure instead — and an African one: MTN spun out Bayobab for its 100,000km of fibre, sold-and-leased-back 5,709 towers to IHS for ~$412m and is now buying IHS outright; Airtel has sold thousands of towers to Helios and ATC.

Nigeria's ntel gives up phone calls to become the landlord of the network

TechCabal · Frank Eleanya · Tue 15 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the “own the rail, not the retail” doctrine as a full corporate reinvention — a failed consumer operator concluding that its only durable assets were always the passive layer beneath the brand, and restructuring itself into a landlord of towers, fibre and power. And it is a clean marker of where value has migrated across African telecoms: the operators keep separating the infrastructure from the service precisely because the infrastructure is the part with pricing power, long contracts and scarcity — the same instinct that has MTN buying IHS and Airtel selling towers to specialists.

TechCabal — “Why Nigeria's ntel is pivoting from telecom operator to infrastructure company”

Read ntel against today's lead and the diptych is deliberate. Amazon rents the licensed operator to reach the customer; ntel gives up reaching the customer altogether and becomes the thing operators rent. Both are voting for the same layer — the passive infrastructure and permits underneath the retail relationship — as the place where durable economics actually sit. ntel is doing it from weakness, not strength: this is a distressed, AMCON-controlled ex-monopoly with a long history of failure, betting that a landlord's cash flows are more defensible than a challenger's subscriber growth. The bull read is that the assets are genuinely scarce and long-lived — 600 towers, 3,500km of fibre, prime real estate — and the tower-co model has repeatedly created value across Africa.

“Its future lies less in selling phone calls than in leasing the infrastructure behind them — joining a growing trend of operators making more money from the networks they own than from the calls they carry.”

The scepticism is equally concrete. Tower-cos and fibre-cos are capital-intensive, and the value in African infrastructure has tended to accrue to the specialists with scale and cheap capital — IHS, ATC, Helios — not to sub-scale operators bolting a landlord unit onto a struggling telco. A relaunch announcement and three brand names are not yet a wholesale business; the test is whether ntel can sign the anchor tenants (the MTNs and Airtels) who would rent its rail, in a market where those very tenants increasingly own their own tower-cos. The forward question we'd carry into any African infrastructure case: is the durable position in owning the passive assets, or in being the scaled, low-cost-of-capital consolidator that everyone else eventually has to sell those assets to — and which of those is actually on offer in the deal in front of us?

Africa · Energy · AI Analysis World's-best-thinkers
Schneider Electric's East Africa president Ifeanyi Odoh argues Africa is preparing for the AI economy by asking the wrong question — obsessing over generating more electricity when the real constraint is rebuilding grids designed for the last century to carry AI data centres, EVs and distributed renewables. The numbers behind the argument: a conventional server rack draws 5–15kW; an AI rack already draws 40–120kW, with next-generation systems approaching 200kW per cabinet, running continuously rather than in peaks. Africa holds roughly 360MW of live data-centre capacity across 217 facilities in 33 countries — under 1% of global capacity. Schneider says it already powers 38% of Africa's installed data-centre capacity (about half of East Africa's) and wants to become the operating system beneath the continent's AI infrastructure — medium-voltage equipment, automation, liquid cooling — on the thesis that countries which modernise their grids first will attract the next wave of AI investment.

Schneider's bet: Africa's electricity grid is the next AI battleground

TechCabal · Frank Eleanya · Thu 16 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is Simon Taylor's “compute is a third form of capital” taken one layer lower and onto our continent — in Africa the binding constraint on compute is not chips or models but power, and whoever rebuilds the grid decides where the AI investment is physically allowed to land. And it is a rare piece that maps the actual African AI-infrastructure landscape in capital-allocation terms: three markets, three strategies — South Africa on grid stability, Kenya on geothermal renewables, Nigeria on a “gas-to-compute” workaround — each solving the same electricity problem differently, and each a different investable shape.

The AI economy runs on watts — and Africa is starting from under 1% of the world's compute power
TechCabal — “Schneider Electric says Africa's electricity grid is the next AI battleground”

Yesterday's closer ended on Taylor's line that the operators who win the next decade allocate a third kind of capital — compute — on purpose. Schneider's argument is the uncomfortable African footnote to that: you cannot allocate compute you cannot power. An AI rack drawing up to ten times a conventional one, running continuously, changes not just how much electricity a data centre needs but where — the facilities are moving into cities, next to the load, and in Kenya's case literally beside the geothermal plants. On a continent with under 1% of global data-centre capacity and grids built for a different century, power is the gating input to the entire AI story, which is why Schneider is trying to reposition itself from equipment supplier to the “operating system” underneath the build-out.

“The traditional grid as we know it is already becoming obsolete. The whole digital economy can only be made possible if we address the underlying electrification need — energy is the enabler for everything else.”

The three-country split is the part worth carrying into any African AI or infrastructure thesis. South Africa has become the hyperscaler destination on the back of 400-plus days without load-shedding and power-wheeling reform — Microsoft has already committed R5.4bn ($300m). Kenya competes on clean power, siting AI campuses beside geothermal (Microsoft's planned $1bn campus with G42). Nigeria has the largest digital market but its weakest grid — 13.6GW installed, only ~4.3GW consistently available — forcing a “gas-to-compute” model of captive power built into the data centre itself. Each is a different investable object: grid-stability plays in SA, renewables-adjacency in Kenya, behind-the-meter generation in Nigeria. The forward question we'd carry: in the African AI build-out, is the durable return in the compute layer everyone is talking about, or in the deeply unfashionable layer beneath it — generation, grid, cooling, behind-the-meter power — where the scarcity actually binds and the contracts actually last? The caution: incumbents like Schneider are talking their own book, and grid modernisation across 33 countries is a decade of capex and politics, not a trade.

Kenya · Payments · Infrastructure Briefing Longitudinal
Paystack (the Stripe-owned African payments company) has extended its partnership with Pesalink — Kenya's interbank transfer network, which connects more than 80 banks and co-operatives — so Kenyan businesses can now accept Pesalink bank transfers directly at Paystack checkout, not just receive merchant settlements over it. The point is the framing: as TechCabal puts it, fintechs are increasingly competing on payment infrastructure rather than payment methods — not adding another checkout button, but putting collection, confirmation, settlement and reconciliation on a single rail so there is no operational friction between a customer paying and a business getting the money. The same piece notes Paystack's wider pattern: Capitec Pay in South Africa, and a June pilot letting Nigerian customers complete purchases through AI agents like Claude and ChatGPT, on the bet that agents are becoming a new interface for commerce.

Paystack stops competing on payment methods and competes on the rail itself

TechCabal · Kenn Abuya · Thu 16 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the private-sector twin of Rwanda's eKash from yesterday — the same conclusion that the durable position is the rail, not the payment method sitting on it, reached by a fintech rather than a state. And the buried lede is the one that matters most to our portfolio: Paystack is already piloting checkout completed by AI agents (Claude, ChatGPT), which means the “own the rail” thesis and the “agentic commerce” thesis are colliding on the same African infrastructure, a year earlier than most would guess.

TechCabal — “Paystack expands Pesalink partnership to bring bank transfers into checkout”

The explicit line — fintechs competing on infrastructure rather than methods — is the thesis this page has been circling for weeks, now stated in the source itself. Klump (Issue 044) slid instalments into checkout without owning the loan; eKash (Issue 045) nationalised the interoperability rail; Paystack here puts collection and settlement on one pipe so the merchant never feels the seam. Each is a bet that the payment method is a commodity and the rail is the moat — and Pesalink, connecting 80-plus banks and now plugged into the pan-African PAPSS network for cross-border shilling transfers, is exactly the kind of shared rail the value is migrating toward.

“Providers are trying to eliminate operational friction after a customer pays — connecting collection, confirmation, settlement and reconciliation into a single workflow. The competition is on infrastructure, not payment method.”

The part to carry into every fintech case is the agentic pilot sitting quietly at the end of the piece. Paystack's Nigerian early-access lets customers complete purchases through Claude and ChatGPT, built on its Zap transfer product, on the explicit bet that “AI agents are becoming a new interface for commerce, where people move from asking questions to completing real tasks.” That is Ramp's agent-cards item from Issue 045 arriving on the consumer side, in Africa, on rails a portfolio company could plausibly plug into. The bull case: whoever owns the checkout rail when the buyer becomes an agent owns a structurally new distribution channel. The scepticism: rails compress to near-zero margin precisely because they win on ubiquity, and Paystack is one integration among many on a Pesalink it does not own — so the durable value may sit not with the fintech at the front but with the shared interbank utility underneath, or with whoever ends up owning the agent the customer actually trusts. The forward question: when the buyer is a model, does the merchant's edge move to the rail, the data, or the agent — and which of those can a Chronos portfolio company realistically own?

Kenya · Regulation · Consumer Credit Analysis Thesis-test
The sceptic's follow-up to yesterday's optimism. The Competition Authority of Kenya's annual report shows complaints against digital lenders climbed to 355 in the year to June 2025, from 67 a year earlier — making digital lenders the single largest source of consumer complaints in financial services, roughly two-thirds of the sector's grievances. Financial services accounted for 564 of 915 total complaints (61.6%). This is the same regulated market the Central Bank celebrated this week in taking licensed digital lenders to 252 (from zero in 2022). The complaints were largely misleading representations, undisclosed charges and unilateral changes to loan terms — in one case a KES 177,720 ($1,375) loan that ballooned to KES 500,000 ($3,869) in added charges before the authority intervened. Licensing built the market; it has not yet fixed the conduct inside it.

Kenya licensed 252 digital lenders — and complaints against them still quintupled

TechCabal · Adonijah Ndege · Thu 16 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is a direct probe of a thesis the partners have watched build across editions — that a licensing regime turns a predatory grey market into a governed one — and the data says the story is only half true: the market is now vetted, but the borrower-level harm rose fivefold in the same window. And it is a useful discipline on every “regulation as market-making” item we run: a licence governs who is allowed in the front door; it does not, on this evidence, yet govern how they behave once inside, which is a different and harder problem.

The market got licensed; the complaints quintupled — vetting the entrants is not the same as governing them
TechCabal — “Digital lenders top Kenya's consumer complaint list despite reforms”

Run this exactly against yesterday's item and the pair is the whole point of a longitudinal service. Issue 045 celebrated the CBK conjuring a licensed category out of a legal vacuum — 252 vetted lenders where there were none, 8.3 million loans, $1.16bn. Today's data, from a different regulator (the Competition Authority) reading the same market, says complaints against those lenders rose from 67 to 355 in a year, and digital lending is now the single largest source of consumer grievances in the country's financial system. Both are true. The synthesis is the useful part: licensing solved the entry problem — who is allowed to lend — without yet solving the conduct problem — how they treat the borrower once licensed. Misleading terms, hidden charges and unilateral repricing are conduct failures, and a fit-and-proper test at the door does not catch them.

“The latest figures suggest the licensing regime has yet to eliminate disputes between lenders and borrowers, even as more firms move into the regulated market.”

For our purposes this sharpens rather than reverses the thesis. It does not say “regulation doesn't work” — the complaint channel that produced this data is itself a product of the reforms, and a borrower whose $1,375 loan ballooned to $3,869 got it waived because a regulator now exists to complain to. It says the value of a licensing regime shows up in two stages, and markets that have completed stage one (vetted entry) can still be years from stage two (enforced conduct). The read for any African financial-services case: a licence is a necessary condition and a marketing signal, not evidence of a clean book or a durable franchise — and the interesting, investable gap may be precisely in the conduct-and-compliance layer (complaints handling, transparent pricing, collections that don't generate 355 complaints) that the licence exposes but doesn't fix. The counter-worry worth holding: rising complaints could equally mean the channel is finally working and surfacing harm that was always there — more visibility, not necessarily more abuse. Either way, it is the honest other half of a story we told at its most flattering yesterday.

Kenya · Banking · Capital Briefing Longitudinal
Absa Group, South Africa's third-largest lender, is running a KES 30.9bn ($240m) tender offer to lift its stake in Absa Bank Kenya from 68.5% to 85% by buying out minority shareholders — and says that once the offer closes it may keep buying on the Nairobi Securities Exchange, pushing ownership higher still. The logic is simple compounding: Absa Bank Kenya's annual profit has risen from KES 7.46bn ($57.2m) in 2019 to KES 22.9bn ($177.1m), with dividends up from KES 6bn to KES 11.1bn over the same period. Owning more of a subsidiary that keeps growing profits and paying rising dividends captures more of that growth without building anything new. There is a clear precedent: Standard Bank ran the same play on Stanbic Holdings, lifting its stake from 60% in 2018 to about 74% by 2022 via a tender offer followed by open-market buying.

Absa watches its Kenyan bank compound — and simply buys more of it

TechCabal Daily · Business Daily · Thu 16 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is capital allocation at its most unglamorous and most sound — a parent looking at a subsidiary that compounds earnings and dividends and concluding the best available investment is more of the thing it already owns and understands. And it threads a pattern the partners have marked before: foreign banking groups turning selective, deploying capital not into new geographies but into buying up the minorities of the African winners already in the portfolio — the same instinct that had Vodacom pay $2.1bn to own M-Pesa outright rather than keep renting it.

TechCabal Daily — “Absa wants a bigger slice of its Kenyan bank” (16 Jul 2026)

Strip away the deal mechanics and this is a clean lesson in where a disciplined allocator puts incremental capital. Absa already controls the Kenyan bank; it is not buying control, it is buying a larger claim on a cash-flow stream it has watched triple in six years. Against a page full of companies reinventing themselves to reach a better layer — Amazon renting a licence, ntel becoming a landlord, Paystack owning the rail — Absa's move is the quiet opposite and just as instructive: sometimes the highest-return use of capital is not a new layer at all, but more of an existing compounder you already know better than any outside buyer ever could.

“In markets where subsidiaries are consistently growing profits and paying healthy dividends, increasing ownership lets parent companies capture more of that growth without building a new business from scratch.”

The precedent makes it a pattern rather than a one-off: Standard Bank did exactly this with Stanbic, tendering and then buying on-market from 60% toward 74%, and the same week's news has a former Safaricom executive taking the Stanbic Bank Kenya CEO seat — the East African banking winners are being consolidated and re-staffed by their regional parents, not sold off. For a firm that holds African financial-services assets, the read cuts close to home. It is a reminder that minority stakes in genuinely compounding African subsidiaries are exactly what the disciplined majority owners want to mop up — which is bullish for holders of those minorities (there is a motivated buyer) and a prompt on our own book (are we the ones who should be buying more of what already works, rather than chasing the new thing?). The forward question we'd carry into any portfolio review: which of our positions is a compounder we understand better than the market, where the best marginal rand is simply more of the same — and which are we holding out of habit rather than conviction? The caution: buying up minorities only compounds if the subsidiary keeps compounding, and paying up through a tender near a profit peak is how “more of a good thing” quietly becomes overpaying for it.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Stop reading documents one at a time — hand Claude the whole stack

Yesterday's tip was to save the prompts that worked. Today's is about the input, not the prompt: the single most underused move is feeding the model many documents at once and asking it to reason across them, not summarise each. Most people paste one PDF, get a summary, and move on — which is the least valuable thing the model does. The value is in synthesis: drop the board pack, the last three months of management accounts, the shareholder agreement and the competitor's results into one conversation and ask what they say together. Where do they contradict each other? What does the cash-flow statement admit that the CEO's letter doesn't? Which number moved that nobody flagged? A human reads a data room sequentially and forgets page 4 by page 40; the model holds all of it at once and will tell you where the seams are — if you ask it to compare rather than to recap.

A worked example

Next time you open a deal folder, don't read it file by file. Load the lot and ask: “Here are the IM, the audited accounts, the management pack and the two most recent competitor results. Read all of them together. Give me: (1) the three places the documents contradict each other, (2) anything the numbers imply that the narrative avoids, and (3) the five questions I should ask management that these documents don't answer.” You'll get a sharper first pass than an hour of solo reading — and a ready-made agenda for the first call.
