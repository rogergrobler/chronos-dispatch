Pan-Africa · Payments · Settlement Analysis Structural design
When a South African business pays a supplier in Malawi, the money rarely moves across the continent directly. It detours through correspondent banks and the US dollar before arriving — a legacy of an era when African economies traded with Europe, Asia and America rather than with one another. Only 15–18% of South Africa's trade is with African countries, and fragmented, dollar-intermediated payment rails are one reason the AfCFTA free-trade area keeps underdelivering. That disconnect is driving a serious, non-speculative use of stablecoins: Onafriq, Yellow Card and Flutterwave are already settling cross-border transactions on dollar-backed stablecoin rails behind the scenes, converting into local currency at the destination in seconds, at a fraction of correspondent-banking cost — with the customer often unaware blockchain is involved. Real-world stablecoin payment volumes hit $7.4tn over the past year, and analysts expect stablecoins to grow from ~3% of the cross-border payments market to as much as 20% within a decade. The binding constraint, the experts stress, is not the technology but exchange-control law and interoperability between fragmented systems.

South Africa wants more African trade — stablecoins are quietly becoming the rail that could carry it

TechCabal · Phathisani Moyo · Tue 21 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the settlement layer being rebuilt one level beneath everything the page discussed yesterday. If the contest has moved to owning the rail, this is a look at what the newest rail is actually made of — a stablecoin settlement leg that erases the dollar detour and collapses days into seconds, without the end customer ever touching crypto. And it reframes the AfCFTA thesis: the barrier to intra-African trade has never mainly been tariffs or goods logistics; it is that moving value between African currencies is slow, expensive and dollar-dependent, so the businesses that fix settlement capture friction that sits on every cross-border transaction.

A continent that trades with the world more than itself — and the invisible rail being built to change the settlement maths
TechCabal — “South Africa wants more African trade. Stablecoins could help make it happen.”

Hold this against the arc the page has been building. For a fortnight the durable position looked like owning the rail — Paystack on collections, Payaza buying licences abroad, ntel turning into a tower-and-fibre landlord. This item asks what the rail is physically made of at the settlement layer, and the answer is increasingly a stablecoin leg that most participants never see. The mechanic is unglamorous and important: instead of routing a South-Africa-to-Malawi payment through correspondent banks and a US-dollar intermediary over several days, the value is converted into a dollar-backed stablecoin, moved in seconds, and paid out in local currency at the far end. The customer stays in their own currency the whole time; the blockchain does its work invisibly. That is the difference between a speculative crypto story, which the page would drop, and an infrastructure story, which is exactly the plumbing our portfolio businesses run on.

“The challenge isn't moving information quickly. It's moving value efficiently across fragmented markets.”

For a firm underwriting African financial-services operators, the read is a genuine reframing of the AfCFTA thesis rather than a trade. The bull case is that settlement is the highest-friction, lowest-visibility layer of intra-African trade — the point every business feels daily — so whoever makes it cheap and instant sits on a toll booth over the continent's commercial future, and the interoperability prize (connecting fragmented rails, cutting reliance on the dollar, freeing working capital) is larger and stickier than the speed headline suggests. The scepticism is precise and worth carrying: both experts are explicit that the binding constraint is not technology but exchange-control legislation, which is slow, national and political — and that stablecoins are a settlement layer, not a new financial system, so they ride on top of banks rather than replacing them. Set beside last week's South Sudan item, the tension sharpens into a single question for any cross-border case: value is learning to move frictionlessly while states remain free to re-erect friction on people and capital at will. The forward question we carry: when we assess an African payments or trade-finance business, is its edge the settlement rail itself — the thing being commoditised toward zero cost — or the licences, corridors and exchange-control navigation that let it use that rail where others legally cannot? The honest caveat: adoption is real but early, the $7.4tn volume figure is global not African, and the 3%-to-20% projection is an analyst's arc, not a fact.

Pan-Africa · Capital · Ecosystem Analysis Longitudinal
TechCabal Insights launched its State of Tech in Africa H1 2026 report in Lagos on 17 July, and the numbers reframe yesterday's Launch Africa anecdote as a system-wide shift. Since 2019, African tech has raised $21bn. H1 2026 funding grew a token 1.4% year-on-year to $1.44bn — but deal count fell hard, 252 to 174. Debt captured 41% of all funding; the earliest-stage startups received just $9m, down from $25m a year earlier. M&A rose 91%, and layoffs climbed 236%, with restructuring and AI adoption the leading causes. Only 7% of the capital came from African investors versus 60% international. The read the analysts draw: funding is no longer growing the industry by creating new companies — it is concentrating into fewer, larger, more mature ones. On the panel, Norebase's Adetola Onayemi called the slowdown "a function of cowardice" rather than caution, urged the ecosystem to stop demanding US-scale unicorns while telling founders to shrink their ambitions, and called for more $100m–$500m companies "small enough to be acquired by a bank and large enough to matter."

Is consolidation the new growth story? Africa's H1 numbers say the money is concentrating, not creating

TechCabal Insights · Stephen Agwaibor · Tue 21 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the aggregate behind yesterday's Launch Africa item — the hard evidence that the earliest cheques have all but vanished ($25m to $9m in a year) and that capital is flowing to consolidation and debt, not company formation. And it is a candid, quotable diagnosis of the ecosystem's psychology from the people inside it: the argument that the constraint is nerve rather than macro, that the sector should target a dense middle of $100–500m businesses acquirable by banks rather than a handful of would-be unicorns, and that only 7% of the capital being African is the structural weakness under all of it.

Total dollars barely moved — but a third fewer deals, two-thirds less at the earliest stage, and debt taking 41 pence in the pound
TechCabal — “State of Tech in Africa H1 2026 recap: Is consolidation the new growth story?”

Yesterday we ran Launch Africa as a single manager's contrarian decision to keep writing early cheques while the market fled. Today's report is the market it was fleeing, quantified — and the aggregate is starker than the anecdote implied. Funding is flat only in the headline; underneath, deal count is down a third, the earliest-stage pool has collapsed from $25m to $9m, debt has taken 41% of the total, and M&A is up 91% while layoffs are up 236%. This is not a downturn that will simply reverse; it is a change in what the capital is for. It has stopped funding the creation of new companies and started funding the concentration of existing ones into fewer, larger, more mature businesses. For a firm that buys and builds positions rather than sprays seed cheques, that is not obviously bad news — a consolidating market is a market that produces acquirable assets — but it does change where value is found and how it is realised.

“What we have now is a crash of companies all trying to be unicorns, a few companies in between, and a lot of debt.”

The panel is worth the partners' time for its candour, and for one structural fact that should anchor any African allocation view: only 7% of the capital deployed into African tech in H1 was African. Sixty per cent was international, which means the ecosystem's funding cycle is set in Washington and London — the Fed's rate path, the retreat of development finance toward defence spending, LP reluctance to back first-time managers — far more than in Lagos or Nairobi. Onayemi's "cowardice, not caution" framing is deliberately provocative, and RegalStone's counter that founders must now "already oversell" cross-market readiness before raising is the more operationally useful truth: capital discipline has raised the bar on proof, not just on nerve. But his call for a dense middle of $100–500m companies — large enough to matter, small enough for a bank or strategic to buy — is precisely the band a PE firm underwrites, and it dovetails with yesterday's Launch Africa "Exit Realism" point that African exits cluster in the $50–150m trade-sale range. The read for us is directional rather than actionable: the African venture funnel is narrowing to a consolidation-and-trade-sale market, which favours disciplined buyers of proven operators over backers of unproven ones — and makes the thinness of domestic capital both the central risk and, for anyone who can supply patient local money, the central opportunity. The caveat: a report launched by a consultancy with a sponsor has an interest in a compelling narrative, and "consolidation as growth" can as easily describe a starved market as a maturing one.

Nigeria · Regulation · Governance Briefing Longitudinal
A Federal High Court in Lagos has restored Nigeria's digital-lending rules while ruling that the consumer-protection regulator, the FCCPC, cannot licence telecoms companies or oversee airtime lending — those powers stay with the telecom regulator, the NCC. The judgment (Suit FHC/L/CS/760/2026) dismissed a challenge by the wireless service providers' association WASPAN to the FCCPC's DEON Regulations 2025, but agreed with WASPAN that the FCCPC's consumer-protection mandate does not stretch to telecoms licensing. The stakes are concrete: airtime and data credit is used by an estimated 40 million Nigerians in a ~₦300–400bn ($217–290m) annual market, and services from MTN, Airtel and Glo were suspended for months in 2026 after the FCCPC classified airtime advances as digital loans. The line the court drew — "concurrency means coexistence, not displacement" — affirms overlapping regulatory authority while preserving each regulator's exclusive turf, and now pressures the FCCPC and NCC to build a joint oversight framework before the next disruption.

Nigeria's airtime-lending fight ends in a split decision — "concurrency means coexistence"

TechCabal · Frank Eleanya · Tue 21 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the rulebook theme from yesterday's CBN item, refined into a question of jurisdiction rather than scope — not what a firm may own, but which regulator gets to say so when a product blurs the line between finance and telecoms. And it is a live lesson in Nigerian regulatory risk for any fintech-adjacent case: a service used by 40 million people was switched off for months by a turf war between two agencies, and the resolution is less a settlement than an instruction to the regulators to learn to share — which means the coordination risk is now the thing to underwrite.

TechCabal — “Nigerian court upholds digital lending rules but rejects parallel telecom licencing”

Read this next to yesterday's CBN item and the pair maps the two axes of regulatory risk in Nigerian financial services. The CBN circulars were about the scope of what a licensed firm may own — capping both sides of payments, ring-fencing capital. This judgment is about the locus of authority — which regulator's writ runs when a product sits on the seam between two of them. Airtime lending is exactly such a product: a telco extends emergency credit that looks, to the consumer regulator, like a digital loan, and to the telecoms regulator, like a network service. The court's answer — "concurrency means coexistence, not displacement" — is elegant and unhelpfully open: both regulators have real authority, neither displaces the other, and they must now coordinate. For the operators, that removed one injunction and replaced it with a standing coordination risk.

“Concurrency means coexistence. The industry now expects both regulators to establish the coordination framework that the court's reasoning requires.”

Why this earns a Chronos slot beyond the Nigerian detail: it is a clean case study in the operational cost of overlapping jurisdiction, which is a live variable in any business whose product straddles regulated categories — embedded finance, telco-fintech, insurtech, BNPL. The bull reading is that the ruling ends months of uncertainty and validates the digital-lending framework, which is good for the licensed lenders and for consumer protection in a market where 40 million people lean on airtime credit. The sceptical reading is the one to carry into diligence: WASPAN itself says "the last may not have been heard," the five airtime-lending approvals the FCCPC already granted now sit on contested legal ground, and a resolution that depends on two agencies choosing to build a joint framework is a resolution that can fail in implementation. The forward question for any Nigerian fintech case: how many regulators can plausibly claim jurisdiction over this product, and is the business's model robust to a turf war between them — because, as MTN, Airtel and Glo just demonstrated, the answer can be a multi-month suspension of a service millions depend on. The measured take: a genuine clarification of principle, but the practical coordination it demands is unbuilt, so the regulatory risk is reduced, not removed.

Nigeria · Fintech · Structural design Analysis Hyper-local
Rank — the Nigerian fintech formerly called Moni — has launched three community-powered products, betting that digitising Africa's traditional savings groups can create a new route to zero-interest capital. Money Circles digitises rotating savings schemes (the ajo and esusu, or ROSCAs, that predate every fintech on the continent): members contribute monthly and receive a lump-sum payout on their turn, with Rank charging a fixed fee based on payout timing and guaranteeing payouts even if a member defaults. Tribe lets existing communities — families, cooperatives, colleagues — manage shared finances with real-time tracking. Rank Perks extends this into payroll, offering salary-backed credit and workplace savings circles with automatic deductions. The launch targets a hard gap: only 4% of Nigerian MSMEs have access to formal bank loans. It comes eight months after Rank acquired the AjoMoney savings platform and Zazzau Microfinance Bank (now Rank MfB), giving it a banking licence, and the company says it has paid out over $100m to community users in the past year.

Rank digitises the ajo and esusu — turning the oldest African savings habit into an institutional engine

TechCabal · Opeyemi Kareem · Tue 21 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is a structural-design bet that the most defensible move in African consumer finance is not to replace informal collective savings but to formalise it — wrapping the centuries-old rotating-savings circle in a banking licence, a default guarantee and real-time tracking, and monetising the timing rather than charging interest. And it threads yesterday's NGX retail story down to its foundation: where young Nigerians were buying shares through apps, this reaches the far larger base that saves collectively rather than invests individually, building a domestic capital pool out of trust structures that already exist in every compound and workplace.

TechCabal — “Rank launches community finance products to expand access to capital”

The instinct worth studying here is the one the page has praised before in other guises: build on the behaviour that already exists rather than the behaviour you wish existed. The ajo, esusu and other ROSCAs are the oldest financial technology on the continent — a group pools a fixed monthly contribution and each member takes the whole pot in turn — and they work precisely because they run on social trust and peer enforcement rather than collateral or credit scores. Rank's bet is that the two things this informal system lacks are exactly what a fintech can add: protection against a member defaulting (Rank guarantees the payout), and the regulatory standing to hold and move the money at scale (the microfinance-bank licence from the Zazzau acquisition). It monetises timing, not interest — a fixed fee scaled to when you take your payout — which sidesteps both the usury optics and the credit-risk exposure that sink so many lending models. That is a structurally cleaner unit economic than the emergency-lending business Moni started as.

“We are bringing these trusted traditions into the modern age … turning collective financial habits into a modern, institutional-grade engine for generational wealth.”

Why it matters to us: it is a live example of the formalisation thesis that recurs across African financial services — taking an informal, trust-based, cash economy and giving it rails, records and a regulated wrapper — and it points at a genuinely large and underserved base (only 4% of Nigerian MSMEs can get a formal bank loan). The bull case is that a licensed, guaranteed, digitised ROSCA is both defensible and sticky: the network effect is the group itself, switching costs are social, and the data exhaust from millions of contribution-and-payout cycles is a proprietary underwriting asset that competitors like PiggyVest and Cowrywise — built around individual saving — do not have. The scepticism is that guaranteeing payouts against member default is a real balance-sheet liability dressed as a feature, that "community finance" at scale imports the fraud and collusion risks that small trusted circles avoid precisely by staying small, and that a company still fresh from rebranding and acquiring a bank is making an ambitious leap from lender to savings-and-wealth platform. The read for a financial-services investor: this is the kind of culturally-native structural design that is hard for an outsider to replicate and worth tracking as a model, with the underwriting question being whether the default guarantee is priced honestly and whether trust survives the transition from a twelve-person circle to a twelve-million-person app. The caveat: this is a launch with a founder's framing and no independent performance data yet — the $100m paid out is a cumulative marketing figure, not audited loss experience.

Francophone W. Africa · Commerce · Retail-tech Analysis Wouldn't-have-seen-otherwise
Africa's informal retail — the corner shop, the kiosk, the market stall — carries up to 90% of consumer-goods transactions on the continent, and in Francophone West Africa the figure is 80–95%. TechCabal's Francophone Weekly argues this overlooked channel is one of Africa's most durable commerce opportunities, and that a generation of B2B distribution platforms is racing to digitise it. The macro is unusually favourable: the UEMOA bloc grew 6.3% in 2024 and stays above 6%, well over the ~3.8% continental average; Côte d'Ivoire alone is ~40% of UEMOA GDP; and the CFA franc's euro peg gives currency stability that naira- or cedi-exposed investors cannot assume. Yet the region has captured only a sliver of the $1.07bn African retail-tech raised 2019–mid-2025 (75%+ went to Egypt, Kenya and Nigeria). The moat is concrete: French-language interfaces and local languages (Bambara, Dioula, Fula) shut out East and Southern African copycats, and OHADA harmonised business law plus BCEAO–CEMAC payment interoperability make one product scalable across eight countries. The lesson the survivors learned post-2022: profit comes from embedded credit and software, not delivery margins.

Francophone Africa's smallest shops — 80–95% of all trade — are the commerce opportunity nobody funded

TechCabal Francophone Weekly · Lina Kacyem · Tue 21 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the counter-image to yesterday's South Sudan toll — a bloc, UEMOA, where integration is the whole investable thesis (one euro-pegged currency, one harmonised commercial-law code, interoperable payments across eight countries), and where language is a genuine moat that keeps East and Southern African incumbents out. And it is a masterclass in the retail-tech lesson the whole sector paid for after 2022: the money is not in owning the delivery van but in the embedded credit and software layer — transactional-data underwriting that turns an invisible informal shopkeeper into a registered SME with a credit history, the point where the impact story and the return story finally converge.

The channel that carries almost all the trade — and almost none of the funding: a first-mover window still open
TechCabal — “Francophone Africa's smallest shops could become its biggest business opportunity”

This is the kind of item the page exists to surface — a structural, contrarian read on a market most of our peers cannot even see, partly because it is written in French. Three things make it worth the partners' time. First, the moat is real and unusually durable: enterprise software, ERPs and logistics platforms were built in English, so serving Ivorian, Senegalese and Malian retailers in French — and in Bambara, Dioula and Fula — is a barrier that keeps the East and Southern African incumbents (the merged Wasoko–MaxAB, Chari) at arm's length. Language is doing the defensive work that capital usually has to. Second, the integration story is the exact inverse of last week's South Sudan toll: UEMOA is one bloc, one euro-pegged currency, one harmonised commercial-law regime (OHADA across 17 states), and a live BCEAO–CEMAC payments-interoperability initiative — so a single product can scale across eight countries, and the currency peg removes the FX risk that naira- and cedi-exposed platforms carry. Where South Sudan showed integration is reversible, UEMOA shows what a functioning integration premium looks like when you can actually underwrite it.

“The path to profitability runs through financial services — not logistics margins. Technology amplifies and formalises the existing distribution rather than displacing it.”

Third, and most useful, is the hard-won unit-economics lesson, because it is the same one the whole retail-tech category learned expensively and the same one that runs through today's Rank item. The first wave assumed technology would replace the traditional distributor and that heavy owned logistics, funded by perpetual venture capital, would win. The 2022 funding winter killed the asset-heavy players and taught the survivors that the model is amplify-and-formalise, not replace: own logistics selectively, partner where you can, and make the money on software and embedded credit rather than delivery margins. OmniRetail — profitable, now expanding from Nigeria into Côte d'Ivoire — is the reference case, and its near-perfect loan-repayment rate is the tell: transactional-history underwriting beats classical credit scoring for retailers the formal system cannot see. For a firm like ours the read is a framework and a watchlist, not a deal: the defensible value in African B2B commerce is the credit-and-data layer that turns an informal shopkeeper into a registered SME with a repayment record — the "formalisation loop" where the social-impact narrative and the commercial return are the same thing. The caveats are candid in the piece itself: there is no clean sub-regional data for Francophone West Africa (the author uses continental proxies), consumer-credit regulation across UEMOA is not yet as accommodating as Kenya's, and better-capitalised regional entrants like OmniRetail are already moving in — so the first-mover window for native Francophone players is real but closing.

Kenya · Banking · Governance Briefing Longitudinal
Safaricom's chief financial services officer, Esther Waititu, will leave at the end of July — the third senior executive to exit Kenya's largest telco in five months, all from roles central to its push beyond telecoms into digital financial services. In March, Sitoyo Lopokoiyit stepped down as MD of M-PESA Africa; chief business development and strategy officer Michael Mutiga is leaving to become CEO of Stanbic Bank Kenya and South Sudan from 1 August. Waititu, who joined in 2023 from banking, oversaw the "Fintech 2.0" re-platforming of M-PESA onto cloud-native architecture (6,000 transactions/second) and the launch of Ziidi Trader, which gave M-PESA's 37m+ users a direct route into the Nairobi bourse. The departures land precisely as Safaricom expands M-PESA from payments into savings, credit and investments — a leadership vacuum at the top of the very unit driving the strategy.

Safaricom loses its third financial-services chief in five months — the people-as-control thread runs on

TechCabal · Kenn Abuya · Mon 20 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is yesterday's GTBank item — control exercised through people — seen from the other side of the same East African labour market. Where GTCO recalled an MD to Lagos, Safaricom is losing its financial-services leadership to rivals and exits, and one of the departures is walking straight into a Stanbic CEO seat. And it is a governance flag on a specific asset: three exits in five months from the exact unit driving M-PESA's expansion into savings, credit and investments is execution risk landing at the worst possible moment in the strategy.

TechCabal — “Safaricom's financial services chief Esther Waititu to leave company”

Yesterday's GTBank story was control through people at its cheapest: a parent recalling one executive across a border, no transaction required. Today's Safaricom story is the same variable read from the other direction — not a parent deploying talent, but an operator losing it. Three senior departures in five months, all from the leadership of the financial-services unit, and all landing as M-PESA pushes from payments into savings, credit and investing. One of the three, Michael Mutiga, is not retiring but crossing the street to run Stanbic Bank Kenya, which makes the pattern legible: the people who can run East African financial services at scale are a small, sought-after cadre, and they move between telco, bank and fintech seats in a way that makes the identity of a unit's leadership a more informative signal than most line items in its accounts.

“The three executives held roles central to Safaricom's strategy of expanding beyond telecommunications into digital financial services.”

Why it matters to us as more than a personnel note: management churn concentrated in one unit, at the moment that unit is executing the company's most important strategic pivot, is a specific and often underpriced risk. M-PESA is not a side project; it is the crown jewel, freshly re-platformed onto cloud-native rails and newly extended into securities trading through Ziidi Trader. Losing the CFSO, the M-PESA Africa MD and the strategy chief inside five months, however individually explicable, removes institutional memory and continuity from exactly the team that has to land savings, credit and investment products against banks that are themselves hiring away its people. The forward question for any position exposed to a group-and-subsidiary or platform-pivot story: how much of the thesis depends on a specific leadership team, how deep is the bench beneath them, and is a wave of exits a coincidence, a symptom of a strategy under strain, or simply the market repricing scarce operators? The measured read: Safaricom is deep enough to absorb this and has named an interim, so this is a yellow flag rather than a red one — but a yellow flag on the highest-value part of the business, and worth watching to see whether the next M-PESA product cycle slips. The caveat: departures were disclosed internally, the company has not given reasons, and executive movement in a hot market is as easily a sign of talent value as of trouble — the tell will be in execution over the next two quarters, not in the exits themselves.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Feed it the whole stack at once — work across several documents in one prompt

Yesterday's tip was to set your standing preferences once. Today's is about the single biggest unlock for deal and diligence work: stop pasting one document at a time. Modern models will hold a dozen files in a single conversation — a board pack, a data room's worth of PDFs, three brokers' notes, a competitor's annual report — and reason across them, not just within each. The value is in the cross-reference: "here are five documents; where does management's guidance in the board pack contradict what the CFO told the analysts?" or "compare the margin bridge in these three notes and tell me where they disagree and why." That is the work a junior spends a day on, done in a minute — and, crucially, the model will flag the contradiction you would not have gone looking for. The discipline that makes it reliable: ask it to cite which document each claim came from, so you can check it rather than trust it.

A worked example

Drop in the target's last three board packs and its two most recent lender covenants and ask: "Read all five. Build me a table of every KPI that appears in more than one document, show how it moved, and flag anywhere the same metric is defined or counted differently between documents — cite the source file for each row." You get a reconciled view no single document gives you, and the definitional inconsistencies — the exact places where a number is being flattered — surface on their own. Then pressure-test it: "Now argue the bear case using only the numbers in these files."
