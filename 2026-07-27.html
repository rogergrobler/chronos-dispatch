South Africa · Institutions · Asset management Analysis Live today
Africa's largest asset manager has lost its CEO, its chair and effectively its entire board in under a fortnight — and the reckoning meeting is today. The sequence: the PIC board suspended CEO Patrick Dlamini; non-executive directors resigned in waves (six inside a week); deputy finance minister David Masondo yielded to pressure from Enoch Godongwana and resigned as chair; and the minister called a Monday 27 July shareholder meeting to deal with what remains, with an interim structure to follow. Underneath sit two live investigations. The FSCA and SIU are probing the R430m the PIC paid Acapulco after arbitration over a 2013 Lanseria Airport loan — a payment made while Acapulco was roughly R600m in arrears, after the PIC agreed to drop the valuer (BDO, R330m) in favour of one introduced by its counterparty (Crowe, R1bn). PwC, brought in by the suspended CEO, found the PIC "essentially gave Acapulco a blank cheque". A whistleblower report seen by Business Times goes further: the investment committee allocating public billions "lacks fit-for-purpose capability to exercise informed investment judgment" — "a fundamental design flaw". Godongwana's stated preference is to let the FSCA and SIU run rather than convene another Mpati-style commission. The PIC manages about R3.7 trillion, overwhelmingly on behalf of the Government Employees Pension Fund; CEO, COO, chief investment officer and chief risk officer are all currently acting appointments.

The PIC loses its head, then its board — and this morning the minister decides what is left

Sunday Times Business Times · Kabelo Khumalo · Sat 25 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the JPMorgan succession essay from Friday's page run in reverse — instead of asking who can hold the integration map of a vast balance sheet, the PIC's whistleblower alleges nobody on the investment committee ever held it. For anyone allocating capital in South Africa, the entity that anchors the JSE's shareholder registers and prices half the country's private-market deals is, as of this morning, an institution with no board and four acting executives.

Sunday Times — “Enoch Godongwana determined to fix PIC governance”

The whistleblower's language deserves close reading, because it is an institutional-design argument, not a corruption allegation. The claim is that private-markets judgement is a craft skill — "developed through direct participation in transactions" — that cannot be substituted by general financial or risk experience, and that a committee without it is "structurally deficient" regardless of the integrity of its members. That is precisely the Rubinstein point from Friday's page: complexity has to be held by someone who has walked the balance sheet line by line. The Acapulco detail is what the design flaw produces in practice — a counterparty in R600m of arrears choosing the valuer, a R670m valuation gap between the two accounting firms, and an arbitration the PIC entered with what PwC called a weak case. Cosatu's diagnosis cuts deepest: board seats filled by deployment negotiation between Treasury, the GEPF and the unions, meaning the PIC "was denied the opportunity to scan the market for the skills required". The institution's governance was designed as a political settlement, and political settlements do not produce transaction craft.

An investment committee allocating public billions "without fit-for-purpose capability" — the integration map, held by nobody.

What happens next matters well beyond Pretoria. The PIC is the anchor allocator of the entire South African capital market — the largest single shareholder on the JSE and the LP every African private-markets GP eventually calls. A paralysed PIC slows private-market deal flow, secondaries and every capital raise that assumed its participation; a reformed one, with a committee built on the whistleblower's fit-for-purpose standard, would change the quality bar for the whole ecosystem. The interim signals to watch: whether the acting-executive layer is stabilised with market hires or more deployment; whether Seiso Mohai (the reported dark horse) or a technocrat gets the chair; and whether the FSCA report survives contact with politics — Masondo's parting request that the Acapulco saga "not be swept under the carpet" reads as a man positioning for the inquiry he suspects will be tempted to do exactly that. The thread runs back through this page's month: GTBank recalling its Kenyan MD, Safaricom shedding fintech chiefs, Nedbank's coming NCBA appointments — control is exercised through people, and the PIC has just discovered what happens when the people layer fails at the top of the pyramid.

South Africa · Trade · US tariffs Briefing Hyper-local
At 06:01 on Friday 24 July, the new US tariff regime took effect against South Africa — one of 60 economies hit under Section 301 of the Trade Act after a USTR investigation found they failed to prohibit imports made with forced labour. The legal architecture is new: the previous IEEPA tariffs were ruled unconstitutional by the US Supreme Court in February, and the March investigation rebuilt the wall on different statutory ground. Three tiers emerged — a 10% club of 17 partial-compliers (the UK, India, Mexico, Canada among them), capped rates for reciprocal-agreement partners (EU, Japan, Korea), and a flat 12.5% for everyone else. South Africa, number 48 of 60, sits in the last group. But the annexure is where the story lives: subheading 9903.05.86 exempts oranges, fruit juices and nuts entirely — the core of SA's $504m of 2025 agricultural exports to the US — and 9903.05.90 covers aluminium, steel, copper, passenger vehicles and bakkies. Agbiz chief economist Wandile Sihlobo, who lobbied Washington and "didn't find a fertile ear", still calls 12.5% a substantial improvement on the 30% Liberation Day rate of 2025, which crushed Q4 agri exports 39% year on year — and notes competitors Australia, Peru and Chile landed at the same level.

Washington's 12.5% lands on South Africa — with an orange-shaped hole in the middle of it

Daily Maverick · Lindsey Schutters · Fri 24 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the headline number is worse than the substance — the goods South Africa actually ships to America (citrus, juice, nuts, vehicles, metals) are largely carved out, making this closer to a 12.5% tariff on the trade that doesn't happen than the one that does. The more durable damage is architectural: tariffs rebuilt on Section 301 forced-labour findings are harder to negotiate away than emergency powers, because the off-ramp now requires proving a negative about supply chains.

The 30% regime cut Q4 agri exports by two-fifths — the 12.5% sequel exempts the crops that matter most
Daily Maverick — “There's a big orange asterisk on Trump's latest 12.5% tariff”

Read the exemption logic and a pattern emerges: the carve-outs track US economic self-interest, item by item. Goods exempted are those the US "cannot grow or produce in sufficient quantities or reasonable prices" — which is why Southern Hemisphere citrus, counter-seasonal to Florida and California, walks free while the abstract tariff rate makes headlines. That makes this regime more stable than its predecessor in one sense (the exemptions are load-bearing for US consumers, so they are unlikely to vanish) and less negotiable in another: South Africa's 12.5% is not a bargaining posture awaiting a deal, it is the residual after American self-interest has already been served. Sihlobo's competitive framing is the operator's correct lens — what matters to a Citrusdal packhouse is not the absolute rate but the spread against Chile and Peru, and that spread is now zero.

Closer to a 12.5% tariff on the trade that doesn't happen than on the trade that does.

The threads run in three directions. To Friday's SARB hold: the tariff adds a trade channel to the rand's weakness at exactly the moment the MPC declined to defend the currency with rates — September's meeting now prices oil, a softer rand and a tariff wall together. To the AfCFTA-stablecoin thread (Issues 048–051): every increment of US protection raises the relative return on intra-African trade infrastructure, which is precisely the corridor logic the settlement-rail items have been tracking — trade diversion needs rails, and the rails are being built in this page's other stories. And to the 2025 precedent: exporters beat the last wall by front-running it in a 90-day pause; there is no pause this time, so the Q3 2026 numbers will show the regime's true bite uncushioned. The caveat cuts both ways: "possibly cars" is doing quiet work in the exemption list — the vehicle carve-out reads clear in the annexure but has not been tested at a port of entry, and SA's automotive exports are an order of magnitude larger than its citrus.

Global · Fintech · AI economics Analysis World's-best-thinkers
Simon Taylor's Sunday letter leads with a deal that would have sounded absurd in May: Stripe in talks to buy OpenRouter — the marketplace-API that sits in front of hundreds of AI models and routes each request to the cheapest one that can do the job — at roughly $10bn, an ~8x markup on the $1.3bn valuation OpenRouter raised at two months ago. Taylor's frame: Stripe already collects AI companies' revenue (OpenAI and Anthropic run on Stripe), bought Metronome (~$1bn) to meter the usage they sell, and OpenRouter would give it the other side — the tokens they buy, usually their single largest input cost. "Money in, money out": both sides of every AI company's P&L, visible in real time, with a take-rate on each. The same letter carries the week's second landmark: Revolut confirming a $115bn secondary valuation — more than Barclays or Deutsche Bank — on $6bn of revenue and $2.3bn of 2025 pre-tax profit, roughly 50x, with Nvidia's venture arm on the cap table and a US charter application the swing variable. Plus Augustus, a $1bn-valued clearing-bank startup with conditional OCC approval, aiming squarely at the correspondent-banking franchise that earns Citi $21bn a year — built for exactly the fintechs in Lagos and Manila that today's African items describe.

Taylor: Stripe bids $10bn to own both sides of the AI P&L — and a travel card becomes worth more than Barclays

Fintech Brainfood · Simon Taylor · Sun 26 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the OpenRouter logic is this page's thesis in miniature — when a new commodity (tokens) becomes the economy's largest new input cost, the durable business is not producing it but routing it, and Stripe is bidding for the routing layer with the target's revenue visible in its own payments data. Revolut at $115bn is the second data point: the market now prices a licensed deposit-taking bank at a software multiple because it behaves like a technology company that happens to hold money.

Fintech Brainfood — “Stripe's $10bn OpenRouter Bid | Revolut Hits $115bn”

The structural insight worth extracting: Stripe has run the same play three times — spot the next category of internet commerce, buy its infrastructure early (Bridge for stablecoins, Privy for wallets, Metronome for AI billing, now potentially OpenRouter for AI routing) — and the play works because take-rates on flows compound with the category. Taylor's sharpest line is that routing is the control point of AI spend: it decides which model gets the token, at what price, from which provider. That is the tollbooth position this page has repeatedly flagged in African payments (PAPSS, PI-SPI below, the Access Point Provider licences in Nigeria's e-invoicing) transposed to the AI economy — whoever stands where the flow must pass earns basis points on everything. The corroborating detail is CFO behaviour: companies burning a year's token budget in four months is what makes a routing layer a procurement necessity rather than a developer convenience, and Ramp attacking the same problem from the spend-management side confirms the category.

Tokens are the new commodity; the durable business is not producing them but routing them.

The sceptic's ledger, which Taylor himself keeps open: an 8x markup in two months is classic bubble behaviour unless Stripe's private view of OpenRouter's revenue (which it processes) justifies it — an information asymmetry that cuts both ways, since Stripe is effectively insider-bidding on data no other acquirer has. Revolut at 50x pre-tax is defensible only if the growth rate holds ($9bn revenue by year-end brings it to ~13x); it is a bet that banking licences plus software economics are additive rather than offsetting. For the businesses on these pages the transmission is concrete: every African fintech and AI startup's unit economics now route through infrastructure owned by two or three US companies — payments through Stripe, models through the routers, dollars through the new clearing banks — and Augustus's explicit targeting of African fintechs for direct dollar-account access is the correspondent-banking disruption this page's stablecoin thread (Issues 048–051) has been predicting from the other end. The threads: Ng's integration premium (Issue 050) — Stripe is integrating the AI P&L end to end; the "money for machines" consortium (Issue 050); and Anthropic's $965bn (Issue 12, May) — the capital signature of the sector whose plumbing Stripe is now buying.

Kenya · Regulation · Digital assets Briefing Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Kenya gazetted the Virtual Asset Service Providers Regulations, 2026, on Friday — Legal Notice No. 134, completing the VASP Act President Ruto signed in October 2025 and giving East Africa's second-largest crypto market (about $19bn of inflows in the year to June 2025, per Chainalysis) its full licensing regime. The architecture: the Central Bank of Kenya supervises fiat-conversion services and stablecoin issuers; the Capital Markets Authority takes exchanges, token issuance, ICOs and tokenisation. The scope is the notable part — the rules cover stablecoins, tokenised real-world assets, wallets, advertising and market conduct, and reach foreign providers that "actively target Kenyan customers or derive economic benefit" from the country even with no physical presence. Licensed firms must meet governance and capital requirements, run AML and cybersecurity controls, safeguard customer assets, and keep transaction records seven years. The gazettement follows a four-month consultation in which crypto firms warned the capital requirements "risked pricing smaller operators out" — and lands three months after the CBK started hiring virtual-asset licensing staff.

Kenya finishes its crypto rulebook — and writes the reach clause the rest of the continent will copy

TechCabal · Emmanuel Nwosu · Fri 24 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the extraterritorial clause is the sleeper — any platform serving Kenyan customers from anywhere now needs a Kenyan licence, which converts every cross-border stablecoin and tokenised-asset product this page has tracked (Daya's $1 S&P 500 access included) from a regulatory grey zone into a licensing decision. Kenya has chosen the opposite instinct to Nigeria's freeze-and-observe: build the gate first, and let the CBK-CMA split decide who mans it.

TechCabal — “Kenya gazettes crypto licensing rules for digital asset firms”

The regulatory design choice is the interesting layer. Splitting oversight between the CBK (anything touching fiat and stablecoins) and the CMA (anything resembling securities) imports the traditional banking/markets divide into a sector whose defining products — a stablecoin that pays yield, a tokenised stock in a payment wallet — exist precisely because that divide stopped describing reality. Daya's product from Friday's page would plausibly need both licences in Kenya. The seven-year record-keeping and the reach clause together make the regime a data infrastructure as much as a gate: Kenya will know, transaction by transaction, what its citizens hold on foreign platforms — the same state-legibility instinct as Nigeria's e-invoicing feed (Issue 051), pointed at digital assets. And the timing against the region matters: with $19bn of annual inflows second only to Ethiopia, Kenya is regulating a market that already exists at scale, not summoning one — which is why the consultation fight was about capital floors pricing out local operators rather than about whether the sector should exist at all.

The reach clause converts every cross-border stablecoin product on this page from a grey zone into a licensing decision.

The threads run dense here. Kenya's own sequence: 252 licensed digital lenders in four years (Issue 048), lender-of-last-resort rules written before they were needed (Issue 47's "write the rules before the crisis"), now VASP — a regulator systematically building capacity ahead of demand, the institutional mirror image of today's PIC story. The corridor thread: SARB's sandbox admitted stablecoin settlement pilots (Issue 9), Nigeria's CBN built dollar-tracing pipes (Issue 047), and the AfCFTA-stablecoin argument (Issue 048) always ended at the same question — under whose licence? Kenya just published an answer. The sceptic's notes: gazettement is not capacity, and the CBK is still hiring the people who will process the applications; the capital floors may indeed hand the market to foreign incumbents wearing local licences, which is the standard cost of gate-first regimes; and the extraterritorial reach is only as strong as Kenya's ability to block non-compliant apps — enforcement against offshore platforms has defeated larger regulators. Watch the first licence cohort: who applies, who withdraws from the market, and whether the stablecoin issuers land at the CBK looking more like banks or more like payment schemes.

Nigeria · Fintech · AI in credit Analysis AI deployment case study
TechCabal's Day 1-1000 series profiles Mida, the Lagos startup built by three ex-Renmoney executives (engineering, product, operations) who watched the same failure from three seats: lenders had automated onboarding, credit checks and disbursement — and none of it mattered when borrowers didn't repay. Founded November 2023 on $50,000 of family money, Mida pivoted fast when the market told it what it wanted — "I have so much bad debt, how do I recover this?" — and built the full recovery stack: Mida Omni (digital collections, call-centre ops, portfolio segmentation), MidaX (field agents, digital skip-tracing), Mida Forms (onboarding and credit workflows). Then the AI layer: first enriching borrower data for lending decisions, then an autonomous AI telecollector that calls borrowers and follows up overdue accounts itself. The result reported at day 1,000: collections rising without matching headcount, improving gross margins, and EBITDA positivity in Q1 2026 — on more than ₦100bn ($73m) of managed debt portfolios, with Access Holdings' digital lender among the clients. The context that makes it structural: Nigeria's licensed digital lenders grew from 173 to 461 in 28 months, defaults are rising per the CBN's credit-conditions surveys, and the company is now raising a $1–3m seed to push AI deeper and enter Ghana and Kenya.

The AI does the calling: how three ex-Renmoney operators reached profitability on Nigeria's bad debts

TechCabal · Opeyemi Kareem · Sat 25 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the practical-AI-deployment story this page exists to surface — not a model demo but an autonomous telecollector changing a unit-economics line (collections up, headcount flat, EBITDA positive) in a people-intensive business that software alone couldn't fix. And it is the missing layer under a month of lending stories: 461 Nigerian lenders, 252 Kenyan ones, M-KOPA's ten million borrowers — every credit boom this page has tracked eventually meets the recovery problem Mida is industrialising.

TechCabal — “Day 1-1000 of Mida”

The operating lesson sits in the middle of the story, where most coverage would skip: Mida tried to be a pure software company and the market refused. Lenders didn't want tools, they wanted debts recovered — so Mida became a services business (call centres, field agents) and then used AI to convert the services cost structure back into software economics. That sequencing — services first to own the outcome, AI second to fix the margin — is becoming the recognisable pattern in African AI deployment, and it inverts the Silicon Valley default of shipping tools and hoping users change their workflow. The AI telecollector is the concrete instance: collections calling is repetitive, scripted, high-volume and outcome-measurable — exactly the profile of work Ng's framework (Issue 050) says automates first — and because Mida owns the recovery outcome, the AI's performance shows up directly in its own P&L rather than in a customer's adoption metrics. EBITDA positivity at day 1,000 on ₦100bn of managed portfolios is the proof the model closes.

Services first to own the outcome, AI second to fix the margin — the emerging grammar of African AI deployment.

The structural read: collections is credit infrastructure's dark side, and it compounds with everything else on this page. M-KOPA's two million daily repayments (Issue 051) are what recovery success looks like when it is designed in from the start; Mida is what gets built when it isn't. The 173→461 licensed-lender explosion is the demand curve; the CBN's rising-default surveys are the margin pressure; and Kenya — where digital-lender complaints quintupled even as licensing spread (Issue 048) — is the obvious next market, which is exactly where Mida says it is going. The caveats are real: all figures are company-reported and unaudited; ₦1bn actually recovered against ₦100bn managed is a modest recovery rate whose denominator does the flattering; the AI-telecollector category carries consumer-protection risk that Kenya's complaint statistics foreshadow, and an autonomous system making collection calls at scale is one viral WhatsApp clip away from a regulatory incident. The forward marker: whether the seed round prices Mida as a collections agency with software (services multiple) or credit infrastructure with AI (the multiple the founders are clearly building toward).

West Africa · Payments · Infrastructure Briefing Chart of the day
Presenting its 2025 annual report in Dakar, the BCEAO put numbers on PI-SPI, the interoperable instant-payment platform it launched across all eight WAEMU countries on 30 September 2025. The reach figures are striking: 30 million connected users — nearly 40% of the Union's adult population — across 80 connected institutions (45 at launch, 74 by December). The usage figures are the story: one million transactions totalling 110bn CFA francs ($190m) in ten months. The platform solves a real problem — fragmentation that forced users to withdraw cash to move money between networks, in a region where financial-services access has risen from 14.6% to 76% of adults in two decades — and it is free for individuals, instant, and QR-standardised for merchants. But onboarding has slowed sharply (security vetting per institution, since each connection is an attack surface on a real-time system), and the connection deadlines have already slipped once: banks and e-money issuers now have until 30 September 2026, microfinance institutions until mid-2027. Governor Kassi Brou's own framing concedes the gap: the system delivers its benefits only when all operators are connected.

Thirty million connected, one million transactions — the BCEAO builds the rail before the habit

Ecofin Agency · Fiacre E. Kakpo · Fri 24 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: PI-SPI has acquired reach thirty times faster than usage — 30 million people who could transact against one million transactions that happened — which makes it a live experiment in whether interoperability mandated top-down can manufacture the network effects that M-Pesa and Wave grew bottom-up. The comparison that stings: Nigeria's NIP processes more transactions before breakfast than PI-SPI has settled in ten months, in economies of comparable population.

The green bar is who could pay; the red sliver is who did — interoperability's demand problem, in one picture
Ecofin Agency — “BCEAO's instant payment network reaches 30M connected users”

The chart's gap is the whole strategic question. Supply-side interoperability — the central bank's chosen "foundation of financial inclusion" — has been delivered: the rail exists, it is free, it settles in seconds, and 40% of adults are technically on it. What has not happened is the habit switch, and the reasons are structural rather than technical: the region's dominant money behaviours run inside closed loops (Wave's own network, Orange Money's own network) that already work for their users, and PI-SPI's value proposition — crossing between networks — is precisely the transaction people have spent a decade learning to avoid. The instructive contrasts are Brazil and India: Pix and UPI exploded not because interoperability existed but because killer use-cases (QR merchants, government disbursements, e-commerce checkout) forced daily contact with the rail. PI-SPI has the rail and awaits the reason.

The central bank built the bridge between networks; the networks' users had already learned to live without crossing.

Why it matters beyond WAEMU: this is the francophone leg of the settlement-infrastructure thread this page has run for a month — CEMAC joining PAPSS by end-2026 (Issue 047), Rwanda routing all retail digital payments through one national rail (Issue 048), and the BCEAO itself preparing a PAPSS pilot with 80-plus banks. The pattern across all of them: African central banks are building public rails underneath private closed loops, betting that mandated interoperability plus deadlines eventually beats network gravity. The sceptic's case is the Kenya precedent — regulated interoperability between M-Pesa and Airtel Money existed for years on paper while the closed loop kept winning — and the deadlines' first slippage suggests the institutions feel limited commercial urgency. The signals worth watching: whether the September 2026 bank deadline holds; whether merchant QR volume (the Pix use-case) appears in the next BCEAO report as its own line; and whether Wave — the region's dominant wallet and conspicuously unnamed in the governor's remarks — connects enthusiastically or minimally. Interoperability that excludes the incumbent's customers is a bridge from nowhere to nowhere.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Hire Claude as your sparring partner — rehearse the hard conversation before you have it

The sceptic test (making the model attack a thesis) is now in the toolkit; today's tip is its interactive cousin, and it earns its keep in a week of board meetings and negotiations. Before any high-stakes conversation — a founder pushing back on valuation, a co-investor wavering, a CEO defending a miss — tell Claude: "Play [the counterparty]. Here is what I know about their position, incentives and style. I'll open; respond as they would, and don't make it easy." Then actually run the conversation, turn by turn. Two things happen. First, the model surfaces counter-arguments you hadn't priced — not because it is clairvoyant but because it isn't attached to your position. Second, and more valuable: you hear your own opening argument out loud before it matters, and weak framing collapses in rehearsal instead of in the room. Round it off with one meta-question — "What did I concede too early, and what was my strongest unused card?" — and you walk in having already lost the argument once, cheaply.

A worked example

Before your next valuation discussion: "Play a founder who believes his company is worth 8x revenue because a US comparable trades there. He is smart, a little defensive, and needs the money in eight weeks but won't admit it. I'll open with our 5x view. Push back as he would — three rounds — then step out of character and tell me which of my arguments landed, which he'd exploit, and the one question that would most unsettle his 8x anchor." Ten minutes, and the real meeting starts on your terms.
