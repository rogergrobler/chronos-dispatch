Pan-Africa · Telecoms · Fintech Analysis Chart of the day
Vodacom's trading update for the quarter ended 30 June 2026 reports that its mobile-money platforms — now including Safaricom's M-PESA, after Vodacom lifted its stake to 55% and took control — processed nearly $548bn in the twelve months to 30 June. Financial services now contribute more than 22% of group service revenue, up from 13% before the deal. Egypt's financial-services revenue surged 73% (service revenue +32.8% in local currency); Tanzania launched what Vodacom calls Africa's first M-PESA tap-to-pay for 22m+ wallets. On the strength of the mix shift, CEO Shameel Joosub upgraded medium-term EBITDA and free-cash-flow growth targets from "double-digit" to "early-teens," and raised the Vision 2030 revenue ambition from >R200bn ($12bn) to >R300bn ($18bn). "A defining moment," he called it.

Vodacom now moves nearly half a trillion dollars a year in mobile money — the continent's biggest telco is becoming a fintech

TechCabal · Telecoms · Phathisani Moyo · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a routine telco quarterly with a big number attached. The one that matters is the confession inside the revenue mix — fintech, not voice and data, is now the growth engine, and by taking control of M-PESA Vodacom has effectively reclassified itself as a pan-African financial-services company that happens to own networks. The half-trillion-dollar figure is throughput, not revenue, but the 13%-to-22% jump in where the money actually comes from is the real signal.

The mix is the message — where Vodacom's money comes from shifted almost ten points toward fintech in one deal
TechCabal — “Vodacom processed $548 billion in mobile money as fintech surges”

The structural read is that a business reprices itself the moment its growth stops coming from where its history did. Vodacom built a voice-and-data operator; the Safaricom consolidation has now tipped more than a fifth of group service revenue into financial services, and management has responded by raising its own growth targets — the clearest possible signal that it believes the fintech line, not the connectivity line, sets the ceiling. Controlling M-PESA is the pivot: it is not a payments feature bolted onto a telco, it is Africa's largest mobile-money network becoming the core of the group, with the network as distribution. The tap-to-pay launch in Tanzania and the 73% financial-services jump in Egypt are early evidence the same playbook travels market to market.

The $548bn is throughput, not revenue — but the number that reprices the company is the quieter one: financial services went from 13% to 22% of group revenue in a single deal.

The threads: to Friday's item 4 — this is Luno's "own the rail" move at continental scale, Vodacom keeping the custody, liquidity and compliance of M-PESA while everyone else's customers cross it. To Friday's item 2 — the same climb up the value chain the SA banks are buying their way up, except Vodacom built (and then bought control of) the layer rather than acquiring a startup. To Friday's item 1 — this is the mega-scale end of the concentration story: capital and revenue pooling in the few very large, infrastructure-heavy names. The sceptic's ledger: $548bn is transaction value, not income, and consolidating M-PESA mechanically inflates the financial-services share without any organic acceleration; Egypt's 73% is off a smaller base and flattered by local-currency effects; and controlling Safaricom brings minority-interest and Ethiopia-loss drag the headline mix hides. The forward marker: whether the "early-teens" growth targets survive once the M-PESA consolidation bump laps its first full year — the test of whether this is a genuine fintech re-rating or an accounting reclassification.

Nigeria · Cross-border payments · Fintech M&A Briefing Longitudinal thread
Zedcrest Group — a Nigerian financial-services group spanning investment banking, asset management and fintech — has fully acquired UK-founded cross-border payments startup Leatherback for an undisclosed amount, converting a 2021 $10m pre-seed stake into outright ownership. It follows Zedcrest's 2024 purchase of RMB Nigeria Stockbrokers. Leatherback will run as an independent subsidiary, keeping its brand and management, and has re-pointed its strategy from retail remittances to enterprise payments infrastructure, with hubs planned in Nigeria, Canada and Kenya. The backdrop: Africa's cross-border payments market is projected at $1 trillion by 2035. Leatherback comes through a turbulent stretch — a 2023 Nigerian regulatory probe (its then-CEO was cleared) and a 2024–25 leadership overhaul that installed ex-Cellulant executive Ochebhoya Ekpete.

Zedcrest buys Leatherback outright — a Nigerian group turns an old stake into ownership of the cross-border rails

TechCabal · Exclusive · Opeyemi Kareem · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the near-term story is another tick in Africa's record M&A run. The deeper one is consolidation as strategy — when fresh equity is scarce, a strategic acquirer with a balance sheet rolls up infrastructure it would once have merely partnered with, and Zedcrest is assembling an integrated financial-services group by buying the enterprise-payments layer rather than building it. Turning a five-year-old minority stake into control is the tell: the relationship, and the diligence, were already done.

TechCabal — “Zedcrest Group acquires cross-border payments startup Leatherback in global fintech push”

The structural read is that cross-border payments is an infrastructure business, and infrastructure businesses consolidate into the hands of whoever can fund licences, compliance and banking partnerships at scale — not whoever has the cleverest app. Zedcrest's logic is explicit: it already owns a brokerage and an asset manager, and Leatherback gives it the payments rail, so the group becomes an integrated stack rather than a set of point products. Leatherback's own pivot — away from competing for retail remittance customers and toward being "the financial rails powering businesses" — is the same "own the plumbing" instinct that runs through this whole page. Buying rather than building is the rational move when the target has already built the core banking system and ledger, and when a scarce-equity market makes a trade sale the founder's realistic exit.

A five-year minority stake becomes control. When fresh equity dries up, the strategic acquirer with a balance sheet is the buyer of last resort — and increasingly that acquirer is African.

The threads: to Friday's item 1 — this is one of the record 63 M&A deals the concentration data flagged, the exit that replaces the next round that isn't coming. To Friday's item 2 — the same consolidation wave, but the buyer is a Nigerian financial group, not an SA bank, and the target is UK-founded: the roll-up is crossing borders in both directions. To today's item 3 — Zedcrest buys the corridor, Quidax builds it with stablecoins; two routes into the same $1tn opportunity. The sceptic's ledger: the price is undisclosed, so the "turned a stake into ownership" framing could equally describe a rescue as a triumph; Leatherback's 2023 regulatory probe and leadership churn are real integration risk; and cross-border payments is a crowded, thin-margin arena where a holding-company roll-up can become a grab-bag rather than a champion. The forward marker: whether Zedcrest's "buy the rails" strategy produces a genuinely integrated pan-African financial group, or a collection of assets under one logo.

Nigeria · Stablecoins · Cross-border Briefing Tests a thesis
Quidax — the first digital-assets exchange to win a provisional licence from Nigeria's Securities and Exchange Commission — has expanded its stablecoin settlement infrastructure to 21+ countries and 14 currencies (supporting USDT, XAUT, USAT; Naira, Cedi, CFA francs, USD and more). It claims cross-border settlement in under 48 hours with no correspondent bank, at a cost in line with the G20/UN 5% target, and counts Tether and Chainalysis as partners while powering 5,000+ businesses. The framing is pointed: Africa loses an estimated $5bn a year to cross-border fees, and a payment from Accra to Durban can route through a European correspondent bank, take up to 7 days and cost up to 13% of value.

Quidax lays stablecoin rails across 21 countries — the "African border levy" meets a compliance-first workaround

TechAfrica News · Fintech · Jennifer Onyeagoro · Wed 29 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a crypto exchange extending its footprint. The more interesting one is the regulatory posture — Quidax is leading with an SEC licence and a Chainalysis partnership rather than routing around the rules, betting that "compliant stablecoin rails" can beat both the correspondent-banking system on cost and unlicensed crypto on trust. In African cross-border, the moat may turn out to be the licence, not the technology.

TechAfrica News — “Quidax Expands Stablecoin Payment Infrastructure to 21 Countries and 14 Currencies”

The structural read is that the correspondent-banking system is a tax on every intra-African payment — a Ghana-to-South Africa transfer priced through Europe is the purest example of the "African border levy" Quidax names — and stablecoins are the first credible technology to route around it at scale. But the technology has been available for years; what is new, and what makes this worth surfacing, is that a licensed exchange is building the rails inside the regulatory perimeter rather than outside it, with Chainalysis compliance and an SEC provisional licence as the pitch. That reframes the competition: not crypto-versus-banks, but compliant-rails-versus-everyone, and it is a bet that African regulators will tolerate dollar-denominated stablecoin settlement so long as it is auditable.

A payment from Accra to Durban can take seven days and cost 13%, routed through a bank in Europe. The wager here is that the winning workaround is the licensed one, not the fastest one.

The threads: to Friday's item 4 — this is the cross-border sibling of Luno's rand-backed Zaru stablecoin and crypto-as-a-service, the same "sell the rail, keep the compliance" model aimed at settlement rather than retail. To Friday's item 3 — Kenya's push to make AI decisions auditable and the compliance-first crypto posture are two faces of the same continental shift: legitimacy is becoming a feature, not a friction. To today's item 2 — Zedcrest buys the corridor, Quidax builds it; two strategies into one $1tn market. The sceptic's ledger: the sub-48-hour, sub-5% settlement claims are self-reported and unaudited; a provisional SEC licence is not a full one, and stablecoin tolerance across 21 jurisdictions is uneven and reversible; and dollar-stablecoin rails sit in direct tension with central banks defending capital controls and local currencies. The forward marker: whether "compliant crypto rails" become the default African cross-border layer, or get squeezed between the banks they undercut and the regulators they depend on.

Morocco · Capital markets · MedTech Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
T2S Group Holding — Morocco's leading medical-technology group (founded 1992, 420+ staff, partnerships with GE HealthCare, BioMérieux, Boston Scientific and Accuray, spanning imaging and radiotherapy equipment, radiopharmaceuticals and hospital software) — debuted on the Casablanca Stock Exchange, the market's first listing of 2026. The ~MAD 1.1bn ($120m) offer (a MAD 350m capital raise plus a MAD 750m secondary sale) priced at MAD 223/share drew 111,149 subscribers and MAD 48.1bn of demand — roughly 44× oversubscribed, with 98.98% of subscribers individuals. T2S targets MAD 2.14bn of 2026 revenue and aims to double to MAD 4.17bn by 2030, expanding into francophone sub-Saharan Africa. The stock fell ~9.5% on its second day. It joins a Moroccan IPO wave (Cash Plus 64×, SGTM 34×).

Morocco prices a home-grown medtech champion at 44x demand — the "list it at home" story finds a new market

African Markets · Casablanca (BVC) · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a hot IPO. The deeper one is two signals at once — the depth of domestic retail appetite for a real, profitable, sub-Saharan-expanding operator (44×, almost entirely individuals), and Casablanca joining the continent's "price it at home" wave alongside Optasia on the JSE and Angola's Unitel on BODIVA. The ~9.5% second-day fall is the counter-signal that says how much of that 44× was scarcity rather than conviction.

Deep home demand, thin float —44× cover on a 99%-retail book, and a day-two wobble to match
African Markets — “T2S Group debuts on the Casablanca Stock Exchange after successful IPO”

The structural read is that a functioning domestic exchange is the same infrastructure for an equity ecosystem that a credible exit is for venture — and Morocco is quietly demonstrating one. Three heavily oversubscribed listings in eight months (Cash Plus, SGTM, now T2S) is not luck; it is a pool of domestic retail and institutional savings that has found local equity worth owning, and it lets a real operator raise growth capital and sell down founders at home rather than waiting for a foreign buyer or an offshore listing. T2S is a good test case because it is not a story stock: 34 years old, profitable, GE HealthCare partnership since inception, and a concrete plan to push imaging and diagnostics into francophone Africa. The 44× cover says the appetite is there; the second-day slide says price discovery on a 99%-retail book is violent.

Three heavily oversubscribed Casablanca IPOs in eight months is not luck — it is domestic savings deciding local equity is worth owning. The day-two drop is the reminder that scarcity is not the same as conviction.

The threads: to Friday's item 2 — Optasia's R23.5bn JSE listing was cited there as proof African companies can exit at scale on a home public market; T2S is the North African instance of the same "price it at home" thesis. To Friday's item 1 — this is where the concentration story points: capital flowing to later, profitable, listable names, not first cheques. To Issue 055's Angola/Unitel-on-BODIVA card — another national champion pricing on its home exchange rather than offshore, the offshore counter-route being Airtel Money's mooted London listing. The forward marker: whether the Casablanca listing wave broadens Africa's chronically shallow domestic equity markets into a durable exit venue, or proves to be a local liquidity bubble that the aftermarket quietly deflates.

Pan-Africa · AI · SME software Briefing Tests a thesis
German software startup Knowlix AI has launched an AI-powered business-management platform across 29 African markets at once — including South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt, Zambia, Rwanda, Tanzania, Uganda and Senegal — targeting small and medium enterprises. It bundles CRM, invoicing, inventory, project tracking and more into one system, with local tax and accounting rules built in per country. Its AI assistant automates routine admin (generating invoices, organising meeting notes, managing inventory), but business owners retain final approval over key actions. It enters a crowded field against Odoo, Zoho, HubSpot, Microsoft and Sage, plus a growing set of African business-software firms.

A German startup wires an AI back-office into 29 African markets at once — with a human keeping the final say

FollowICT · African Startup News · Mohamed Fathy · Fri 31 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is another SME SaaS entrant in a crowded category. The two things worth watching are the go-to-market and the design choice — a 29-market simultaneous launch with localised tax and accounting, betting that AI collapses the cost of the localisation that usually protects incumbents; and "human retains final approval" shipped as a headline feature, which is precisely the meaningful-human-control posture Kenya is now trying to legislate.

FollowICT — “African Startup News (July 31, 2026)”

The structural read is that the moat in African SME software has never been features — it has been localisation: every market's tax code, invoice format, currency and accounting convention is a separate build, and that per-country cost is what has kept the field fragmented and protected regional incumbents like Sage. Knowlix's bet is that AI changes the unit economics of that localisation, letting a newcomer stand up 29 markets simultaneously where a traditional vendor would roll out one at a time. Whether or not Knowlix specifically wins, that is the thesis worth tracking: if AI genuinely lowers the cost of compliance-heavy localisation, the incumbents' deepest moat erodes. The second, quieter signal is the "human keeps final approval" framing — automation sold with its own oversight switch, because the market (and soon the regulator) is starting to price opacity.

The moat in African SME software was never features — it was localising to 29 tax codes. The bet here is that AI melts exactly that moat.

The threads: to Friday's item 3 — Kenya wants to legislate "meaningful human control" over automated decisions; Knowlix is shipping the human veto as a product feature, the market and the regulator converging on the same design from opposite ends. To Friday's item 4 — there automation showed up as a smaller Luno workforce; here it is the product itself, the same force sold as capability rather than cost-cut. To the AI-deployment thesis Chronos cares about — this is a concrete case of AI being used to attack an incumbent moat rather than as a back-office efficiency. The sceptic's ledger: a 29-market simultaneous launch is a press-release ambition, not demonstrated traction, and localised tax compliance across 29 jurisdictions is exactly where such platforms usually break; the incumbents (Sage, Odoo) are entrenched with distribution and trust; and a German startup with no visible African footprint claiming instant continental coverage should carry a heavy discount. The forward marker: whether AI actually lets a newcomer undercut the localisation moat — the single question that decides whether African SME software stays fragmented and defensible or gets flattened.

AUDA-NEPAD (the African Union's development agency) and X Green Capital have launched the Africa Green Transition PPP Fund (AGTPF), targeting $584m (€500m) in first-phase capital for renewable energy, electricity transmission, water-energy infrastructure, industrial decarbonisation and climate-resilient projects. AUDA-NEPAD launches a $584m fund to turn green blueprints into financed steel — the missing "financial-close" layer

Empower Africa — “AUDA-NEPAD Launches $584 Million Africa Green Transition Fund”

"Africa does not lack green projects. It lacks the platforms that turn preparation into investment." The bet is that the constraint is structuring, not money — and that African pension capital will follow bankability.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Red-team your own conclusion — make Claude build the strongest case against you

When you have reached a view — back the deal, pass, hire, restructure — the failure mode is almost never bad analysis. It is confirmation: once you have a conclusion, you unconsciously recruit evidence for it and skim past the evidence against. The cheap, high-leverage habit is to externalise the sceptic you already keep in your head but, under time pressure, stop listening to. Paste your conclusion and your reasoning to Claude and give it one instruction: "You are the partner most likely to vote against this. Give me the three strongest arguments to kill it, the evidence I'm underweighting, and the single fact that — if it turned out to be true — would flip my decision." Then either defend your view against that, or update it. What you get back is a pre-mortem you would otherwise only write after the loss, delivered in the time it takes to read it. It works on an IC memo, a board position, a hire, or a personal call — anywhere the danger is that you have already decided.

A worked example

"Here's my case for backing this company and the three reasons I find it compelling. Now argue the opposite as hard as you can, as if you're the IC member who wants to vote no — and end with the one disconfirming fact I should go and find before the meeting, not after." What comes back is your own blind spot, named. You spend two minutes reading it; it can save you a quarter of arguing the wrong side.
