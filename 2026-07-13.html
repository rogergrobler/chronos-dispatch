Global · Fintech · Regulation Analysis Thesis test
Simon Taylor's Sunday Brainfood lands on one question: what happens after everyone gets the licence? Circle received final OCC approval to open First National Digital Currency Bank, operating as Circle National Trust — a bank in the narrow sense: no deposits, no loans, no FDIC insurance, but the infrastructure behind USDC now sits under direct federal supervision, comfortably before the GENIUS Act rules bite. The same fortnight: Klarna filed with Utah and the FDIC for an industrial bank (30 million American users, $91.3bn of credit extended since 2019), and Sony — a PlayStation maker — won conditional OCC approval for Connectia Trust, a dollar-stablecoin issuer, over the objections of the big-bank lobby. American Banker counts roughly two dozen fintechs, crypto firms and payment companies that have applied for or conditionally received charters in the first half of 2026. Taylor's punchline: the long drought of de novo charters made them look impossible to get. They weren't — nobody was granting them.

Getting the charter was the easy part: Circle becomes a bank in the fortnight everyone else does too

Fintech Brainfood (Simon Taylor) · Fintech · Sun 12 Jul 2026

In plain English: the scarce asset in US fintech just moved. When charters were unobtainable, holding one was the moat; when the OCC approves Circle, Ripple, Paxos, BitGo, Fidelity, Bridge, Crypto.com and Sony inside eight months, the moat relocates to the compliance operation behind the licence — "keeping a charter is the work, times ten, forever," as Taylor puts it, citing Anchorage's path from 2021 trust charter to 2022 AML consent order. Two details worth the read: management of the actual USDC reserve is listed only as a "future capability" of Circle's new bank, and the real prize — a Fed master account, the thing that makes a dollar finally a dollar — sits in the Fed's slowest review lane.

Fintech Brainfood — “Getting a charter was the easy part”

For a partnership that has spent years around bank licences, the repricing here is the story. A charter regime that moves from scarcity to abundance shifts the value to exactly the things Issue 012's Paga item identified from Lagos: regulatory relationships, operational tooling, fraud monitoring, settlement experience — the moat you only acquire by clearing millions of transactions under supervision. Klarna's filing makes the economics explicit: it has been a licensed European bank since 2017, funding its book with Swedish and German deposits, while in America it rents WebBank and borrows at market rates. The charter is how the European margin crosses the Atlantic.

“Charters have stopped being rare… The long drought of new de novo charters made these look impossible to get. They weren't. Nobody was granting them.”

The thread runs straight through this page's stablecoin coverage: OpenUSD's 140-company float giveaway (Issue 038), Circle buying onto Flutterwave's rails (Issue 040), Djamo negotiating its BCEAO sandbox (Issue 041). The issuer that just became a federally supervised bank is the same issuer collecting African distribution — and federal supervision now includes obligations Circle has historically resisted, like freezing and burning tokens on lawful order, in the same week a Wisconsin prosecutor accused it of obstructing a warrant. The forward question for the African corridors pricing themselves off USDC: does a supervised, freeze-capable dollar token make the rail more bankable for regulators — or less usable for the flows that made it grow?

Central Africa · Payments · Chart Analysis Longitudinal
Afreximbank announced on Thursday that the Bank of Central African States (BEAC) has joined the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System — and because BEAC is the central bank of a six-country monetary union, one signature brings Cameroon, Gabon, the Republic of Congo, the Central African Republic, Equatorial Guinea and Chad into the network at once, with CEMAC's commercial banks connecting by end-2026. PAPSS now covers 28 African countries, 16 payment switches, more than 190 commercial banks and fintechs, and over 250 financial institutions. The system, built by Afreximbank with the African Union and the AfCFTA secretariat, settles cross-border payments directly in local currencies instead of routing them through correspondent banks in New York, London or Paris — and claims transaction-cost reductions of up to 27% in participating markets. Later this year comes the bigger move: a pilot connecting BEAC with BCEAO, the West African central bank serving eight countries — the first direct payment corridor between West and Central Africa.

One signature, six countries: Central Africa plugs into the continent's own settlement rail

Ecofin Agency (Chamberline Moko) · TechCabal Daily · Fri 10 Jul 2026

In plain English: the significance is the unit of accession. PAPSS has been adding countries one at a time since 2022; a monetary union joining as a bloc changes the arithmetic and the precedent — Africa's two big Francophone currency zones are now one pilot away from settling each other directly, in their own currencies, without a dollar or euro leg in the middle. BEAC governor Yvon Sana Bangui, who also chairs the Association of African Central Banks, framed it as trade integration through financial plumbing.

A settlement network compounding by blocs, not by countries
Ecofin Agency — “CEMAC Banks to Join PAPSS by End-2026”

This page has spent a month tracking how African money finds its dollar-settlement layer — DR Congo's dollar-settled mobile money, Djamo negotiating a stablecoin sandbox with this very BCEAO (Issue 041), crypto-backed loans filling the credit gap. PAPSS is the counter-programme: settlement that never leaves African currencies at all. Both approaches are building the same asset — the rail — and the Flutterwave lesson (Issue 040) applies to rails of every flag: whoever owns the rail sets the terms for everyone renting it. Here the owner is Afreximbank and the African Union, which makes PAPSS as much an industrial policy as a payment system.

“One signature brings six countries into the network simultaneously… the first direct payment corridor between West and Central Africa.”

The structural read: PAPSS's binding constraint has never been technology — it is liquidity and trust between central banks, which is why accession by monetary bloc matters more than accession by country. A BEAC–BCEAO corridor would put fourteen Francophone economies on direct local-currency settlement, exactly the trade-finance plumbing the AfCFTA has lacked. The open question is the one the stablecoin thread keeps asking from the other side: when a business in Douala can settle Lagos in naira via PAPSS or in USDC via a licensed issuer, which rail wins — the sovereign one with mandate and reach, or the private one with speed and a dollar peg? The next twelve months of corridor volumes, not white papers, will answer it.

Global · AI · Deployment Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Ramp, the US spend-management platform, launched “Ramp for agents” — cards, transfers and bank accounts issued to AI agents, with controls governing every dollar they move. Each agent gets its own identity, its own budget, its own spend limits and its own audit trail. Credentials expire on a schedule, exceptions route to a human for approval, and every transaction lands in the dashboard attributed to the agent that made it, receipt attached. Setup is a connector inside Claude, ChatGPT, Cursor or Perplexity; for websites that don't speak agent protocols — most of the internet — a browser automation drives the checkout while the agent card pays. Simon Taylor's framing in the same Sunday issue: the mental model is agents as employees — and an employee who works around the clock, spends in milliseconds and occasionally hallucinates a purchase is exactly the customer a spend-controls company was built for.

Agents get the corporate card: identity, budget and audit trail per AI employee

Ramp, via Fintech Brainfood · AI × Fintech · Sun 12 Jul 2026

In plain English: this is the corporate half of agentic commerce arriving before the consumer half, and the deployment pattern is the transferable part — no integration project, no new protocol, just a connector into the assistant a team already uses. The governance primitives are the ones any operating company will need the moment agents do real work: one identity per agent, one budget per agent, human sign-off on exceptions, and attribution on every action. That is an organisational design pattern wearing a fintech product's clothes.

Fintech Brainfood — “Ramp launches Ramp for agents”

Run this against the agentic-workflows thread from the other direction. The Remote Labor Index (Issue 040) measured agents completing paid freelance work; Microsoft's “ownership, not access” line (Issue 017) framed who benefits; this launch supplies the missing administrative layer — because once an agent can complete work, the binding constraint moves from capability to accountability, and accountability is precisely what expense policy, credential expiry and receipts-per-transaction solve. The same week, a startup called Humanos is building the neutral version of the same primitives — mandate, limit, receipt — across Visa's, Mastercard's, Stripe's and Google's competing agent-authorisation standards, which tells you a standards fight is already underway one layer down.

“An identity, a card, a budget, an expense policy, an approval chain, an audit trail… agents as employees.”

For any operating company, the practical read is that the governance template now exists off the shelf: treat every deployed agent as a junior hire with a limit and a ledger line, not as software with a password. The strategic read loops back to Friday's Ben Evans lead — if value accrues to whoever owns the user touchpoint, then watch where agent spending data pools. The platform that holds the agent's card sees the agent's behaviour; the lab that hosts the agent sees the intent; the bank underneath sees neither. The forward question: when procurement, subscriptions and compute purchasing all route through agent cards, does the spend-management layer quietly become the system of record for how a company actually operates?

South Africa · Banking · Strategy Briefing Hyper-local
Daily Maverick's Lindsey Schutters attended Capitec's #BizTalks in Cape Town on Sunday — the coming-out party for its business banking ambitions. The base: business banking contributed 5% of FY26 earnings (about R840m), growing headline earnings 20%; active business clients up 71% to 456,000; business app users up 65% to 172,000; and the zero-monthly-fee Entrepreneur Account, launched December 2025, onboarded 78,000 informal businesses by February. The credit book is deliberately conservative — a 2.4% business credit-loss ratio against 8.2% in personal banking. The contested ground is mid-corporates (R100m–R1.5bn revenue), where returns on equity exceed 30%, roughly double retail — and where Standard Bank holds a dominant 28% share and is targeting R725bn of deposits by 2028. Capitec sits below 2% of the business banking market, with a R407bn market value and 25 million retail clients behind it. CEO Graham Lee on the systems: “We can 10x tomorrow what our current system bodies are. We design that deliberately.”

Capitec points its retail flywheel at business banking — from under 2% of the market

Daily Maverick (Lindsey Schutters) · Banking · Sun 12 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the playbook is the same one that took retail — scale drives unit costs down, savings pass back as lower fees, clients bring data, data sharpens credit scoring and business insights — now aimed at the most profitable segment in South African banking, with every incumbent aiming at the same mid-corporate pool. And the self-identified risk is reputational carry-over: the payday-outage years were survivable with retail customers and clever marketing; a business that cannot transact does not meme about it, it leaves. Lee's candour on the record — “we were stupid enough to make those mistakes, but not stupid enough to not learn from them” — is itself a data point on the culture.

Nigeria · Fintech · Consolidation Briefing Longitudinal
Gigbanc, the Lagos-built neobank for Africa's freelancers, creators and remote workers, is winding down after three years and pursuing an acquisition by an undisclosed Nigerian fintech-infrastructure provider. The numbers at closure: more than 150,000 users across 30+ countries, over ₦10bn (about $7.2m) processed in its lifetime, multi-currency wallets in dollars, euros and naira, and payouts to more than 200 Nigerian banks. Customers have until 31 July to withdraw. Co-founder Paul Omoregie Okundaye's diagnosis: a fundraising climate that never opened — and the high KYC and infrastructure costs of running a B2C cross-border product. The context is the H1 2026 funding pattern: $1.44bn raised, up 1.4% year on year, but across only 146 disclosed deals versus 252 in H1 2025 — fewer, larger rounds, concentrated on companies already at scale. Gigbanc follows Chimoney (May) and FoodCourt (March) into the same funnel: shut down, or sell to whoever remains.

The neobank for Africa's freelancers runs out of road — 150,000 users, no Series A

TechCabal (Opeyemi Kareem) · Fintech · Fri 10 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the mechanical cause is the barbell — capital now skips the middle of the market, so sub-scale B2C fintechs with heavy compliance costs cannot bridge to profitability, and infrastructure providers pick up their books at wind-down prices. And the uncomfortable macro echo: Gigbanc's customer was the African freelancer earning from overseas — the precise segment the Remote Labor Index (Issue 040) showed AI beginning to compete against — meaning the demand side of this business was thinning at the same time as its funding side. When a thesis dies, it is worth writing down which of the two killed it first.

Ghana · Telecoms · Regulation Briefing Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Ghana is preparing to sell 5G licences to mobile operators, with MTN Ghana and Telecel already signalling they will bid — a reversal of the 2024 decision to hand Next-Gen InfraCo (NGIC), a state-backed wholesale provider, exclusive rights to the country's 5G network for a decade, with telcos required to lease capacity rather than build their own. The experiment's scoreboard: NGIC commercially launched in March, no major operator signed on — Telecel waited to see how spectrum would be allocated after the regulator moved to end NGIC's exclusivity — and the network stands at roughly 49 operational 5G sites against a government target of 1,200 by 2027. The stated logic of the monopoly was lower broadband costs through shared infrastructure; the observed result was that nobody had an incentive to build.

Ghana's single-operator 5G experiment ends at 49 towers

TechCabal Daily (Opeyemi Kareem) · Telecoms · Fri 10 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is a clean natural experiment in infrastructure design — a state-anchored wholesale monopoly versus licensed operators who own their economics — and it resolved in under two years, at 4% of the tower target. And the wider pattern matters for every connectivity-dependent thesis on the continent: spectrum policy, not technology, keeps deciding the pace at which African markets come online, and governments that mistake control for coordination pay for it in coverage. Ghana conceding the point quickly is the encouraging half of the story.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Stop re-briefing Claude every morning — set your standing instructions once

Friday's tip was about saving your best prompts. Today's is about the sentence you type before every one of them — “be direct, UK English, lead with the conclusion, no preamble” — which most people retype dozens of times a week. Every serious AI assistant now has a standing-instructions setting (Claude calls them preferences and project instructions; others call them custom instructions). Whatever you put there is silently prepended to every conversation, which means your working style becomes the default rather than the request. The compounding effect is real: a well-written instruction block is the difference between an assistant that starts every session as a stranger and one that starts every session already briefed. Write it once, in your own words, and revise it the way you would coach a new hire — when an answer annoys you, ask what standing instruction would have prevented it, and add that line.

A worked example

A block that earns its keep: “UK English, always. Lead with the conclusion, support with data, end with the open question. No preamble, no flattery, no bullet-point avalanches — write in prose unless I ask for a table. When I ask for analysis, give me the strongest case against your own answer in the final paragraph. If information might be stale, say so and check. My default context: private-markets investing, Southern Africa and Singapore time zones.” Six sentences, typed once — and every future conversation starts on the 22nd floor instead of the lobby.
