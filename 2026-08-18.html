Pan-Africa · Venture Capital · Fund Design Analysis Chart of the day
Olu Oyinsan, general partner of Oui Capital, has given the most candid public account yet of how an African seed fund actually returned capital — and why he thinks the asset class is failing. In January 2025 Oui told investors it had returned its $4m debut fund in full, one of very few African managers to do so this cycle. The headline driver was a $150,000 cheque into Moniepoint in 2019, worth roughly $8m when the fintech crossed $1bn five years later — a 53x single-position return. But Oyinsan's point is the opposite of the one usually drawn: Fund I is a 4x portfolio, and would still have been ~2x without Moniepoint, helped by a second realised exit (US healthcare company AMOpportunities). The post-mortem is the valuable part: five of the six companies written off early were its smallest cheques — $25,000 and $40,000 positions taken for optionality rather than conviction. Fund II, deploying since 2022, is an explicit correction: cheques up to $750,000 (averaging $400–500k, up to $1m over two rounds), ownership targets lifted from 2–3% to 5–10%, and portfolio count cut from ~20 to 10–11. Seven companies are in so far. Oyinsan's structural claims: no African seed fund should exceed $50m (maximum $100m) if it intends to return capital; markets are smaller than the population implies once illiteracy, connectivity and purchasing power are stripped out; "you cannot innovate your way out of core underdevelopment"; and African venture is in an existential crisis, drifting toward what he calls "venture philanthropy" reported in non-financial metrics. He also names what he now declines to fund: "SaaS companies that AI prompts can build."

The fund that returned 53x on one cheque says the lesson is the opposite — five of its six write-offs were the small bets it made just to keep an option open

TechCabal · Ask An Investor · Muktar Oladunmade · Mon 17 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a GP interview about a good outcome. The one that matters is a rare piece of honest attribution — a manager separating the luck from the method, in public, with the numbers attached. Almost every fund with a 53x position lets that position tell the story. Oyinsan does the harder thing and reports what the portfolio did without it, then publishes the failure analysis showing that low-conviction cheques were not cheap options but the most expensive thing the fund owned — because they consumed the scarce resource, which is partner time, not money. It threads straight from Issue 065's finding that three-quarters of July's African capital arrived as debt: here is why the equity alternative is struggling to earn its place.

Fewer companies, larger cheques, more ownership — the correction a returned fund made to itself
TechCabal — “Oui Capital's Olu Oyinsan on why African VC remains in an existential crisis”

The structural read is that this is a fund manager publishing his own attribution analysis, which is rarer and more useful than any single return figure. The 53x on Moniepoint is the number that will travel; the number that should travel is the ~2x ex-Moniepoint, because that is the one that says the method worked rather than the lottery ticket. And the failure analysis is sharper still. The conventional defence of a small cheque is that it is a cheap option — limited downside, unlimited upside, why not. Oyinsan's data says the opposite: five of six early write-offs were the smallest positions, and the true cost was never the $25,000. It was that struggling companies consume partner attention in inverse proportion to their contribution, so the low-conviction position taxes the very resource the winners need. In a power-law business the scarce input is not capital but the bandwidth to double down, and an option that quietly draws down that bandwidth is not cheap at all. That reframing — time as the binding constraint, not money — applies well beyond a seed fund.

The cheque you write to keep an option open is not a cheap option. It is a claim on the only thing you cannot buy more of, which is the attention of the people who are supposed to be helping the winners.

The threads: to Issue 065's item 3 (75.2% of July's African capital arrived as debt) — that was the market voting with instruments; this is a practitioner explaining the vote from inside, and the two together say the African equity story has a returns problem rather than a narrative problem. To Issue 062's item 1 (the collapse in $100k–$1m rounds, 179 to 100 in six months) — read against this, that collapse looks less like capital scarcity and more like the deliberate death of exactly the low-conviction cheque size Oyinsan has stopped writing; the same behaviour, arrived at independently across the market. To Issue 060 and Issue 059 (Egypt's funds abandoning mark-ups for DPI, A15 at 10x DPI, Beltone banking partial exits) — North and West Africa converging on distributions as the only scoreboard that counts. To Issue 065's item 2 (E-finance buying 183,000 borrowers) — concentration as strategy, corporate edition. To the Chronos read on portfolio construction — the "$50m ceiling on an African seed fund" claim is a testable structural argument about where fee-driven AUM growth stops being compatible with returns, and it is worth marking against the funds now raising above it. Note also the throwaway line with the longest tail: he no longer funds "SaaS companies that AI prompts can build," which is a working diligence filter, not a slogan. The sceptic's ledger: this is a single GP with one returned $4m fund talking his own book while Fund II is still deploying and unrealised, and a 4x on $4m is a small sample that would not survive being called a track record anywhere else; the ex-Moniepoint 2x is self-reported and unaudited, and includes marks on companies that have not exited; the post-mortem correlating write-offs with cheque size may simply be reporting that early-stage failure is common and small cheques were common, which is not the same as causation; "no fund above $50m" is convenient for a manager who has not raised one; and concentrating 10 companies at 5–10% raises variance materially, which is the right trade only if the selection is genuinely better rather than merely bolder. The forward marker: Fund II's DPI when the investment period closes — if a concentrated 10-company portfolio cannot beat the sprayed 20, the thesis was style, not edge.

Pan-Africa · Payments · Settlement Analysis Chart of the day
Sabine Mensah, deputy CEO of AfricaNenda, sets out in detail why Africa's cross-border payment problem has quietly relocated — from the rails, which are close to solved, to settlement, which is not. The continent has more than 40 currencies (she puts it at ~42), many not directly convertible, forcing providers into correspondent banking, settlement banks and pre-funded accounts held in multiple countries and currencies. Her central correction: currency fragmentation does not prevent cross-border payments — they already happen at both wholesale and retail level — it just makes them expensive. The World Bank figure she cites: sending $200 across Africa costs 8.78%, roughly $16–17, against an SDG target of 3%, and a large share of that gap is the foreign-exchange step where a hard currency has to sit between two local ones. Four regional instant-payment systems are being built — the EAC masterplan, the WAEMU system spanning eight countries, GIMAC Pay across six in Central Africa, and TCIB targeting SADC's 16 (roughly six to eight onboarded) — and connecting them would reach 60–70% of African countries. Notably, TCIB settles in South African rand, making the rand a de facto regional settlement currency; the CFA franc plays that role in West and Central Africa. Her asks are regulatory rather than technical: harmonised payment, licencing and settlement rules, plus licence passporting across borders. The prize she attaches: AfCFTA's ambition to lift intra-African trade from ~15% to 50–60%. She also credits Nigeria as the continent's only "mature" instant-payment system, built on NIPS plus BVN digital identity, phased use cases, near-zero fees and real-time fraud dashboards.

Africa nearly solved the payment rail and discovered the cost had moved — 42 currencies, 8.78% to send $200, and a settlement layer nobody owns

TechCabal · Ecosystem · Kenn Abuya · Mon 17 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is an interview about payment interoperability. The one that matters is a precise relocation of where the money is actually lost — the industry has spent a decade building message-passing between systems, and the toll booth turns out to sit one layer down, in the currency conversion and pre-funded float that no single party owns or is incentivised to compress. It threads directly into Issue 065's Standard Bank item: holding 30% of South Africa's cross-border payment value is a fine position, but the 8.78% is the pool everyone in that value chain is swimming in. The most investable detail is the quietest one — the rand is already doing the settlement work for SADC.

Four rails, three settlement currencies, one gap — and the rand is already doing the work for Southern Africa
TechCabal — “Africa is building a single payments market but its currencies remain divided”

The structural read is that African payments has been solving the visible half of the problem. Technical interoperability — getting a message from a provider in Nairobi to a provider in Dar es Salaam — is genuinely hard engineering and it is largely done or in flight across four regional blocs. Settlement is the other half, and it is not an engineering problem at all: it is a balance-sheet and regulatory problem. Somebody has to hold pre-funded accounts in multiple currencies in multiple jurisdictions, absorb the FX risk between two currencies that will not exchange directly, and earn a return for doing it. That working capital is the real toll booth, and it explains the persistence of the 8.78% far better than any story about legacy technology. Which points at the genuinely interesting commercial question buried in the piece: whoever ends up owning multi-currency settlement across connected African rails is not a payments company, it is closer to a clearing bank — and Mensah's own examples show that role is already being filled by default rather than by design, with the CFA franc covering fourteen countries by monetary union and the rand doing it for SADC by operational convenience.

Interoperability moves the message. Settlement moves the money. Africa has spent a decade building the first and has largely left the second to whoever was willing to fund the float — which is how the rand quietly became a regional settlement currency without anyone deciding it should be.

The threads: to Issue 065's item 1 (Standard Bank's 30% of SA cross-border payment value, 19% Africa-wide) — the largest transactional franchise on the continent sits directly on top of this cost pool, and the same institution is the natural candidate to own rand-based SADC settlement, which is a materially different and more defensible business than payment processing. To Issue 063's item 3 (SARB withdraws PASA recognition and takes direct control of the national payment system, operations to PayInc by 2 Sep) — a regulator consolidating domestic authority over exactly the rail that TCIB settles through; the domestic and regional stories are the same story one level apart. To Issue 065's item 2 (E-finance buying the credit layer above its own rails) — another operator concluding the rail alone does not pay. To Issue 062 and Issue 061 (the rail thesis, and value migrating to whoever controls it) — this is the sharpest refinement of that thesis so far: controlling the rail is not the same as controlling the economics, because the economics have moved underneath it. To the Chronos read on payments and financial infrastructure — the diligence question on any African cross-border asset becomes "where in this chain does the FX spread and float income actually land, and do you own any of it?" The sceptic's ledger: this is an advocacy organisation making the case for the harmonisation agenda it exists to promote, and regulatory harmonisation across dozens of sovereign central banks is the slowest process in finance — the EAC common-currency agreement Mensah cites has been pending for many years and has not materialised; the 8.78% is a remittance-corridor figure and does not map cleanly onto commercial or wholesale flows, so it overstates the addressable pool for anyone building B2B; "connecting four systems reaches 60–70% of countries" counts countries, not volume, and the volume is concentrated in a handful of corridors; stablecoin rails are a live competitive answer to precisely this settlement problem and go unaddressed here; and the rand's role in TCIB carries the obvious corollary that SADC settlement inherits South African currency volatility. The forward marker: whether TCIB moves materially beyond six to eight of sixteen countries, and whether any regulator actually passports a payment licence across a border — that would be the first hard evidence the harmonisation agenda is more than a communiqué.

South Africa · Retail · Governance Briefing Structural design
Spar Group announced via SENS on Monday that both its chairman Mike Bosman and deputy chair Shirley Zinn have resigned with immediate effect. The wording is doing visible work: the statement says that "while Mr Bosman and Dr Zinn continue to have the full support of the Board, taking into consideration the context of the period that both these directors and the Board have recently experienced," they had separately concluded that stepping down was right for them and the company. The context matters — the board had publicly backed Bosman in May after shareholders called for his resignation, and Bosman had at one point served as executive chairman, running the company after the previous CEO resigned suddenly. The underlying commercial problem is structural: Spar's franchise model has become a liability against Shoprite, which owns its stores directly and can therefore drive its delivery business through them; and Spar's KwaZulu-Natal logistics failure is estimated to have cost around R1.5bn in turnover. Separately, the SAA board suspended "interim CEO" Matshela Seshibe on Friday 14 August, reportedly over matters at his previous role at Air Chefs, with his reputation already affected by events at Daybreak Farms. Stephen Grootes links both to African Bank's Kennedy Bungane, who resigned with immediate effect six months ago amid difficulties integrating three acquired financial institutions — noting the same board that removed him had signed off every transaction that created the problem.

Spar loses its chairman and deputy chair in one announcement — and the diagnosis underneath is a franchise model that gave away the ownership Shoprite kept

Daily Maverick · After the Bell · Stephen Grootes · Mon 17 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a governance shake-out at a listed retailer. The one that matters is a live demonstration that an ownership structure chosen for good reasons in one era becomes the binding constraint in the next — Spar's franchise model distributed capital risk and local incentive brilliantly for decades, and now cannot execute a centralised digital-delivery strategy because the person who owns the store is not the person who owns the strategy. It threads straight from Issue 063's Sixty60 item, where Shoprite's R25.5bn delivery business was explicitly built on ~3,000 stores it already owned. Grootes's sharper point is the governance one: boards keep firing the executive who sailed the course the board itself set.

Daily Maverick — “Spar and SAA lose their captains while becalmed in treacherous waters”

The structural read is that Spar is running a controlled experiment against Shoprite on the single question of who should own the last mile of retail, and it is losing it for reasons that predate any individual chairman. The franchise model solved a real problem: it put capital, local knowledge and owner-operator incentive into thousands of neighbourhood stores without the group funding them. What it also did was permanently separate the group's strategy from the assets that strategy needs to command. Sixty60 works because Shoprite can requisition shelf space, staff time, picking workflow and stock allocation in a store it owns, and can do it on Tuesday. Spar has to persuade independent business owners to fund and adopt a system whose main beneficiary is the group brand, one negotiation at a time. That is not a technology gap or an execution gap; it is a property-rights gap, and no amount of board renewal closes it. The KZN logistics failure and its ~R1.5bn cost then sits on top as the operational insult — the one function the group does own outright, and it broke.

The same board that wanted the captain off the bridge chose the compass bearings that got them there. Somebody signed off every transaction, and it was rarely the person who walked the plank.

The threads: to Issue 063's item 1 (Sixty60 at R25.5bn / $1.6bn, up 34.5%, nearly five times Shoprite's 7.1% supermarket growth, built on ~3,000 owned stores) — that item read as a digital-commerce success; read against today it is better understood as an ownership success that happened to express itself digitally, and Spar is the control group that makes the point legible. To today's item 1 (Oui Capital) — the identical lesson in a different asset class: distributed small stakes look like prudent risk-spreading and turn out to be the thing that prevents decisive action. To today's item 6 (Puck, Defector, Hunterbrook) — three media businesses deliberately re-cutting who owns the upside, which is the same question Spar answered forty years ago and cannot now re-answer. To Issue 062's item 5 (MTN's HEPS warning on the Irancell write-down) and the IHS take-private thread from Issues 060 and 061 — South African groups reversing or paying for structural decisions made in a previous cycle is becoming this dispatch's most recurrent pattern. To the Chronos read on portfolio governance — the transferable question for any board seat we hold: which of our structural choices were optimal for the last decade and are now the constraint, and would we notice before a chairman resigns? The sceptic's ledger: this is an opinion column, not reporting, and it explicitly reasons from resignations to distress without access to what actually happened in the boardroom; the "separately concluded" language is unusual but two directors leaving after a bruising period is not evidence of wrongdoing by anyone; the franchise-versus-owned argument is real but partial — franchise models throw off capital-light returns and Spar's problems in KZN were a self-inflicted logistics implementation rather than a franchising consequence; the SAA and African Bank cases are governance anecdotes bundled by theme rather than by cause; and the R1.5bn turnover estimate is exactly that. The forward marker: who Spar appoints as chairman and whether the appointment comes with any mandate to change the franchise economics — a caretaker appointment would say the board still believes this is a people problem.

DR Congo · Telecoms · Infrastructure Briefing Build vs rent
Airtel Africa and Starlink have launched a satellite-to-mobile (Direct-to-Cell) service in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, letting Airtel subscribers reach mobile data and messaging directly through the satellite network in areas no tower serves. Coverage is deliberately modest — light browsing, WhatsApp and SMS — and critically it works on compatible low-end Android handsets rather than specialist hardware. The launch follows a strategic partnership signed in December 2025 to introduce Direct-to-Cell across 25 African countries, and successful pilot testing in Kenya earlier this year. Airtel has been building toward this for years, having signed a backhaul deal with SpaceX in 2024 to route traffic from remote base stations via satellite; this goes a step further by removing the base station from the path entirely. The market context is the point: the DRC has roughly 2.3m km² of largely rural territory and internet penetration of about 30.5%. Airtel operates in 14 African markets, and the economics being tested here — universal coverage without building thousands of physical towers across difficult terrain — are portable to the other 13.

Airtel declines to build towers across 2.3m km² of Congo and rents the sky instead — the coverage problem solved from orbit, on a low-end Android

TechCabal Daily · Zia Yusuf & Yemi Kareem · Mon 17 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a satellite connectivity launch in a hard market. The one that matters is a large operator publicly concluding that the marginal rural tower will never be built — and that the last stretch of the coverage curve, the part every African telco has carried as an unfunded universal-service obligation, is now cheaper to rent from orbit than to construct. It threads sharply against Issue 065's ASRI item, where South Africa is trying to build indigenous launch capability on a tenth of the needed funding: Airtel skips the entire sovereignty question and buys the capability as a service today. The strategic cost is that it hands a permanent position in its own coverage map to a supplier it does not control.

TechCabal Daily — “Airtel and Starlink bring satellites to DR Congo”

The structural read is that this quietly rewrites the cost curve that has governed African telecoms economics for twenty-five years. The industry's defining constraint has always been that coverage costs rise steeply as population density falls, so the last 20–30% of a country's geography never earns its tower and gets covered, if at all, by regulatory obligation rather than by economics. Direct-to-Cell attacks that constraint at exactly the right end: it is bad at capacity and fine at reach, which is precisely the inverse of what a tower is good at, and therefore complementary rather than substitutive. That the service runs on low-end Android handsets rather than satellite phones is the detail that makes it a mass-market product instead of an enterprise one. But the ownership question that runs through today's edition applies here with force. A tower is an asset on Airtel's balance sheet that it controls, depreciates and can sell — as IHS and MTN have spent two years demonstrating. Constellation access is a contract. Airtel gets coverage without capital, and in exchange a third party acquires a durable position in the least contestable part of its network.

The tower was an asset you owned, financed and could eventually sell to somebody like IHS. The satellite is a contract you renew. Both deliver a signal; only one of them is still yours when the terms change.

The threads: to Issue 065's item 4 (UKZN's ASRI targeting a 2030 orbital launch with R100m against a R3bn requirement) — the two together frame the continent's sharpest build-or-rent trade: indigenous capability is slow, underfunded and cannot be withdrawn, while rented capability is immediate, cheap and revocable, and there is no evidence anyone is choosing between them deliberately. To Issue 061's item 4 and Issue 060's item 5 (IHS completing its ~9,000-tower Latin American exit to Macquarie; MTN winning approval to take IHS private at ~$6.2bn) — the tower asset class is being re-consolidated onto operator balance sheets at exactly the moment a technology arrives that makes the marginal tower unnecessary, which is a timing question worth putting to anyone holding African tower exposure. To Issue 061's item 1 (Spiro's battery-swap density following policy rather than headcount) — another case where the physical-infrastructure question decides the market, not the product. To Issue 065's item 1 (Standard Bank) and today's item 2 — own the rail or rent it, asked in a third industry. To the Chronos read on infrastructure and connectivity — for any African digital-services thesis, the addressable-market assumption that quietly rested on tower coverage may now need re-drawing. The sceptic's ledger: Direct-to-Cell is a very thin pipe — SMS and light messaging is not internet access, and describing it as ending the era of the unreachable subscriber flatters it considerably; ARPU from newly covered rural subscribers in the DRC will be extremely low and may not cover the wholesale cost of the satellite capacity; the announcement is a launch rather than a disclosed commercial arrangement, with no published terms, pricing or revenue share; regulatory treatment of satellite-direct services is unsettled across most of the 25 target markets and spectrum questions are unresolved; and there is real strategic risk in an operator ceding rural coverage to a single non-African supplier with pricing power and its own ambitions. The forward marker: whether Airtel discloses any subscriber or revenue contribution from Direct-to-Cell markets at its next results, and whether the Kenya pilot converts into a commercial launch — absent numbers, this is a coverage-map press release.

South Africa · Telecoms · Regulation Briefing Regulatory
ICASA, South Africa's communications regulator, has retracted its accusation that MTN and Vodacom colluded in opposing new data-expiry rules, withdrawing the claim on Friday on the basis that it had no evidence. The regulator had raised the suspicion in an August parliamentary briefing after both operators separately filed High Court applications in July challenging parts of the regulations. The underlying rule survives untouched: from January 2027, operators must allow unused data, voice and SMS bundles to roll over at least once, free of charge — the culmination of a process running since 2022. MTN's stated objections are commercially specific: it argues the rules could reduce competition and raise prices by removing operators' ability to offer cheaper bundles without rollover or transfer features, and that they create an uneven playing field because MVNOs are not subject to the same requirements. Vodacom has filed separately. South African consumers currently forfeit eligible unused data when bundles expire, which is the value the rule reassigns. The regulator is defending the regulations while abandoning the collusion charge.

ICASA withdraws the collusion charge but keeps the rule — from January 2027 the unused data belongs to the person who paid for it

TechCabal Daily · Zia Yusuf & Yemi Kareem · Mon 17 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a regulator walking back an unevidenced allegation. The one that matters is a transfer of property in something the industry has always booked as its own — expired data is breakage, it is high-margin, it is recognised revenue nobody had to deliver a service for, and from January 2027 a meaningful slice of it goes back to the customer. It threads from Issue 065's Lagos item, where the state conscripted platforms to withhold from creators: the same instinct to reach inside a private commercial relationship and reassign who owns the residual. MTN's MVNO objection is the one worth taking seriously, because asymmetric obligations reshape competitive structure far more durably than the headline rule does.

TechCabal Daily — “ICASA goes back on its ‘collusion’ accusation”

The structural read is that breakage is being regulated out of the South African mobile P&L, and the operators' public argument is more honest than it first appears. Expired bundles are close to pure margin: the revenue is recognised, the capacity was never consumed, and the customer has no recourse. Removing it does not simply transfer value to consumers — it removes the cross-subsidy that made the cheapest prepaid bundles viable at their advertised price, which is exactly MTN's stated case that prices could rise and cheaper no-rollover options disappear. That case is self-interested and may also be correct; both things are routinely true in regulated industries. The more consequential detail is the MVNO asymmetry. If the rollover obligation binds facilities-based operators but not virtual operators riding their networks, the regulator has handed MVNOs a structural cost advantage on a product feature, which over several years shapes the market more than any single price intervention. Meanwhile the retracted collusion charge is its own small governance lesson: a regulator inferred coordination from two firms responding identically to an identical rule, said so in Parliament, and could not evidence it.

Two firms behaving the same way in response to the same rule is not a cartel. It is what a rule is for. The regulator has withdrawn the accusation and kept the regulation, which is the correct outcome arrived at awkwardly.

The threads: to Issue 065's item 5 (Lagos moving to make Selar, Mainstack and Nestuge withhold 5% on creator payouts) — two regulators, two continents apart, both reaching into an existing commercial contract to reassign the residual; the African regulatory posture this year is consistently interventionist about who owns value that was already transacted. To Issue 063's item 3 (SARB withdrawing PASA recognition and taking the payment system) and Issue 060's item 1 (the Central Bank of Kenya forcing Safaricom to halve M-PESA merchant fees) — the same pattern in payments: once a private rail becomes infrastructure, the state prices it and reassigns its economics, and mobile data has now crossed that threshold in South Africa. To Issue 062's item 3 (South Africa's ML-driven border system) — a state that is simultaneously building its own capability and constraining private operators'. To today's item 4 (Airtel renting coverage from orbit) — note the regulatory arbitrage forming: satellite-direct services sit almost entirely outside frameworks like this one. To the Chronos read on regulated infrastructure — for any telecoms or payments exposure, the question is no longer whether the regulator will act but which line of the P&L it will find next, and breakage was always the softest target. The sceptic's ledger: the quantum here is undisclosed and no South African operator publishes breakage, so the earnings impact is genuinely unknown and could be immaterial; the rule requires rollover only once and only for eligible bundles, which leaves substantial room for product redesign that recovers most of the value; January 2027 is far enough away for pricing architecture to be rebuilt well before it bites; the court challenge is unresolved and parts of the regulations may not survive it; and consumer-welfare gains from rules like this are frequently competed away in headline price within a year. The forward marker: the first operator to restructure its prepaid bundle range ahead of January 2027 — the shape of that repricing will show exactly how much breakage was worth without anyone having to disclose it.

Global · Media · Ownership Design Analysis Structural design
Kenn Abuya's Next Wave essay examines three media businesses that have each re-cut who owns the upside, as AI and search absorb the distribution advantage publishers spent twenty years building. Puck News gives journalists equity in the company and ties bonuses to the subscribers their work generates — roughly 240,000 total subscribers, ~40,000 paid, annual subscriptions of $100–$250, having raised a $10m Series B at a $70m valuation. Defector Media, founded in 2020 by former Deadspin journalists who quit over a dispute with the site's private-equity owner, went the other way: a worker cooperative where the journalists own the company outright, running deliberately small at about 42,500 active subscribers in year four, ~$3.8m in subscription revenue plus ~$800,000 from podcasts and merchandise, with no outside investor awaiting an exit. Hunterbrook is the strangest: Hunterbrook Media reports alongside an affiliated fund, Hunterbrook Capital, which can trade on the newsroom's research — $100m raised in seed capital and a reported 23% return over the first nine months of 2025. Abuya's framing: the exposed publishers are those in the middle, carrying a large newsroom without journalism differentiated enough to command a direct reader relationship, and the underlying shift is that 500,000 casual readers are not necessarily worth more than 20,000 who will pay, attend, buy research and open every email.

When distribution stops being the moat, three newsrooms rewrite who owns the upside — the reporter, the workers, or a hedge fund attached to the reporting

TechCabal · Next Wave · Kenn Abuya · Mon 17 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is an essay about the media business. The one that matters is three fully-worked answers to the question every commoditising industry eventually faces — when the scarce thing you controlled stops being scarce, where does value relocate and who is positioned to capture it? The answers are structural rather than technological: give the producer equity, give the workers the company, or attach a balance sheet that can monetise the output directly. It threads straight from Issue 065's AI-made feature film, where the observation was that a creative industry's moat had been a cost structure. This is the same diagnosis with three prescriptions, and the ownership question is the through-line of today's entire edition.

TechCabal — “Next Wave: The next media business will be a strange one”

The structural read is that all three models are answers to one economic fact: when the input a business monetised becomes abundant, the surviving margin attaches to whatever remains scarce, and the ownership structure has to be redrawn so that the scarce thing is actually held by the business. Puck's answer is that the scarce asset is a named individual's access and judgement — and since that asset can walk out of the building, the only stable structure is one where the individual owns part of the enterprise. Defector's answer is that scarcity lies in a small, committed audience relationship, which requires no outside capital and is actively damaged by a growth mandate; 42,500 subscribers producing $3.8m is an excellent business and a catastrophic venture outcome, and the co-operative structure exists precisely to remove anyone who would insist on the latter. Hunterbrook's answer is the most radical and the most fragile: it accepts that readers will never pay what investigative reporting is worth and attaches a trading account to capture the value directly — solving journalism's oldest problem, that it creates enormous value while capturing almost none, at the cost of the independence that made the reporting worth trading on. Three different scarce assets, three different owners, one diagnosis.

Distribution was the thing publishers thought they owned and never did. The question every one of these three is answering is the one Spar is answering badly and Oui Capital is answering deliberately: who holds the asset that actually earns?

To Issue 062's Next Wave thread and Issue 065's editor's note — the dispatch's running observation that African businesses keep discovering the economics have moved one layer from where they built. To the Chronos read on AI and business-model durability — the transferable diagnostic is Abuya's "middle": any business large enough to carry real fixed cost but not differentiated enough to command a direct customer relationship is the one AI reprices first, and that description fits a great many services businesses better than it fits media. The sceptic's ledger: three cherry-picked cases, none at scale — Defector's entire revenue is under $5m and Puck's 40,000 paid subscribers is a rounding error against the newsrooms it is held up against; Puck raised at $70m in 2023 and no updated valuation or profitability is given, so the model is unproven where it counts; Hunterbrook's 23% over nine months of 2025 is a single short window with no disclosure of strategy, leverage or drawdown, and its conflict problem is not a detail to be waved past but the reason regulators and readers may eventually kill it; worker co-operatives have a long history of stability at small scale and difficulty raising capital for anything else; and the essay selects survivors, saying nothing about how many attempts at each model failed. The forward marker: whether any of the three crosses into a second cohort — a Puck-style equity model adopted by an established newsroom, or a Hunterbrook imitator surviving its first regulatory challenge; single specimens are not yet a species.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Rehearse the hard conversation before you have it — make Claude play the person across the table

Some conversations only happen once. Telling a founder the follow-on is not coming, taking a governance concern to a chairman, renegotiating terms with a co-investor who thinks the relationship entitles them to something — you get one attempt, in the room, with your own adrenaline running. The usual preparation is to write bullet points, which rehearses what you intend to say and nothing at all about what comes back. The better use of the model is to make it play the other person. Give it the counterparty properly: their position, what they want, what they are afraid of, how they typically argue, what leverage they think they hold. Then instruct it to stay in character and push back hard rather than fold at the first reasonable thing you say — models are agreeable by default, so you have to explicitly license them to be difficult. Run the conversation three or four times. What you are looking for is not a script; it is the two or three moments where you found yourself with nothing to say, because those are exactly the moments that will arrive in the real room. Then ask it to drop character and tell you where your position was weakest — it has just spent twenty minutes attacking it and knows.

A worked example

Instead of “Help me prepare for a difficult conversation with a founder”, try: “You are the founder of a portfolio company. You have missed plan for three consecutive quarters, you believe the misses are macro rather than execution, and you are expecting us to lead the bridge round. You are proud, well-liked in the market, and you think our reputation depends on not being seen to abandon you. Stay in character throughout and do not concede easily — push back, get frustrated, use the relationship. I am going to tell you we are not leading. Begin when I do. Afterwards, drop character and tell me the three weakest points in what I said and what you would have done to me if you were a tougher negotiator.”
