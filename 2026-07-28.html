Africa · Venture capital · Funding markets Analysis Chart of the day
Africa's startups raised roughly $1.44bn in H1 2026 across 146 disclosed deals — broadly flat on H1 2025 — but the headline hides a hollowing-out. The 30 most-funded companies absorbed 84% of all disclosed capital; the remaining 100-plus ventures shared the rest. Deal count fell 42% year on year (fewer, much larger cheques), mid-sized rounds of $10m–$99m took 66% of the money, and early-stage rounds below $500k collapsed to 19% of all deals — down from 52% in H1 2021. The number of ventures raising more than $100k fell to 190, the lowest since at least 2021, with the $100k–$1m band cratering from 179 companies in H2 2025 to 100 in H1 2026. Grants, which had cushioned the retreat, are running well behind pace (15 disclosed grants above $100k in Q1, ~$4m, versus 27 worth ~$20m a year earlier), and DFI share of Africa-focused fund commitments slid from ~45% (2022–24) to 27% in 2025. Egypt led country funding at $327m (one Spiro round), then Nigeria $254m, Kenya $126m, South Africa $83m — SA slipping to fourth after leading a year earlier. Debt hit a record $614m across 36 transactions against $818m of equity.

Africa's funding total holds — but the base is thinning, and 30 companies now take 84% of it

TechCabal · Follow the Money · Temitayo Jaiyeola · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the number that matters is not the $1.44bn but its distribution — venture capital is concentrating into a small set of mature, asset-heavy businesses with proven revenues, while the small first cheques that manufacture the next generation of companies are disappearing. As Launch Africa's Uwem Uwemakpan puts it, "if nobody writes that cheque in 2026, there's no Series A class in 2029" — the pipeline risk is invisible precisely because today's nine-figure rounds look healthy.

Totals hold; concentration deepens — the pipeline that produces tomorrow's breakouts is being defunded
TechCabal — “African venture capital is backing fewer founders than ever”

The structural read is that Africa's venture market is maturing and de-risking at the same time, and the two are hard to tell apart from the top line. Concentration into proven, asset-heavy businesses — Spiro's $327m mobility round alone was nearly a quarter of H1 funding — is exactly what a lender-friendly, revenue-first cohort looks like, which is why debt hit a record $614m across 36 deals: EV fleets, logistics and energy infrastructure have the tangible collateral and predictable cash flows that non-dilutive capital wants. That is healthy for the companies raising it. The problem is compositional: the same shift starves the sub-$500k experimentation layer where Flutterwave, Moniepoint, Wave and (the piece names it explicitly) TymeBank all began, before any of them raised a nine-figure round. The Big Deal's own phrase — "the base is thinning" — is the whole thesis, and the grant data is the quiet alarm: the philanthropic capital that had been substituting for absent seed cheques is itself running at a fifth of last year's Q1 pace.

"If nobody writes that cheque in 2026, there's no Series A class in 2029." — and the base is thinning in grants too.

The threads run straight back through this page's month. Mida (Issue 052) is raising a $1–3m seed into precisely the band that is collapsing — its pricing will be a live read on whether the missing-middle is a valuation problem or an appetite problem. The consolidation theme sits underneath today's Zedcrest–Leatherback deal (item 4): when equity retreats, scale is bought rather than grown, and M&A becomes the growth story. And the country data reframes the SARB and tariff threads — South Africa slipping to fourth on funding, failing to clear $100m after leading a year earlier, is the capital-markets echo of the same risk aversion the rand and the 12.5% tariff have been pricing. The sceptic's ledger: all figures are disclosed-deal trackers, so undisclosed rounds (common in debt and in DFI facilities) understate the true base; "flat year on year" flatters a market propped by three June mega-rounds (Spiro, Flutterwave ~$100m, MNT-Halan) without which H1 would read clearly down; and concentration is not automatically bad — a smaller number of fundable companies may simply reflect a decade of over-seeding correcting. The forward marker: whether Launch Africa's contrarian 15-deal early-stage push in 2026 is joined by others, or stays a lonely bet against the tide.

West Africa · Payments · Cross-border M&A Briefing Consolidation thread
Zedcrest Group — a Nigerian financial-services group spanning investment banking, asset management and fintech — has acquired UK-founded cross-border payments startup Leatherback for an undisclosed sum. Leatherback keeps its brand, management and independence as a Zedcrest subsidiary. The deal formalises a relationship that began in 2021, when Zedcrest backed Leatherback's $10m pre-seed, and follows Zedcrest's 2024 purchase of RMB Nigeria Stockbrokers — a deliberate roll-up into an integrated group spanning banking, asset management, securities, financing and fintech. Zedcrest says the acquisition gives Leatherback the balance sheet to chase new-market licences, banking partnerships and senior compliance/risk hires; Leatherback brings its own core banking system and payments ledger. Leatherback's backstory is turbulent: a 2023 Nigerian investigation into funds that passed through one of its accounts ended with founder Ibrahim Toyeeb Ibitade cleared and departing in October 2024; by August 2025 ex-Cellulant executive Ochebhoya Ekpete had taken over, pivoting the company from retail remittances to enterprise, infrastructure-led cross-border payments. Africa's cross-border payment market is projected at $1trn by 2035.

Zedcrest buys Leatherback — building the integrated stack Stripe was bidding into last week

TechCabal · Exclusive · Opeyemi Kareem · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the same "own the whole flow" instinct as Friday's Stripe–OpenRouter item, run at African scale — Zedcrest is assembling banking, brokerage, asset management and now cross-border rails into one group so that every transaction a client makes passes through infrastructure it owns. And the timing is not accidental: when equity funding concentrates and retreats (item 1), consolidation becomes the growth story, and a well-capitalised group buys the rails a cash-starved startup can no longer scale alone.

TechCabal — “Zedcrest Group acquires cross-border payments startup Leatherback”

The strategic logic worth extracting is the "integrated financial services group" being built in the open. Zedcrest's sequence — a fintech arm, then a stockbroker (RMB Nigeria, 2024), now a cross-border payments rail — is the African echo of the tollbooth thesis this page keeps returning to: whoever stands where the flow must pass earns on everything that crosses. Leatherback's own repositioning is the more interesting half. Its 2025 pivot from retail remittances (competing for individual senders) to enterprise infrastructure ("the financial rails powering businesses") is the same move Stripe made and the same move the payments-rail essay below (item 6) says is where the durable value sits — not the consumer-facing app, but the plumbing other companies rent. Buying that plumbing while it is still private, from a founder it has known since pre-seed, is Zedcrest bidding on infrastructure with information no outside acquirer has — the insider-bid dynamic Taylor flagged in the Stripe deal, transposed to Lagos.

When equity retreats, scale is bought, not grown — and the rail is worth more than the remittance app that runs on it.

The threads: to item 1 directly — this is what consolidation looks like on the ground when the funding base thins, and it is why H1's M&A count is rising even as deal count falls. To Friday's Stripe–Revolut item — the same "integrated stack beats point solution" logic, now visible on both sides of the equator within a week. And to the AfCFTA-corridor thread (Issues 048–052): every increment of US trade protection raises the relative return on intra-African payment infrastructure, and a $1trn-by-2035 cross-border market is the prize Zedcrest is buying exposure to. The sceptic's notes are substantial: the price is undisclosed, which usually means it was not flattering to disclose; Leatherback's 2023 compliance episode — even with the founder cleared — is exactly the kind of history that makes a cross-border payments licence harder to win, and Zedcrest is explicitly buying to fund the compliance and risk hires that gap implies; and "independent subsidiary keeping its own management" is the structure that most often precedes either a clean integration or a quiet write-down, rarely anything in between. The forward marker: whether Leatherback's promised Canada and Kenya hubs actually open under Zedcrest's balance sheet, or whether the group absorbs the rail and lets the brand fade.

Kenya · Digital lending · Regulation Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
In two judgments delivered on 17 July, Nairobi Resident Magistrate Gladys Kiama struck out debt-recovery claims brought by Tri-State Capital and Mombo iCapital, ruling that digital lenders operating without a Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) licence lack the legal capacity to enforce their loan agreements. Rather than ask whether the borrowers had defaulted, the court asked first whether the lenders had authority to advance credit at all — and, citing Section 3 of the Banking Act, held that "conducting lending business without such licencing amounts to an illegality." One lender had chased KES 500,000 ($3,858) on a KES 213,500 vehicle loan; the other KES 162,297 on a KES 65,000 loan that had ballooned on interest and weekly default charges. Both suits were dismissed for lack of locus standi. The rulings reinforce the CBK's supervisory push that began with Parliament's 2021 amendment (a response to abusive collection, excessive rates and data misuse); since licensing started in 2022, the CBK has approved just 252 digital credit providers from more than 800 applications — leaving hundreds either awaiting approval or operating outside the regulated market.

A Nairobi bench rewrites the economics of lending: no CBK licence, no recovery through the courts

TechCabal · Adonijah Ndege · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is Friday's Mida story answered from the bench — you can industrialise collections with an AI telecollector all you like, but if you are one of the hundreds of unlicensed lenders, the courthouse door is now shut, and the loan is legally uncollectable. The precedent quietly repriced the risk of lending-before-licensing for an entire category: regulatory approval just moved from a compliance checkbox to a precondition for having any enforceable asset at all.

TechCabal — “Unlicenced Kenya's digital lenders cannot recover loans after court ruling”

The legal architecture is the interesting part, because the court did not create a new rule — it applied an old one (the Banking Act) to a new category and let the consequence fall where it may. By treating an unlicensed loan as an illegality that denies the lender standing, the magistrate converted the CBK's licensing regime from a supervisory gate into a commercial life-or-death line: 252 licensed providers keep a fully enforceable loan book; the several hundred unlicensed or pending ones now hold portfolios that a defaulting borrower can, in effect, walk away from with the court's blessing. That is a far sharper enforcement mechanism than fines or shutdown orders, because it works through the lenders' own self-interest — and it will be watched most closely by exactly the firms whose licence applications are still pending, who must now weigh whether to keep lending into an unenforceable book or freeze until approved.

Regulatory approval just moved from a compliance checkbox to the precondition for owning an enforceable asset.

The threads run dense. To Mida (Issue 052): Friday's page called collections the missing recovery layer under a month of lending booms — today's ruling adds that the layer only exists legally where the licence does, which reframes Mida's Kenya-expansion ambition around a licensing question, not just an operational one. To Kenya's VASP gazettement (Issue 052): Friday said "gazettement is not capacity, and the enforcement test would come" — it came, from an adjacent bench, showing that Kenya's gate can bite through the courts even where the regulator's own machinery is still hiring. To the CBK's broader sequence this page has tracked — 252 licensed lenders, lender-of-last-resort rules written before the crisis (Issue 047), affordability checks before approval (April 2026) — a regulator systematically building capacity ahead of demand, now backstopped by a judiciary willing to enforce the perimeter. The sceptic's notes: these are two magistrate's-court rulings, not an appellate precedent, and a higher court could narrow or overturn them; the immediate effect may be perverse — unlicensed lenders shifting from courts to the abusive informal collection the 2021 law was meant to stop, precisely because legal recovery is now closed to them; and the ruling does nothing for borrowers already harassed, only for those who default and get sued. The forward marker: whether the CBK accelerates its licensing backlog in response, or whether the unenforceable-book problem simply drives consolidation into the 252 who cleared the gate.

Kenya · Mobile money · Leadership Briefing Hyper-local
Anne Kinuthia-Otieno has stepped down as managing director of Airtel Money Kenya after nearly five years, leaving weeks before parent Airtel Africa floats the mobile-money unit in London (venue confirmed 23 July; targeted for H2 2026). She announced her exit on LinkedIn on Saturday without naming her next role. During her tenure, Airtel Money narrowed the gap with M-PESA from a low base: its share of Kenyan mobile-money subscriptions rose from 3.1% in September 2021 to 10.9% by March 2026, while M-PESA's fell from 96.8% to 89.1%; Kenya now has 53.4 million active mobile-money subscriptions at 100.1% penetration. Her departure extends a striking wave of fintech-leadership churn at Kenya's two largest providers: Airtel Kenya's telecoms MD changed on 1 July (Djibril Tobe replacing Ashish Malhotra); Safaricom's chief financial services officer Esther Waititu is due to leave on 31 July (Boniface Mungania interim); and former M-PESA Africa MD Sitoyo Lopokoiyit left earlier in 2026 for Absa. Airtel Money had 56.5m customers across Africa at end-June (up 23.3% y/y) on annualised transaction value above $245bn.

Airtel Money's Kenyan chief walks — weeks before the London float, and amid a fintech-leadership exodus

TechCabal · Kenn Abuya · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: a managing director leaving weeks before her unit's IPO is the kind of people-signal that matters more than any single quarter — and it is the fourth senior fintech exit at Kenya's top two operators in a fortnight, turning Friday's "control is exercised through people" line into a pattern. The backdrop is a genuine competitive shift: Airtel Money has tripled its subscription share to 10.9% while M-PESA's near-monopoly has slipped below 90% for the first time — the London listing is a bet on that trajectory continuing without the executive who drove it.

Five years of share-taking, then the exit — the London float bets the climb outlasts the climber
TechCabal — “Airtel Money Kenya chief exits months before planned London listing”

The people-signal is the story, and it compounds. Four senior financial-services departures in a fortnight at Kenya's two dominant operators — Airtel Kenya's telecoms MD, Airtel Money's MD, Safaricom's chief financial services officer, and (earlier in 2026) M-PESA Africa's MD to Absa — is either coincidence or a market telling you something about where mobile-money talent thinks the value is migrating. Note the direction of two of those moves: senior mobile-money operators leaving for banks (Lopokoiyit to Absa; Kinuthia-Otieno herself came from Absa and Barclays). The banking sector, having watched M-PESA eat its lunch for a decade, is now hiring the people who built the thing that ate it — the clearest possible signal that the bank/mobile-money boundary is dissolving from the talent side, exactly as the payments-rail essay (item 6) argues it is dissolving from the infrastructure side.

Control is exercised through people — and the people who built mobile money are now walking into the banks it disrupted.

The threads: to Friday's page, which closed on the line that "control is exercised through people" (GTBank recalling its Kenyan MD, Safaricom shedding fintech chiefs, the PIC's collapse) — today adds Airtel Money and turns an observation into a fortnight-long pattern. To the London-listing angle: Airtel Africa is floating a business whose Kenyan growth engine just lost its driver on the eve of the IPO, which is precisely the kind of key-person risk a prospectus has to disclose and an underwriter has to price. And to the competitive read: M-PESA slipping below 90% share is genuinely notable — the duopoly is loosening, and the listing is a bet that a well-capitalised, publicly-traded Airtel Money keeps compounding that gain. The sceptic's ledger: an executive leaving before an IPO is not necessarily a bad signal — five years is a full tenure, and pre-float is a natural, well-compensated exit point; share gains "from a low base" flatter the percentages (10.9% of subscriptions is still a distant second, and subscription share is not transaction-value share, where M-PESA's dominance is far heavier); and a wave of exits at two companies in one small market can reflect a single poaching bank rather than a sector in flux. The forward marker: who Airtel names as replacement, and whether the London prospectus quantifies the Kenyan-leadership gap or files it under boilerplate risk.

South Africa · Institutions · Asset management Briefing Longitudinal thread
The PIC saga reached its procedural climax. Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana confirmed on Friday he will appoint a new board for the Public Investment Corporation after a series of resignations — eight board members have now gone, including Deputy Finance Minister David Masondo, who chaired the board and left on Thursday. CEO Patrick Dlamini remains suspended pending a whistleblower-triggered investigation, while the FSCA runs a separate governance probe. Per News24, Godongwana called a general meeting on Monday 27 July to vote on removing whatever remained of the board; candidates for the new board are reportedly being drawn from the presidency, National Treasury and the Department of Trade, Industry & Competition. The PIC manages more than R3-trillion, overwhelmingly for the Government Employees Pension Fund, and is the single largest investor on the JSE. Reuters and Engineering News note the institution has grappled with governance failures for over a decade — many of the current issues echo the 2020 Mpati judicial inquiry, which flagged the same weaknesses in oversight, accountability and investment decision-making.

Godongwana will appoint a new PIC board — the sequel to Monday's lead, and the same problem it named

Engineering News / Reuters · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the resolution confirms the diagnosis rather than dissolving it — a new board drawn from the presidency, Treasury and the DTIC is precisely the deployment-by-negotiation model that Cosatu (in Monday's page) said denied the PIC the chance to "scan the market for the skills required." And the echo of the 2020 Mpati inquiry is the uncomfortable part: the same oversight and investment-judgement weaknesses were formally identified six years ago, which makes this less a crisis than a relapse.

Engineering News (Reuters) — “Godongwana to appoint new board at PIC”

The continuity worth naming is that the fix and the flaw share a mechanism. Monday's whistleblower argument was that the PIC's investment committee lacked "fit-for-purpose capability" because its seats were filled by political settlement rather than transaction craft — and the remedy now on the table is a board sourced from the presidency, Treasury and the DTIC, which is the same settlement machinery producing the next cohort. That does not make it wrong — a caretaker board of senior officials is the fastest way to restore a quorum and legal capacity to an institution that has neither — but it does mean the structural question the whistleblower raised is being answered, for now, in the negative. The Mpati echo is the tell: when a 2020 judicial inquiry has already mapped the identical weaknesses, a 2026 board reshuffle that does not change how seats are filled is treating a governance-design problem with a personnel patch.

The same weaknesses the 2020 Mpati inquiry named, resurfacing in 2026 — a relapse, treated with a personnel patch.

Why it matters beyond Pretoria, restated from Monday because the resolution sharpens it: the PIC is the anchor allocator of the entire South African capital market and the LP every African private-markets GP eventually calls. A caretaker board stabilises legal capacity but does not, by itself, restart considered capital deployment — secondaries, co-investments and raises that assumed PIC participation stay in limbo until a permanent, credible investment committee exists. The signals to watch are unchanged but now datable: whether the acting-executive layer (CEO, COO, CIO, CRO all currently acting) is filled with market hires or more deployment; who takes the chair; and whether the FSCA report survives contact with politics. The thread ties to today's people-signal item (item 4) and Friday's close: from Airtel Money's Kenyan MD to the PIC's entire board, this fortnight's running theme is that institutions are only as sound as the people layer at the top — and when that layer fails, capability, not capital, is the binding constraint. The sceptic's note: markets have priced PIC dysfunction for a decade and the JSE has not seized up, so the systemic-risk framing can be overdone; the more realistic damage is slow — a quarter or two of deferred private-market decisions — not a cliff.

Africa · Payments · Infrastructure design Analysis World's-best-thinkers
Writing from an AfricaNenda media training in Abidjan, TechCabal's Kenn Abuya turns Friday's PI-SPI chart into a continental thesis: Africa now has 36 domestic and regional instant-payment systems, but few pass AfricaNenda's SIIPS inclusion tests. His argument is that speed has been solved and is now the wrong metric — "once payments become fast enough, shaving another second off settlement matters much less than who can use the system, how much they pay and which institutions are allowed to connect." A switch that clears in two seconds but forces fintechs and mobile-money operators to connect through a sponsor bank is instant but not inclusive: the bank becomes a gatekeeper adding cost and a commercial relationship. Nigeria's NIBSS Instant Payment reaches AfricaNenda's "Mature" tier because a broad market (banks, microfinance, mobile money) can participate directly and it handles frequent small transfers; Kenya's PesaLink, built by and for banks, works well yet misses the mobile-wallet and SACCO activity where most Kenyans actually transact. The unresolved questions are access, economics and control — and cross-border, where different currencies, FX rules and capital controls mean better technology alone cannot close the gap.

Africa's payment rails have a participation problem — speed is solved; access, price and control are not

TechCabal · The Next Wave · Kenn Abuya · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: this is the structural-design essay Friday's BCEAO chart was pointing at — interoperability mandated top-down delivers reach, but inclusion depends on who is allowed to connect directly, what they pay, and who carries settlement risk, none of which faster settlement fixes. The sharpest reframe: a bank-owned switch does not have to mean bank-only participation, but opening access without supervision "is not necessarily inclusive and can simply redistribute risk" — the inclusion argument cuts both ways.

TechCabal — The Next Wave: “Africa's payment rails have a participation problem”

The essay's real contribution is a metric correction, and it is the kind of structural-design insight this page exists to surface. The industry's favourite number — settlement speed — has become a vanity metric, because past a low threshold nobody's financial life improves from shaving a second off a transfer. The variables that actually determine whether a rail broadens the economy are participation (who can connect, and directly or through a gatekeeping sponsor bank), economics (whether flat fees make small transfers uneconomic for the people who most need them), and channel (whether the system reaches USSD, agents and feature phones, or only smartphone banking apps). Abuya's Nigeria-versus-Kenya contrast makes it concrete: NIBSS is "Mature" not because it is fast but because its market is broad and its small-transfer economics work; PesaLink is technically excellent yet bank-centric in a country where the mobile wallet is the primary account — so a system can score well on interoperability while missing most of the actual economy. The deepest point is that payment systems inherit the economies and regulations around them, which is why copying NIBSS's architecture into a smaller market does not reproduce its inclusion.

Speed is solved. The harder task is deciding who gets access to the rails, on what terms, and who controls the points where they meet.

The threads run straight back to this page's month-long settlement-infrastructure arc. Friday's BCEAO PI-SPI item — 30 million connected, one million transactions — was exactly the "reach without participation" gap Abuya generalises: the rail exists, but the closed loops (Wave, Orange Money, M-PESA) that already work for users have no reason to route across it. His PesaLink analysis is the mirror of the PI-SPI diagnosis: interoperability that connects banks while missing wallets and SACCOs is a bridge between the already-served. And the cross-border coda ties to the Zedcrest deal (item 2) and the AfCFTA-corridor thread (Issues 048–052): PAPSS and the East African Payment System connect the switches, but currencies, FX rules and capital controls remain, so the durable value accrues to whoever owns the plumbing that makes crossing cheap — the rail, not the app. The forward marker Abuya sets up is the right one for Chronos to watch: whether African regulators pursue "one national rail" (control, single point of failure) or "make existing networks talk cheaply" (interoperability without a monopoly) — and whether direct-access regimes get the supervision to stop open access from simply redistributing risk to the least-capitalised participant. The sceptic's note: AfricaNenda is an inclusion advocate, so its framework privileges participation over the commercial sustainability that keeps a rail alive — and a switch can pass every inclusion test while users still meet fraud, failed transactions and hopeless dispute resolution, which is the lived reality no ranking captures.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Draft-then-edit — stop perfecting the prompt, start correcting the output

A common failure mode is spending ten minutes engineering the perfect instruction, when the faster path is to ask for a deliberately rough first draft and then edit it in conversation. Editing is where you actually think. Tell Claude: "Give me a quick, imperfect first draft — don't polish, I'll steer." Then react to what comes back: "too formal," "cut the second paragraph," "this bit is wrong, here's why," "more like how I'd say it." Three or four rounds of correction get you somewhere no single upfront prompt would, because you are no longer guessing what you want in the abstract — you are responding to something concrete on the page. It also surfaces your own preferences faster: you often don't know a framing is wrong until you see it written down. The one discipline that makes it work: react specifically. "I don't like it" teaches nothing; "too much hedging, and it buried the number" teaches the model exactly how to redraft — and, over time, how you think.

A worked example

Drafting a note to an LP on a portfolio write-down: don't spend the effort front-loading every nuance into one prompt.
