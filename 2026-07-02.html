Africa · Capital Markets · Structural Chart
Launch Base Africa’s tabulation of every disclosed H1 2026 round strips the framing off the funding numbers. African tech raised at least $1.21bn across 151 deals — a deal count barely below the 159 of H1 2025, which reads like stabilisation until the dollars are examined. Disclosed volume fell 17% YoY (~$1.45bn→$1.21bn). Debt-labelled transactions rose to 36.7% of capital from 18.5%, with the number of debt deals nearly tripling (9→26). The median deal nearly halved, $4.65m→$2.65m, while the mean held near $12m — a gap kept wide by a single outlier. Spiro, the pan-African battery-swapping and e-motorcycle operator, raised $270m equity plus a $50m facility: $320m, more than a quarter of the entire disclosed pool. Strip Spiro out and the rest of the market raised less than 2025’s top three deals combined. Fintech (50 deals) and mobility (21) together made 47% of transactions; agritech fell 12→8; the Big Four markets’ share of deals eased 64%→53%, with Nigeria overtaking Egypt by count.

Africa’s H1 funding cleared $1.2bn — but the money that moved was debt against hard assets, and one company took a quarter of it

Launch Base Africa (Udoh Charles) · Analysis & Opinions · Wed 1 Jul 2026

The headline — “Africa crosses $1.2bn” — is the least interesting thing in the dataset. Underneath it are three facts that change how the year should be read. First, debt has displaced equity as the default instrument, which is a statement about what kind of business can raise: predictable, asset-backed cash flows can, unproven unit economics increasingly cannot. Second, the “boom” sectors — e-mobility above all — are a securitisation story, capital lent against motorcycles and batteries rather than conviction in a model. Third, the median founder is raising less than half of a year ago, a reality the mean conveniently hides. This is the AFC’s “capital-trapped” thesis from Wednesday, seen from the cap table: the capital that does move now demands collateral it can repossess.

Deal count barely moved; the composition did — more debt, smaller typical cheques, and a single outlier holding up the average
Launch Base Africa — “Seven Brutal Truths About African Tech Startup Funding in H1 2026”

This is the most useful single read on African venture we have surfaced this quarter, precisely because it refuses the press-release frame. A deal count that holds flat while dollar volume falls 17% is not stabilisation; it is a market doing more, smaller, safer deals. And the instrument mix tells you why: when debt goes from a fifth to over a third of capital in twelve months, the market is repricing risk in favour of anything with collateral a lender can seize.

“Remove Spiro from the H1 2026 total and the remainder of the market raised less than 2025’s top three deals combined.”

For Chronos the value is in three calibrations. First, it is a direct read on the AFC’s “capital-trapped” argument from Wednesday: capital is not absent, but it is now conditional on hard assets, which is exactly why development-finance debt against motorcycles and solar receivables dominates the top of the table while equity for unproven models thins out. Second, it is a discipline check on our own pattern-matching — the mean deal size held near $12m and would flatter any “the market is fine” narrative; the median, at $2.65m, tells the truth about what a typical founder actually secured. Any internal read on African venture that quotes the average without the median is quietly wrong. Third, the sector concentration — fintech and mobility at 47% of deals, agritech shrinking — maps neatly onto financeability: the categories with payment receivables or physical hard assets attract the debt, and the categories without it are starved. The thread from Wednesday, made concrete: the capital that moves in Africa now moves toward things it can repossess. The question for any growth-equity thesis on the continent is whether the business is on the collateral side of that line — and if not, what substitutes for collateral in the eyes of the only investors still writing cheques.

Africa · China · Monetary Sovereignty Longitudinal
Ecofin Agency reads two Chinese moves as one strategy. First, China’s largest banks are shutting retail “paper” gold trading — ICBC, the world’s biggest bank, ends individual precious-metals trading on 24 July 2026, with Bank of China and Postal Savings following. Second, Beijing is building physical-pricing infrastructure: a gold futures clearing system anchored in Hong Kong and backed by Shanghai vaults launches in July, and the Shanghai Gold Exchange requires delivery of real bars — unlike New York, where under 1% of contracts settle physically. Removing leveraged paper bets pushes price discovery toward physical demand, and Africa holds close to 40% of the world’s gold still underground (UNEP). The PBoC has bought gold for 18 straight months through April 2026 (holdings above 2,300 tons); central banks bought 244 tons in Q1 alone; gold overtook US Treasuries as the largest reserve asset by value late in 2025 (ECB: ~27% of reserves vs 22%), the first time since 1996. And on 26 June, Standard Bank and ICBC launched the first continent-wide yuan clearing hub — the rail beneath Wednesday’s story.

China is rebuilding how gold is priced — and Africa, sitting on 40% of the metal underground, has to decide whether it captures the value or ships the ore raw again

Ecofin Agency (Idriss Linge) · Finance · Tue 30 Jun 2026

The continent’s leverage has rarely been so clearly stated: whoever can deliver physical metal will decide the next gold market, and no region has more of it in the ground. Yet leverage in the ground has almost never become value captured on the surface — most African gold still leaves unrefined, mostly for the UK, where the profitable stages of refining, trading and pricing sit offshore. That is the exact gap Beijing’s physical-pricing push suddenly makes worth billions. African central banks are already responding — Ghana’s Gold Reserve Act, Tanzania’s 27.5 tons since 2023, Zimbabwe backing its ZiG with gold — while Guinea and Namibia restrict raw-ore exports to force refining onshore. The through-line from Wednesday’s yuan hub: China is building the rails and the pricing, straight through African soil.

Ecofin Agency — “China Escalates the Global Gold Contest and Africa’s Untapped Ore Becomes the Prize”

Wednesday we said the yuan clearing hub was the plumbing beneath a Kenyan debt swap. Today Ecofin shows what that plumbing is being built to carry. The two Chinese moves — killing paper gold at home, standing up a physical-delivery clearing system in Hong Kong and Shanghai — are a single act: shift price discovery from leveraged bets toward real metal, in a market China has been accumulating for eighteen straight months. A gold price set on physical demand lifts the value of exactly the asset Africa holds in abundance and has never learned to keep.

“China is building a gold and yuan architecture that runs straight through African soil, and the countries that own the metal will decide whether this moment delivers real financial weight or another century of shipping wealth abroad in raw form.”

For Chronos this is a macro thread to hold rather than a trade, and it matters on three levels. First, it sharpens the reserve-asset story that has been building across editions — gold passing Treasuries by value late in 2025 is not a curiosity; it is the backdrop against which African central banks buying domestic gold in local currency (Ghana, Tanzania, Zimbabwe) starts to look less like nationalism and more like balance-sheet strategy, albeit one that ran Ghana ~$629m of losses in 2022–24 when the price swung. Second, it is the same “capture the value” question that runs through the whole edition, at the level of a continent: owning the ore is worthless if refining and pricing happen in London or Shanghai, which is precisely why Guinea and Namibia are banning raw-ore exports. Third, it threads directly into Wednesday — the yuan hub and the gold-pricing system are two halves of one design, and the read-across for our portfolio is that African monetary and commodity infrastructure is increasingly being architected in Beijing, with terms attached. The forward question: as physical-gold pricing gains weight, which African refining, custody or settlement assets move from strategic afterthought to genuinely investable — and who is already quietly buying them.

South Africa · Manufacturing · Trade Wouldn't have seen otherwise
The Absa Purchasing Managers’ Index for June 2026 fell from 50.8 to 47.3 — back below the 50 line that separates expansion from contraction. The counterintuitive driver: the purchasing price index dropped sharply, down 13.5 points to 71.3, after Brent crude fell on a US–Iran memorandum of understanding — and instead of spurring buying, cheaper inputs triggered a slump in demand, as clients postponed purchases betting prices will fall further. Economists flag a looming risk: if buyers all move to restock at once against still-slow supply chains, acute raw-material shortages follow. Layered on top is a structural threat — Chinese overcapacity. PSG’s Gavin Rabbolini cites a “disruptive combination of hyper-speed and structural cost advantage”; Actom Static Power’s Richard van Moltke says local manufacturers “cannot compete with ultra-low-cost PV modules from China, nor with the growing influx of cut-price batteries.” A bright spot: African air-cargo volumes grew 13.3% in May (IATA), the fastest of any region.

SA manufacturing falls back into contraction — cheaper inputs, collapsing demand, and Chinese overcapacity undercutting the factory floor

Daily Maverick / Business Maverick (Lindsey Schutters) · Wed 1 Jul 2026

A PMI that falls because input prices fell is a tell about confidence, not costs: buyers are postponing, not purchasing, because they expect prices lower still — a wait-and-see freeze that leaves factories idling and skills underused. Beneath the cycle sits a structural problem the piece names squarely: Chinese firms, forced lean by domestic overcapacity, are exporting cut-price PV modules and batteries into exactly the renewable-energy supply chain SA hoped to build, undercutting local producers before they can reach scale. The suggested escape — stop competing on commoditised components, capture the 55–60% local value-add through durable, contextually engineered systems for the African market — is the same “capture the value” question the gold and cobalt stories ask, brought home to the factory floor.

Daily Maverick — “SA manufacturing plummets as buyers wait out prices and China undercuts local trade”

Wednesday, Labia found South Africa locked out of the AI economy setting global asset prices. Today the lock-out is one rung lower and far more tangible: the factory floor, where a lean Chinese industrial machine is undercutting local manufacturers in the very sectors — solar modules, batteries — that the energy transition was supposed to build at home. The PMI shock is cyclical and probably passes; the China dynamic is structural and does not.

“Local manufacturers cannot compete with ultra-low-cost PV modules from China, nor with the growing influx of cut-price batteries. The result is a volatile environment in which domestic producers are undercut before they can scale.”

For Chronos the read is less about the June print and more about where defensible margin can still sit in a South African industrial business. The article’s own answer is the useful one: not in commoditised components, where China wins on cost every time, but in the 55–60% of value that comes from durable, contextually engineered systems designed for African operating conditions — the reliability, servicing and integration that imported kit cannot easily replicate. That is a moat thesis, and it is the same one that runs through the whole edition: owning the raw thing (the ore, the module, the cell) captures little; owning the stage where context and reliability are priced captures the margin. It also quietly reinforces the case for African-market-facing industrials over import-substitution plays that try to beat China head-on. And the 13.3% jump in African air cargo is worth filing — it says intra-continental demand is real and growing, which is where a contextually-engineered SA manufacturer would sell. The thread to carry: in every layer of this edition, the winner is whoever prices the context, not whoever holds the commodity.

Global · Fintech · AI Deployment AI for the portfolio
In this week’s Fintech Brainfood, Simon Taylor — read by 47,000+ in banks, fintechs and VCs — leads with Anthropic launching Claude Tag, an AI agent that lives inside a company’s Slack. The framing that matters: Anthropic says 65% of the code its own product team ships is now written by an AI that lives in their Slack, and it has packaged exactly that as a product — you add @Claude to a channel, scope which tools and data it can reach, and tag it like a coworker; it reads the thread, keeps context across the channel, breaks a task into steps and posts the work back where the team can see it. Taylor reads it through Karpathy’s three UI patterns for LLMs: first a website you visit, then an app you download, now a standing teammate wired into your org’s tools and memory. He pairs it with the week’s prediction-markets drama (Meta’s “Arena”, the CFTC suing states, Polymarket’s ad scandal) and the sharper point buried under it: a market that priced a 2026 Fed hike at 6% in January, 35% on 22 June and 57% by 24 June is, in his words, close to a perfect hedging instrument.

Anthropic ships “Claude Tag” — the AI stops being a website you visit and becomes a standing teammate inside Slack, and the real question is who owns it

Fintech Brainfood (Simon Taylor) · Sun 28 Jun 2026

The Claude Tag launch is the more portfolio-relevant of Taylor’s threads, and his framing is the useful part: this is the third redesign of how we use these models — from website, to app, to a coworker you tag in the channel where work already happens. Because the pattern is now a standing agent wired into tools and memory, the decision shifts from “should we put an agent in chat” to who owns the agent — rent it from a lab (hosted, metered per token, your context pooling inside their walls) or self-host an open version (Paradigm’s MIT-licensed Centaur is already the same tag-it-in-Slack teammate, months behind on polish, well ahead on control). The prediction-markets thread carries a quieter gift: a liquid market on rate decisions is a genuinely novel, real-time hedging and sentiment instrument.

Fintech Brainfood — “Prediction Markets: A Messy Path to Mainstream”

Wednesday’s Airwallex item framed the AI finance department as a product you buy. Claude Tag is the other route to the same destination: the assistant appears inside Slack — the tool the company already lives in — and adoption happens without anyone deciding to “roll out AI.” That Anthropic writes 65% of its own product code this way is the tell: the tag-it-in-the-channel pattern is how the most advanced users already work, and a lab packaging it for the enterprise is the logical next step.

“The question moves past ‘should we put an agent in chat’ to who owns the agent. Rent it from a vendor, and your org’s context pools inside their walls. Own it, and your secrets and your roadmap stay on your side of the firewall.”

For Chronos there are three takeaways. First, practical and immediate: when we press portfolio companies on AI, the instinct is to ask which tools they have licensed. The better question, on this evidence, is where the assistant now sits — an agent inside Slack, the CRM or the repo drives adoption a standalone app never will, and adoption is the only thing that turns an AI budget into a productivity number. Second, Taylor’s “own vs rent the agent” question is a real governance fork for any business where the context flowing through the agent is the moat: renting from a lab is faster and cleaner but pools your proprietary context inside someone else’s walls, while self-hosting an open teammate (Centaur, MIT-licensed) keeps roadmap and secrets on your side of the firewall at the cost of polish. For a portfolio company whose data is its edge, that trade deserves a deliberate answer, not a default. Third, the prediction-markets thread is worth filing not as a punt but as an instrument: a market that repriced a Fed hike from 6% to 57% in six months is a low-cost, real-time read on consensus probabilities for the events we underwrite — rate moves, deal outcomes, elections — and a disciplined investor treats a liquid one as an occasionally sharper sentiment gauge. The thread to carry forward, and the one that rhymes with the whole edition: value in enterprise AI is accruing to whoever owns the surface where work already happens — own the layer that prices the context, whether that layer is a Slack channel or a gold vault.

Namibia · Critical Minerals · China Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Australia’s Celsius Resources has agreed to sell its Opuwo copper-cobalt project in Namibia to Chinalco Mining Corporation — a subsidiary of state-owned Aluminum Corporation of China — for $15m, subject to shareholder and regulatory approval (Namibia’s Competition Commission, the Bank of Namibia and the Ministry of Trade, plus licence renewal and environmental clearance). Celsius calls Opuwo “the largest cobalt deposit outside the DRC” (a claim it does not substantiate), with estimated resources of 259,000 tonnes of contained cobalt, 970,000 tonnes of copper and zinc. The binding agreement was signed 30 June; close is targeted by 29 December. Chinalco will fund $1m of non-refundable exploration during the closing period. The context: the DRC dominates cobalt mining and China dominates refining, but a new Congolese export-quota regime has squeezed Chinese refiners — and China imported over 50% of the DRC’s 484,202 tonnes of cobalt exports in 2023.

A Chinese state miner buys the “largest cobalt deposit outside the Congo” in Namibia — for $15m

Ecofin Agency (Aurel Sèdjro Houenou) · Industry · Wed 1 Jul 2026

The number is the story. $15m — the price of a good Cape Town house — hands a Chinese state giant control of a greenfield resource its seller describes as the largest of its kind outside the DRC, at the exact moment Congolese export quotas are squeezing China’s refining machine. Whether or not Celsius’s “largest outside the DRC” claim survives scrutiny, the direction is unmistakable: as one African jurisdiction tightens the tap on a critical mineral, China moves to secure the next, less-developed one — and does so early, cheaply, and at the exploration stage, before the value is proven or the host state has learned to price it.

Ecofin Agency — “Chinalco Targets Major Namibia Cobalt Deposit in $15 Million Deal”

Read alone, a $15m exploration deal in Namibia is a footnote. Read against the gold story two items up, it is the same move one metal down: China securing physical control of an African resource at the point of maximum leverage — here, just as the DRC’s export quotas tighten supply to the refiners China owns. Buying at exploration stage is the tell: the value is unproven, the price is trivial, and the option on future supply is enormous.

“The Namibia transaction highlights China’s efforts to establish positions in alternative and less-developed cobalt jurisdictions… as global competition for critical minerals intensifies.”

For Chronos the interest is thematic, not a live deal, and it lands on the edition’s central question: who captures the value of what Africa holds in the ground. A $15m entry price for “the largest cobalt deposit outside the DRC” — if the claim holds — is a stark illustration of how cheaply strategic African resource optionality still trades when it is early-stage and the host state lacks the tools to price it. Three things to hold: first, the buyer is a state champion executing an industrial-policy mandate, not a return-seeking financial investor, which means it will pay for security of supply in ways a private buyer cannot — a recurring feature of the critical-minerals landscape our portfolio touches. Second, the regulatory gauntlet (Competition Commission, central bank, trade ministry, licence renewal) is where an African state can still extract terms, and it is worth watching whether Namibia does. Third, the read-across to the whole edition: from gold to cobalt to the aluminium smelter below, the pattern is foreign capital acquiring the physical base while the value chain’s profitable stages sit elsewhere. The forward question for us: where, in African critical minerals, does value actually accrue to a financial investor rather than a strategic — and is it ever at the deposit, or only further up the chain?

South Africa · M&A · Capital Longitudinal
South32 — the BHP spin-off — will sell a global set of aluminium assets, including South Africa’s Hillside smelter in Richards Bay, to US giant Alcoa in a cash-and-shares deal valued at up to $5.6bn (announced 30 June; close expected H1 2027). Notably, the sale excludes the Mozal smelter in Mozambique (32.4% held by SA’s IDC), which South32 shut in March after failing to agree a power supply deal — while Hillside, described as a “high-quality, low-cost” asset, is a centrepiece. Hillside was launched in 1996 as the largest private investment in SA at the time (Mandela opened it); it is Eskom’s biggest private customer, taking ~5% of demand — more power than Malawi — on a ~50% discount that Open Secrets estimates could total R92bn over the deal’s life. The current pricing arrangement expires in 2031; Daily Maverick understands Alcoa is confident of a successor deal with Eskom, with political backing (Ramaphosa spoke at the smelter’s 30th-birthday gala in May).

A US giant pays up to $5.6bn for South32’s aluminium — and by keeping Hillside, bets that South Africa can keep the lights on past 2031

Daily Maverick / Business Maverick (Ed Stoddard) · Wed 1 Jul 2026

The counterpoint the edition needs. Two items up, the SA factory floor is contracting; here, foreign capital is paying billions for a South African industrial asset and, crucially, choosing to keep the one tied to Eskom while South32 walks away from the Mozambican smelter it could not power. Alcoa taking Hillside — a plant that consumes more electricity than Malawi on a ~50% discount — is a wager that a successor power deal past 2031 gets done, and that SA’s grid is reliable enough to smelt on. It is investor confidence, but of a specific, conditional kind: contingent on cheap, guaranteed power, which is exactly the variable South Africa still cannot promise most other buyers.

Daily Maverick — “South32’s $5.6bn aluminium sale to Alcoa includes Hillside, signalling investor confidence in SA”

This is the edition’s optimistic note, but it earns its optimism carefully. Alcoa did not have to keep Hillside — South32 excluded Mozal precisely because it could not secure power for it — and a global aluminium major choosing to inherit Eskom’s biggest private customer is a real vote of confidence in SA’s grid and labour force. But the vote is conditional on a power price no ordinary buyer gets, which is the tension worth naming.

“The only way to currently attract foreign investors to power-intensive operations in South Africa is to lower the costs and boost reliability.”

For Chronos there are three things to hold. First, on the reliability question the article makes an important distinction: Eskom has made genuine strides on availability (load-shedding down sharply) but almost none on cost — and it is cost, via the ~50% Hillside discount, that makes the smelter viable. Any SA industrial thesis that depends on competitive power has to underwrite the same variable, and most portfolio businesses will not get Hillside’s terms. Second, the deal is a clean read on how foreign capital actually prices South African risk right now: it will pay up — $5.6bn — for a high-quality, low-cost asset with a bankable power arrangement, and it will walk from one without (Mozal). That is a precise map of where the confidence is and isn’t. Third, it closes the edition’s loop: from gold to cobalt to aluminium, foreign and state capital is acquiring Africa’s physical industrial base — but here, unusually, the value (jobs, output, industrial standing) does stay onshore, provided the power holds. The forward question: the R92bn Eskom “gift” is politically contested, and the successor negotiation past 2031 is where this thesis is actually decided — worth tracking as a live proxy for whether SA can keep any power-intensive industry at all.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Run the sceptic test — make the model argue against your own conclusion

The most common way to misuse these tools is to ask them to help you build your case — and they will, cheerfully, because they are trained to be agreeable. That is the opposite of what an investor needs. The higher-leverage move is to turn the model into the sharpest sceptic in the room: hand it your thesis, your memo, your gut call, and instruct it to demolish it. A good adversarial prompt surfaces the objections a tired partner would miss at 6pm, and it does it without ego, without office politics, and in thirty seconds. You are not looking for the model to be right — you are looking for the one objection you hadn’t weighted, so you can go and check it yourself.

A worked example

Before an IC, paste your recommendation and prompt: “You are a sceptical investment-committee member whose job is to reject this deal. Give me the five strongest reasons this thesis is wrong, ranked by how much they should worry me, and for each one name the single piece of evidence that — if I went and found it — would most change my mind. Be specific, be unkind, and do not hedge. UK English, no preamble.” Then do the same to yesterday’s winning idea. The trick is to separate the generation of objections (which AI is genuinely good at) from the judgement of which ones matter (which stays yours). Over time you’ll notice the model keeps raising one or two objections you habitually under-weight — that pattern is worth more than any single answer.
