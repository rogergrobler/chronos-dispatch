Africa · Fintech · Telecoms Chart
A new Africa Analysis report forecasts that South Africa's mobile virtual network operator (MVNO) market will more than triple — from 4.4 million active SIMs in 2025 to 14.4 million by 2030. The growth engine is no longer the telcos but the banks: Capitec Connect already holds roughly 41% of the market with ~1.9m subscribers, with FNB Connect, Standard Bank Connect and Nedbank Connect close behind. MVNOs are still just 3.7% of SA's total mobile market versus 12.6% for comparable markets at similar age.

South Africa's banks become mobile networks — the customer relationship is the prize, not the airtime

Connecting Africa · Paula Gilbert · Wed 17 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is Thursday's Nigeria story run in reverse. Where Lagos unbundled a foreign-owned rail to reclaim the customer, South African banks are building their own rails to own that same customer end-to-end. Two — the airtime is a loss-leader; the data is the asset. Africa Analysis calls it the “MVNO 3.0” model — connectivity embedded inside a bank's ecosystem to drive engagement, loyalty and lifetime value, not to sell minutes. Three — the headroom is real but so is the concentration. A market still at 3.7% penetration tripling by 2030 is a genuine growth runway; one bank holding 41% of it is also a warning about who captures the upside.

Source: Africa Analysis, 2026 SA MVNO Market Outlook · Capitec Connect alone holds ~41% of the market today
Connecting Africa — “South Africa's MVNO market set to triple by 2030”

The number to sit with is not 14.4 million SIMs; it is 41%. One bank — Capitec — already owns nearly half of an entire telecoms sub-sector it entered only recently, and the report's whole thesis is that the next leg of growth belongs to banks, retailers and insurers, not to a new MTN or Vodacom. For an allocator on the continent, that is the structural signal: the most valuable layer in consumer finance is increasingly the connectivity-plus-data bundle that sits closest to the customer, and the institutions with the existing distribution and balance sheet are the ones positioned to take it.

“When a bank sells you a SIM, it isn't in the airtime business — it is buying the right to see every transaction you make and never let you leave.”

Read it directly against Thursday's Nigeria item and the pattern sharpens into a single thesis we have now traced across three editions: the financial-services and connectivity stacks are collapsing into each other, and the winners are whoever ends up owning the customer relationship outright. MTN wants to lend from its own book (Issue 023); Nigeria reclaimed its credit rail from a foreign operator (Issue 026); now South African banks are quietly becoming telcos. The same convergence, expressed in whichever direction the local incumbent happens to start from.

The honest caution is the concentration. A market tripling off a 3.7% base sounds like open field, but if banking MVNOs are the engine and one bank already holds 41%, the “new entrants” story may resolve into a handful of large balance sheets dividing the spoils — with the long tail of independent MVNOs squeezed exactly as the report's own “price competition is over” framing implies. The thread to keep pulling: watch whether eSIM (already supported by ~59% of SA MVNOs) genuinely lowers the entry barrier for smaller players, or simply makes it cheaper for the banks to acquire customers they were always going to win.

AI Frontier · Sovereignty · Banks Tests a thesis
JPMorgan Chase has reportedly cut off its Hong Kong employees from Anthropic's AI models, removing Claude from the internal list of approved tools. The bank is caught between two pressures: the White House has blocked all foreign nationals from using Anthropic's most advanced models (citing national security), and Anthropic itself bars its products from use in Greater China to guard against “distillation attacks.” JPMorgan follows Goldman Sachs, which made the same move in April. The FT frames it as a threat to Hong Kong's revival as a financial centre, given how fast AI is being adopted in coding.

JPMorgan switches off Anthropic in Hong Kong — the AI sovereignty wall now runs through the bank's own dropdown menu

Semafor / Financial Times · Tom Chivers · Thu 18 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is the kill-switch thesis arriving inside the enterprise. We flagged in Issue 024 that AI access had become a geopolitical lever; now that lever is being pulled at the level of an individual employee's toolbar in Hong Kong. Two — the restriction is bilateral, and that is what makes it sticky. Washington fences the models on one side, Anthropic fences Greater China on the other, and the bank is simply the place the two walls meet. Three — the cost is competitiveness, not compliance. If coding assistance is now table stakes, a financial centre whose bankers can't use the best models is quietly handicapped against one whose bankers can.

Semafor — “JPMorgan restricts Anthropic in Hong Kong”

Place this beside Thursday's DeepSeek item and the symmetry is the whole point. There, sovereignty was something a state-and-industry consortium bought with a $7.4bn cheque. Here, sovereignty is something imposed for free, by subtraction: a model simply disappears from an approved-tools list because of where an employee happens to sit. Both are the same force — AI capability being partitioned along national lines — but this version is more instructive for an operator, because it shows the partition reaching all the way down into the daily workflow of a single office.

“The frontier is no longer fenced at the border. It is fenced at the login — and the same model can be a productivity tool in one office and a forbidden import in another.”

The transferable lesson for Chronos and its portfolio is concrete. Any company building a core process on a single frontier model from a single jurisdiction is now carrying a geopolitical dependency it did not price in. A US model can be switched off for your Hong Kong team; a Chinese model is off-limits to your US clients; a European buyer may insist on neither. The architecture decision — keep models swappable, abstract the provider, retain the option to move — is the same control-plane argument we made in Issue 024, now validated by a bank that just had the choice made for it.

The thread to hold: watch whether “made in Europe” (and, eventually, “made in Africa”) becomes a genuine procurement category rather than a slogan. The next item in this edition is a company raising real money on exactly that premise — and the demand it is selling into is the dependency JPMorgan's Hong Kong staff just discovered they had.

Africa · AI · Sovereignty Hyper-local
President William Ruto announced he met OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman on the sidelines of the G7 Summit in France, discussing establishing Nairobi as the home of the first OpenAI Academy in Eastern Africa and expanding AI education. TechCabal's Adonijah Ndege reads the announcement sceptically: no investment figure, no agreement, no timelines — just “potential collaboration.” His sharper point is structural: the global AI race turns on compute, capital and talent; Africa is short on the first two and rich only in the third, which is why education — cheap, headline-friendly — has become the centrepiece of African AI diplomacy.

Ruto meets Altman — and Africa keeps celebrating conversations as though they were investments

TechCabal · Adonijah Ndege · Thu 18 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is the demand-side companion to today's JPMorgan story. While rich incumbents partition access to frontier models, a Kenyan president is courting one of those model-makers for skills, not silicon. Two — the analysis is unusually honest for a government-adjacent story. Ndege names the trap directly: training programmes produce graduates and photographs, but rarely enduring companies, and a machine-learning engineer in Nairobi can build value that accrues entirely to California. Three — it reframes the sovereignty debate as industrial policy. The winners will be the countries that own the compute, finance the research and retain the IP — not the ones with the most developers.

TechCabal — “What Ruto's meeting with Sam Altman tells us about Kenya's AI ambitions”

This is the most useful African AI piece we have surfaced in weeks precisely because it refuses the press-release framing. The thesis we have been tracking — that AI sovereignty is decided on compute, capital and talent — gets its African stress test here. Kenya genuinely has the third leg: deep software talent, regional engineering hubs for Google and Microsoft, developers contributing to global open source. What it lacks is the compute and the capital, and an OpenAI Academy does nothing to change that ledger. It trains people who can then build value for whoever owns the infrastructure, which today is not in Nairobi.

“A machine-learning engineer in Kilimani can train models for a company in California without leaving Nairobi. That keeps salaries flowing into Kenya — it does not build Kenyan AI companies.”

For an allocator the read is not cynicism about education — the engineers are real and the talent is bankable — but discipline about where value settles. This rhymes precisely with Issue 025's Rest of World piece on Africa's sovereignty rhetoric outrunning its infrastructure, and with Thursday's DeepSeek counterfactual: China bought a frontier lab; Kenya is being offered a training academy. Those are not the same tier of the value chain. The investable question for us is the one Ndege ends on: which African businesses are positioned to own IP and retain the value their talent creates, rather than rent it back from a platform headquartered elsewhere.

The thread to hold across editions: watch whether any of these government-Silicon-Valley announcements convert into something with a number attached — a data centre, a research budget, an equity stake — or whether they remain, in Ndege's phrase, programmes whose greatest achievement is producing photographs for government websites. The presence or absence of a balance-sheet commitment is the entire signal.

AI Frontier · Agents · Security For the portfolio
Barcelona's NeuralTrust has raised a $20m (€17.2m) seed — which it claims is the largest cybersecurity seed ever raised by an EU company — led by Munich's Alstin Capital, to govern enterprise “agent sprawl.” Its pitch: inside large companies, AI agents now multiply faster than security teams can count them, each one wired into databases, email and outside tools. NeuralTrust sells a single control layer — TrustGate (a gateway every agent call passes through), TrustGuard (a live runtime defence) and TrustLens (which maps every agent and watches it). It says it inspects millions of agent interactions a day and flags ~1.2% — about one in 80 — as malicious.

$20m to police the AI agents companies can't even count — and to sell Europe a watchdog that isn't American

The Next Web · Cristian Dina · Thu 18 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is Thursday's Salesforce/Fin deal seen from the risk side. Once the agent layer is worth billions to own, the layer that governs the agents becomes its own category — the build-vs-buy window the workshop has been flagging. Two — the sharper pitch is geography, and it ties straight to today's JPMorgan story. NeuralTrust leans hard into “not American,” selling European banks and governments a sovereignty option exactly as Washington tightens model access. Three — the timing bet is explicit. Gartner predicts 40% of enterprises will pull autonomous agents back by 2027 over governance gaps found only after something breaks; NeuralTrust is betting buyers move before the incident, not after.

The Next Web — “NeuralTrust raised $20M to police the AI agents companies can't even count”

The portfolio-relevant insight is the problem statement, not the round. “Agent sprawl” is the predictable next stage of the deployment story we have tracked since Andrew Ng's letter: once you let agents do delegated, multi-step work, they proliferate — one team builds a support agent on one model, another automates the back office on a second, vendor software arrives with agents of its own — and within months no one can say how many are running, what each may touch, or whether one has been talked into leaking data. Any of our companies that have started deploying agents are already accumulating this exposure whether or not anyone has named it.

“If you connect AI to your email and it starts sending mail to outside addresses, that's a disaster. Most security teams cannot even count the agents already running inside the building.”

This is the workshop's recurring point — the edge is the guardrails, not the model — arriving with a venture round and a Gartner statistic attached. It also rhymes with today's JPMorgan item in a way worth holding: NeuralTrust's second pitch is that it is European, not American, and it cites institutions already swapping US security vendors for home-grown ones. Provenance is becoming a feature you can charge for. For a board, the transferable question is unglamorous and immediate: do we have an inventory of every agent running across the business, a gateway they all pass through, and someone watching what they actually do — or are we, like most enterprises in this story, going to find out the answer only after something breaks?

The thread for future editions: the precedent is set — Palo Alto Networks bought Protect AI for a reported $500m in 2025 — so expect the incumbents to acquire their way into agent security just as they did the agent layer itself. The same consolidation dynamic, one rung up the stack, and the same dependence question underneath it.

Africa · Connectivity · Satellite Structural
Starlink has received a 12-month provisional licence to launch fixed high-speed satellite internet in Côte d'Ivoire in July 2026, the country's digital-transition minister announced. It enters a market that already has Orange Côte d'Ivoire (with Eutelsat) and an MTN–Eutelsat agreement, and follows a fresh licence in Uganda — even as Starlink was denied a licence in Namibia in March and is still fighting ownership rules to enter South Africa. The service is now live in roughly 30 African markets; the contrast between where it is waved in and where it is kept out is becoming a map of the continent's connectivity politics.

Starlink plants another flag — Côte d'Ivoire in July, while Pretoria still keeps it locked out

Connecting Africa · Matshepo Sehloho · Wed 17 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — the satellite land-grab is now a country-by-country political map. Côte d'Ivoire and Uganda wave Starlink in; Namibia blocks it; South Africa ties it up in local-ownership rules — each decision a small sovereignty choice about who controls the sky over the network. Two — the incumbents are no longer waiting. Orange and MTN have already partnered with Eutelsat in the same market, so Starlink arrives into competition, not a vacuum — a healthier dynamic than the first-mover monopoly it enjoyed elsewhere. Three — this is the connectivity twin of Thursday's off-grid energy story. Both are about reaching the users national infrastructure will never economically serve, and both are increasingly contested rather than conceded.

Connecting Africa — “Starlink to go live in Côte d'Ivoire in July”

The interesting variable is no longer whether low-earth-orbit internet reaches Africa — it is who gets to own the gateway and on whose terms. Côte d'Ivoire's provisional licence sits inside a seven-pillar digital roadmap that also covers AI, cybersecurity and local applications: connectivity is being treated as one input into a sovereignty strategy, not a favour granted to a foreign billionaire. Contrast that with South Africa, where Starlink remains blocked behind Black-ownership rules and an active advocacy campaign, and Namibia, which simply said no. The same product, three different answers — and the difference is entirely about who controls the layer.

“The map of where Starlink is waved in and where it is held out is becoming the clearest chart we have of how each African state weighs connectivity against control.”

Hold this against Bezos's parallel move — Amazon's Leo (formerly Kuiper) is applying for its first African ground-station licence in Kenya, promising speeds it claims will double Starlink's. Two of the world's richest men are now racing to own the orbital layer over African connectivity, and the continent's leverage lies precisely in the licensing decision: the one moment a state can extract local ownership, ground infrastructure, or terms before the satellites are overhead. That rhymes with the demand-side sovereignty thread running through this whole edition — from JPMorgan's dropdown to Kenya's AI academy — the question is always who you depend on, and what you can negotiate before you do.

The thread for Chronos: the investable layer here is rarely the satellite operator — it is the terrestrial plumbing the system still needs (ground stations, points of presence, fibre backhaul, local distribution and the telco partnerships that integrate direct-to-device). As Issue 023's Spacesail item flagged, the satellite race is a demand magnet for exactly the kind of unglamorous African infrastructure that captures value locally regardless of whose constellation wins overhead.

Africa · Fintech · SME For the portfolio
At its Yoco Next 2026 event in Johannesburg, the South African fintech — built on helping ~200,000 small merchants accept card payments — unveiled more than 20 new products and a strategic pivot: from payments company to small-business operating system. The launches include Yoco AI (an assistant arriving Q3 2026, built on the May acquisition of Dyner.ai), Yoco Connect (linking accounting, e-commerce and inventory), sector-specific software for restaurants, retailers and salons, savings and lending products, and transaction-fee cuts of up to 40% (~R250m / $15.2m a year returned to merchants).

Yoco stops selling card readers and starts selling an operating system — fewer apps, one platform, the whole relationship

TechCabal · Phathisani Moyo · Thu 18 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is the SME version of today's ownership thread. Like the banks becoming telcos, Yoco is using a beachhead (payments) to capture the entire operating relationship — inventory, accounting, loyalty, cash flow, lending. Two — it is a real AI-deployment case for a portfolio-shaped company. Yoco bought an AI-native SME operating system (Dyner.ai) in May and is wiring it into 200,000 merchants' transaction data — the “agents do the work” posture, applied to the corner shop. Three — the moat is bundling against fragmentation. Its bet is that small businesses are drowning in disconnected tools and will pay (less, deliberately — fees cut up to 40%) for one platform that does it all.

TechCabal — “Fintech Yoco thinks South Africa's small businesses need fewer apps”

Strip away the product launch and Yoco is running the same play as the SA banks in today's first item, one tier down the market: start with the rail closest to the customer (payments), then expand outward until you own the entire operating relationship. The strategic line from CEO Carsten Höltkemeyer — “we are no longer just a payments company” — is the whole thesis. The defensible position in SME software is not the cheapest card reader; it is becoming the system of record the merchant runs their day on, because that is the relationship competitors cannot easily dislodge and the data that makes everything else — lending, insurance, loyalty — underwritable.

“Cutting fees up to 40% while launching twenty products is not generosity — it is buying the right to be the one platform a small business never leaves.”

For Chronos this is a clean, local case study in the AI-deployment thesis we keep underwriting. The Dyner.ai acquisition gives Yoco an AI layer that reads transaction and customer-behaviour data to hand small merchants the kind of insight that used to belong only to large corporations — precisely the “democratise the capabilities of big business” move that creates value in fragmented SME markets. The merchant quoted in the piece — a Cape Town fashion manufacturer competing against Shein and Temu — is exactly the operator for whom one integrated platform is the difference between time spent on admin and time spent surviving.

The honest caution: pivoting from a beloved single-purpose product to an “everything platform” is one of the hardest moves in software, and twenty launches at once is a lot of surface area to execute and support well. The thread to hold is execution, not ambition — whether Yoco's AI and software actually reduce merchant churn and lift lifetime value, or whether the fee cuts simply compress margin while the super-app promise takes longer than the runway allows. It is a thesis-confirming story for the continent; it is also a reminder of how much organisational capability a platform pivot demands.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Don't ask for an answer — make the model build you the 2×2

When a decision is genuinely hard, the most valuable thing a model can give you is not a recommendation but a frame: the two variables that actually matter, laid out as a grid you can place options on. Prose hides the structure of a decision; a 2×2 (or a small decision matrix) forces it into the open and makes the trade-offs arguable. Used before an IC or a tricky portfolio call, this turns the model from an opinion-machine into a thinking tool — it surfaces the axes you were implicitly weighing, shows you where each option actually sits, and exposes the quadrant you have been avoiding. You stay the decision-maker; the model just builds the board you reason on.

A worked example

Paste the situation and say: “Don't give me a recommendation yet. First, identify the two variables that most determine whether this decision succeeds or fails — the real axes, not generic ones like 'risk' and 'reward'. Build a 2×2 with those axes, label all four quadrants with what each would mean in practice, and place each of my options on the grid with a one-line justification. Then tell me which quadrant I am implicitly betting on, which one I am ignoring, and what single piece of evidence would move an option from one quadrant to another. Only after that, give me your view.”
