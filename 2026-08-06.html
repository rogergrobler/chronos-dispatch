Kenya · Payments · Mobile Money Analysis Chart of the day
From 7 August 2026, Safaricom is cutting the fees merchants pay to move money out of their M-PESA tills — by as much as 50% — in a shake-up the company says aligns with the Central Bank of Kenya's pricing principles. On Pay to Mobile (a trader withdrawing daily takings to a personal wallet), the fee on Ksh101–500 drops to Ksh4 from Ksh7, on Ksh501–1,000 to Ksh7 from Ksh13, and on Ksh15,001–20,000 to Ksh53 from Ksh105 — a 49.5% cut. The free-collection threshold on Lipa na M-PESA Buy Goods is doubled to Ksh500, so most small retail sales now carry no fee. For context: M-PESA processed Ksh41.68tn in the year to March — 2.4× the size of Kenya's economy — earning Safaricom Ksh182.7bn, and financial inclusion has climbed to 83.7% of adults from 26.7% in 2006. Inflation held at 6.5% in July, with a general election ~14 months out.

Kenya's regulator just halved M-PESA's merchant fees — what a maturing monopoly rail costs is now a political number

The Standard · Business · Brian Ngugi · Mon 3 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is cheaper transactions for Kenyan traders. The one that matters is who set the price. M-PESA is not a startup fighting for share — it is systemic infrastructure moving 2.4× GDP a year, and once a rail reaches that scale the state stops treating its take-rate as a market outcome and starts treating it as policy. This is the mirror image of the whole book's “own the plumbing, price the crossing” thesis: build the dominant rail and the fee is yours — until it is large enough that the regulator legislates it down.

When the rail becomes systemic, the state prices it — the fee that fragmentation used to earn gets legislated down
The Standard — “Safaricom halves M-Pesa merchant fees in CBK-led move”

The structural read is that transaction-fee margin is a rent the state tolerates only while a payments network is still competing. M-PESA long ago stopped competing in any meaningful sense — 2.4× GDP through one operator, 83.7% inclusion, Ksh182.7bn of Safaricom revenue — and a CBK-blessed fee cut of up to half is what “systemic” costs you. The strategic point for anyone holding a payments thesis is that the durable margin migrates: away from the visible per-transaction fee that regulators can see and compress, and toward the float, the lending book, the merchant data and the cross-sell that sit underneath. Safaricom keeps the volume and the customer; it just can't keep pricing the withdrawal.

Build the dominant rail and the fee is yours — right up until it is large enough that the regulator decides the fee is theirs.

The threads: to Issue 059's item 5 — Airtel Money is racing to become a bank with a spectrum licence; this is the more mature version of the same organism, and the tell for where Airtel is heading is that Safaricom's fee line is now set in Nairobi's central bank, not its boardroom. To the Kenyan merchant war (Issue 058) and Vodacom's half-a-trillion-dollar mobile-money flow — the whole continent's telcos are converging on “own the transaction,” and Kenya is the leading indicator of what happens on the far side of that victory: the price gets capped. To the Chronos payments thesis — it argues for underwriting the businesses whose margin lives in float, credit and data rather than in a headline take-rate a regulator can halve overnight. The sceptic's ledger: this is a fee schedule, not an earnings revision — Safaricom will recover much of it in higher volume and in the lending/float economics the cheaper rail encourages; the timing (14 months to an election, 6.5% inflation) makes it as much political relief as regulatory doctrine; and a “CBK-aligned” cut a dominant operator announces itself is not the same as a binding price control. The forward marker: whether CBK moves from nudging Safaricom's tariffs to setting them — interoperability mandates and hard fee caps are the difference between a maturing rail and a regulated utility, and Kenya will show the rest of the continent which one mobile money becomes.

Nigeria · Mobility · Autonomy Briefing Tests a thesis
Moove, founded in Lagos in 2020 by Ladi Delano and Jide Odunsi, has raised a $250m Series C at a $2.1bn valuation — a 2.8× step-up from the $750m it commanded in its 2024, Uber-led Series B. The round is led by Abu Dhabi's Mubadala, co-led by Woven Capital (Toyota's growth fund) and Ion Pacific, with Uber, BlackRock, MUFG and Franklin Templeton among returning backers. From 76 vehicles in Lagos, Moove now runs ~42,000 vehicles across 29 cities in 13 countries, employs 3,300 people and generates $420m in ARR; it is Uber's largest global fleet partner. The capital funds a hard pivot: from financing human drivers to operating robotaxi infrastructure — “Nests” (robotics-first depots that charge, service and orchestrate driverless fleets) for Waymo in Phoenix and Miami, with London next. Moove raised $1bn+ in debt last year to buy the Waymo vehicles and plans to grow its AV workforce 220% (150→500) by year-end.

A company that started financing Lagos ride-hail drivers just raised $250m to own the physical layer of autonomy

Launch Base Africa · Ecosystem News · Staff Writer · Tue 5 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a big African-founded up-round. The one that matters is the thesis it is built on — autonomy is an infrastructure problem, not a software one. Waymo keeps the driving brain; Moove owns the boring, capital-heavy layer beneath it (the fleet, the charging, the depots, the 24/7 ops) that actually keeps driverless cars on the road. It is the same instinct running through the whole book — the durable value is in the physical plumbing others don't want to build — carried from a Lagos credit gap all the way to a Phoenix robotaxi depot.

Launch Base Africa — “From Lagos Ride-Hailing to Global Robotaxis: Moove Raises $250M at $2.1B Valuation”

The structural read is that Moove has repriced itself by moving down the stack, not up it. It began by solving an African credit gap — financing cars for drivers who couldn't buy them — and has generalised that into a claim: whoever owns and operates the fleet, charging and maintenance layer captures durable margin in a world where the driving software is commoditised by a handful of AV labs. Delano's framing is the whole pitch: “the internet required data centres, AI required compute, autonomy requires fleets.” That is why a Toyota growth fund and a Gulf sovereign are co-leading — they are buying picks-and-shovels for the robotaxi build-out, with a UAE anchor, and Moove is the operator willing to hold the asset-heavy end Waymo and Uber want off their own balance sheets.

Waymo owns the brain; Moove owns the body — the fleets, chargers and depots that turn a driving algorithm into a business that shows up every morning.

The threads: to Issue 059's item 4 (Project BRIDGE) — both are bets that in African-rooted tech the scarce, defensible thing is the physical infrastructure, not the app; the difference is ownership model, and it is worth watching — BRIDGE argues build one backbone and share it, Moove argues own the fleet outright, and today's MTN/IHS item is a third data point on the same own-versus-share axis. To the “African founder scaling into global capital markets” read that ran through the Morocco IPOs — Moove is the venture-side version, a Lagos company now domiciled in Dubai and operating in Phoenix, which raises the honest question of how “African” the returns stay. To the Chronos AI-progress test — this is a rare case where AI's advance shows up as demand for a boring, ownable, cash-generative asset base rather than as another thin model wrapper. The sceptic's ledger: this is one of the most capital-intensive models in the venture universe — $500m+ equity and $1bn+ of debt to date — and its growth leans on a very small number of partners (Uber and Waymo), so concentration and refinancing risk are real and structural; robotaxi timelines have slipped before, and a depot business scales only as fast as the AV rollout it serves; and a $2.1bn mark is a step-up struck in a friendly private round, not a public price. The forward marker: whether the London launch and the Nests model prove Moove can run third-party AV fleets profitably across cities — or whether it remains a superbly-funded financing business wearing an autonomy costume, exposed to whoever it depends on for the cars and the code.

Egypt · Venture Capital · Exits Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Egyptian venture funds have hit an inflection point: with the IPO window frozen, the pound repeatedly devalued and foreign currency scarce, the ecosystem's defining metric has flipped from portfolio mark-ups to DPI (distributed-to-paid-in — actual cash returned to LPs). Firms are engineering liquidity by hand. Beltone Venture Capital booked a partial exit from proptech BirdNest at 3.5× and an 80% IRR while keeping a strategic stake, after an earlier partial exit from logistics startup Bosta (75% IRR); A15 reached 10× DPI on its first fund entirely through trade sales. The casualties are just as visible — Disruptech-backed Brimore (>$30m raised), Mumerz and Nexta have shut. Where exits happen, strategic buyers dominate: US-listed Vercel ($9.3bn) acquired Cairo's Stakpak (autonomous DevOps agents) and Ethiopia's Better Auth, both for their AI roadmap. Launch Africa, a 2020-vintage fund, has returned ~7% of committed capital across 11 exits.

Cairo's funds stopped chasing valuations and started hunting cash — and the exits that clear are strategic buyers, not IPOs

Launch Base Africa · Venture Capital · Staff Writer · Mon 4 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a tough Egyptian funding market. The one that matters is a hard lesson about what an exit is worth in a currency crisis — paper marks in devaluing pounds are meaningless, so DPI becomes the only metric, and the exits that actually clear are trade sales to strategics buying infrastructure and engineering talent (Vercel taking Cairo's Stakpak), not IPOs. The losers are the consumer-facing, marketing-subsidised businesses with no proprietary moat; the winners built distribution rails or deep-tech others wanted to buy. It is the whole continent's consolidation read, seen at the exit end.

The capital arrived; the cash didn't — a record-adjacent H1 sits on top of a frozen exit window
Launch Base Africa — “Egyptian Venture Funds Hunt for Cash Returns Amid Liquidity Crunch”

The structural read is that a capital cycle only completes when it returns cash, and Egypt is now living the completion phase in the harshest possible conditions: a frozen IPO window, a currency that keeps devaluing the very marks funds report, and LPs who want dollars, not story. That forces a brutal sorting. Beltone and A15 show the surviving playbook — partial exits that bank a real multiple while retaining upside, and infrastructure-first companies (payment gateways, distribution, DevOps agents) that strategic acquirers actually want. The casualties — Brimore, Mumerz, Nexta — share a profile: consumer-facing, subsidy-fuelled, no proprietary rail. The single most important line for an allocator is A15's own post-mortem: its wins were built on distribution moats, its failures on commoditised consumer sectors where distribution was absent.

In a devaluing currency, a portfolio mark-up is a rumour. DPI is the only sentence an LP believes — and the market that can't IPO learns to sell to whoever needs the rails.

The threads: to Issue 059's item 6 (Côte d'Ivoire's state-funded demand) — Abidjan is trying to manufacture the top of this cycle; Cairo is living the bottom, and the pairing is the reminder that seeding demand is easy relative to producing realised cash years later. To the instrument-mismatch thread (Rwanda's patient debt, Issue 058) — Egypt is the case study in what happens when equity-and-a-ten-year-clock meets a market with no exit door: you get creative liquidity engineering because the standard instrument simply doesn't fit. To “context is the new IP” and the Chronos AI-progress test — the exits that cleared were AI/deep-tech acqui-hires (Vercel/Stakpak, Better Auth), which says the durable African asset a global buyer will pay for is engineering talent plus proprietary rails, not a localised consumer app. The sceptic's ledger: this is a single-market snapshot dressed as a trend, and Egypt's FX crisis is idiosyncratic — you can't read Lagos or Nairobi straight off Cairo; partial exits and 10× DPI headlines are self-reported by the funds that benefit from telling that story; and “strategic buyers seeking talent” is a polite name for down-round acqui-hires that can wipe out the equity beneath the founders. The forward marker: whether DPI, not TVPI, becomes the number African LPs underwrite to across the continent — because if it does, the whole ecosystem reprices toward businesses with a plausible cash exit, and away from the growth-at-all-costs consumer plays that defined the last cycle.

Nigeria · Fintech · Capital Markets Analysis Longitudinal thread
PalmPay — the Nigeria-focused fintech backed by Taiwan's MediaTek and China's Transsion (Tecno, Infinix, Itel) and NetEase — is preparing a Hong Kong IPO at a $1bn-plus valuation, and is in talks to raise ~$200m in a round that would confer unicorn status. It has signed with Hong Kong's OASES to base global operations there as an Africa–Asia bridge. Founded 2019 on a $40m seed, PalmPay now reports 35m registered users (mostly Nigeria) and ~1m business clients, helped by pre-installation on millions of Transsion phones. It turned profitable in 2025; revenue doubled from $64m (2023) to $128m+ (2024). Its rival OPay (SoftBank-backed) is exploring a US IPO at ~$4bn — so Nigeria's two largest Chinese-backed fintechs are splitting across two oceans. Hong Kong's IPO market has roared back: $41.6bn raised to end-July, up 153% year on year, with a pre-profit “Chapter 18C” tech channel.

Nigeria's two payments giants pick different oceans to list on — PalmPay to Hong Kong, OPay to New York

Launch Base Africa · Capital Markets · Staff Writer · Tue 5 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is another African fintech eyeing an IPO. The one that matters is where — and what the choice reveals. PalmPay's cap table is Asian (MediaTek, Transsion, NetEase), so it routes to Hong Kong's resurgent, tech-friendly market and its pre-profit listing channel; OPay's is SoftBank, so it points at New York. Two companies serving the same Lagos streets are being pulled toward different capital pools by their owners, not their customers — a live test of the “where does an African champion list” question the book keeps circling.

Launch Base Africa — “Why African Fintech PalmPay Is Eyeing Hong Kong for a $1bn IPO”

The structural read is that a company's listing venue is set by its capital lineage more than its customer base, and PalmPay is the clean illustration. Its distribution moat is Transsion pre-installation across African handsets; its ownership and its natural investor audience are Asian; so Hong Kong — where mobile-payments models from Ant and WeChat Pay are deeply understood, where the IPO market has rebounded 153% and where Chapter 18C lets pre-profit tech list on potential — is the logical home. OPay, wearing SoftBank's colours and chasing a $4bn US tag, points the other way. The same underlying business — Nigerian agent banking and consumer payments — is being pulled toward two different exchanges by the geography of its shareholders, which tells you the “African fintech IPO” will rarely be an African-exchange event.

PalmPay serves Lagos and lists in Hong Kong; OPay serves Lagos and eyes New York. The customers are the same street; the capital lives on opposite oceans.

The threads: to Issue 059's item 5 — Airtel is carving out its mobile-money arm for a London listing to earn a fintech multiple; PalmPay and OPay are the pure-play version of the same trade, and the three venues (London, Hong Kong, New York) map the fractured menu of where African financial-services value gets priced. To the Morocco “list it at home” story (Issue 058) — that was the counter-current, a champion staying on its domestic bourse; PalmPay is the dominant current, the champion leaving for the deepest compatible pool, and the contrast is the real strategic question for any African company weighing an exit. To the Chronos read on capital access — it argues the scarce asset is a credible path to public markets, and that path increasingly runs offshore, which shapes how and where value accrues to founders and early backers. The sceptic's ledger: this is “in talks,” sourced to people familiar — a $200m private round is the confirmed step, an IPO is contingent on markets and on PalmPay being ready for the disclosure a listing forces; the Transsion pre-install that built the 35m-user base is exactly what public investors will probe, because installed is not the same as active or profitable; and Nigeria's FX, fraud and regulatory risks (see the running crypto and licensing threads) all land on the main market these numbers depend on. The forward marker: whether either giant actually files — a real PalmPay HK prospectus or OPay S-1 would be the first true public price on Nigerian consumer fintech, and the disclosure alone would tell us more about unit economics than three years of funding-round headlines.

Pan-Africa · Telecoms · Infrastructure Briefing Longitudinal thread
IHS Holding shareholders voted at an EGM on 4 August 2026 to approve MTN Group's acquisition of the remaining shares in the tower company — clearing the two-thirds special-resolution bar and a key condition for MTN to take IHS fully private in a deal valuing it at about $6.2bn. Under the structure, a temporary Sub-Merger Co merges into IHS, which survives as a wholly-owned MTN subsidiary and is delisted from the New York Stock Exchange. IHS operates the passive infrastructure — the towers — on which mobile networks hang; MTN is its anchor tenant. The transaction still needs regulatory approvals in several markets. It reverses a decade-long industry orthodoxy in which operators sold their towers to independent tower companies to lighten their balance sheets; MTN is buying them back.

MTN moves to buy back its towers and take IHS private — the industry that spent a decade selling passive infrastructure reverses course

TechCabal · Telecoms · Frank Eleanya · Tue 5 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a shareholder vote clearing a $6.2bn take-private. The one that matters is the direction — for a decade telcos sold their towers to independent tower companies to look asset-light and please equity markets; MTN buying IHS back and pulling it off the NYSE is that orthodoxy going into reverse. When a public tower company can't hold a valuation its anchor tenant thinks is cheap, the tenant re-integrates the asset and prices it privately. It is the same own-versus-share question running through the connectivity thesis, answered here with “own.”

TechCabal — “IHS shareholders back MTN's bid for full ownership”

The structural read is that the sale-and-leaseback era for African towers is being tested by the company that started much of it. The independent-tower model rested on a bet that passive infrastructure deserved its own listed, specialist owner and that operators were better off asset-light. MTN's move says the opposite for its own footprint: that at $6.2bn it would rather own the towers outright, capture the economics it was paying away in lease fees, and stop the public market from discounting an asset it considers strategic. Delisting from the NYSE removes the daily price and the public scrutiny — control and quiet in exchange for the balance-sheet weight the industry spent ten years shedding.

For a decade telcos sold their towers to look asset-light. MTN is buying them back, taking them private, and betting that owning the steel beats leasing it.

The threads: to Issue 059's item 4 (Project BRIDGE) — the sharpest contrast in the book right now on how to structure African infrastructure: BRIDGE wants one shared, open-access backbone owned by a purpose-built SPV, while MTN wants to own its own passive layer outright and take it private; both are convinced the durable value is the physical infrastructure, and they disagree entirely on who should hold it. To today's Moove item — a third point on the same own-versus-share axis (own the fleet), which is quietly becoming this week's connective question. To the Chronos connectivity book — a tower re-integration changes the economics of who pays whom for coverage, and matters for any position exposed to African passive infrastructure or to MTN itself. The sceptic's ledger: taking a NYSE-listed asset private removes a transparent price and concentrates a strategically vital, capital-heavy business inside one operator — the opposite of the diversification the tower-co model was built to provide; the deal still hangs on multi-market regulatory approvals that can reshape or delay it; and re-loading towers onto MTN's balance sheet is exactly the weight equity investors once rewarded it for shedding, so the market's verdict on the leverage is not yet in. The forward marker: whether other operators follow MTN back toward ownership or double down on the independent-tower model — the answer decides whether this is one company's balance-sheet play or the start of the African tower industry re-integrating.

Kenya · AI · M&A Briefing Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Cloud9, a Kenyan business digital-banking startup, has acquired Chpter — a social-commerce platform whose AI sales tools live inside WhatsApp and Instagram — in an all-stock deal after roughly four months of talks (terms undisclosed). It is Cloud9's second acquisition in three months, after buying ticketing platform M-Tickets in May for ~KES100m ($773k). The deal reunites founders Tesh Mbaabu and Mesongo Sibuti with the company they left in September 2025 — weeks before launching Cloud9. Cloud9 has shut the standalone Chpter app and is folding its AI sales tools into its banking platform; ~4,500 Chpter businesses carry over, and Chpter's product, engineering, customer-success and commercial teams have joined. The stated strategy is explicit: acquire businesses where customers already transact, then layer financial services on top.

A Kenyan neobank keeps buying the places merchants already sell — then layers a bank on top of the AI

TechCabal · Startups · Kenn Abuya · Tue 5 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a small all-stock acqui-deal between founders who know each other well. The one that matters is the playbook it makes explicit — buy the surface where commerce already happens (chat-commerce AI, ticketing), then attach the bank. It is the same “own the transaction, monetise the flow” instinct the telcos are executing at continental scale, run here as a nimble, all-equity roll-up: Cloud9 isn't acquiring revenue so much as acquiring distribution and the AI that sits on it, cheaply, in stock.

TechCabal — “Exclusive: Kenya's Cloud9 acquires Chpter in all-stock startup deal”

The structural read is that the durable prize in African fintech is proximity to the transaction, and Cloud9 is buying its way to it one small deal at a time. Rather than compete for merchants from a cold start, it acquires the tools those merchants already use — Chpter's AI sales agents inside WhatsApp and Instagram, M-Tickets' ticketing rails — and then layers business banking, credit and payments on top of an installed base that is already transacting. Paying in stock keeps cash for the banking build and turns acquired founders into aligned shareholders; shutting the standalone Chpter app signals the point was never the product but the ~4,500 merchants and the engineering team that built the AI. This is the “context is the new IP” micro-M&A pattern applied with a balance-sheet purpose.

Cloud9 isn't buying apps — it's buying the places merchants already sell, and the AI that runs the selling, so it can quietly become their bank.

The threads: to Issue 059's item 5 and today's Safaricom item — the same “own the transaction stream” logic the telcos pursue at 2.4×-GDP scale, executed here by a startup at the opposite end, buying distribution instead of building it. To today's Egyptian-exits item — the flip side of the same consolidation coin: Cairo shows funds engineering exits through trade sales, Nairobi shows an operator doing the buying, and both are the African market concentrating capital and talent into fewer, more integrated platforms. To the “context is the new IP” AI-M&A read (and today's Vercel/Stakpak thread) — the asset being bought is proprietary workflow plus the team, exactly the acqui-hire logic playing out from Cairo to Nairobi. The sceptic's ledger: two acquisitions in three months by a startup barely a year old is a roll-up in a hurry, and roll-ups more often destroy value than compound it; an all-stock deal with no disclosed terms and a shuttered flagship app can quietly mean “talent rescue” rather than “accretive acquisition,” with churn risk as Chpter users are migrated; and layering a bank onto acquired merchants only works if those merchants actually adopt the financial products, which is a much harder sell than getting them to keep using a tool they already had. The forward marker: whether Cloud9 converts the ~4,500 inherited merchants into active banking customers — deposits, credit, payments — or simply accumulates logos and engineers; the first is a compounding distribution machine, the second is an expensive way to hire a team.

From the Workshop

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Give the work a home — use a Project so you stop re-attaching the same five files every morning

Most recurring work carries the same context every time: a portfolio company, a live deal, a board cycle. If you paste that context into a fresh chat each morning — the shareholders' agreement, the last four board packs, the cast of people, the way you like updates written — you pay a re-upload tax daily and the assistant still starts cold. A Project fixes this: it holds the reference material and your standing instructions once, so every new conversation inside it already knows the terms, the history and your house format. You move from “here is everything again, now help” to simply “what changed, and what should I be worried about,” and the answer comes from inside the accumulated context instead of from zero. The discipline is to stop treating each question as a standalone chat and start treating a recurring subject as a place the work lives.

A worked example

Create one Project per portfolio company. Drop in the last four board packs, the SHA, the latest management accounts, and a one-line note: “write me updates lead-with-the-conclusion, UK English, flag anything off-thesis.” Then each month just ask: “What changed since last quarter, what did management not explain, and what would you watch?” It answers against the full stack you loaded once — no re-attaching, no re-explaining — and every future question in that Project inherits the same memory.
