Africa · Banking · Credit Chart
An analysis of the 2025 annual reports of Nigeria's four largest banks — GTCO, Access, UBA and First Bank — finds they held ₦89.94 trillion ($65.6bn) in customer deposits, much of it from millions of retail savers, yet directed nearly 90% of their lending to corporates. Retail loans totalled ₦3.47tn (10.5%) against ₦29.60tn (89.5%) to companies — a 1:10.3 ratio. The gap widens by bank: GTCO 1:5, Access 1:6, UBA 1:11, First Bank 1:18. Only ~6% of Nigerian adults borrow from formal sources even though 64% are financially included, and the sector's non-performing-loan ratio has climbed to 8.03%, above the 5% prudential threshold.

For every ₦1 Nigeria's banks lend a consumer, a corporate gets ₦10 — the credit gap, finally counted

TechCabal · Follow the Money · Temitayo Jaiyeola · Mon 22 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is yesterday's ownership thread, inverted. The CBN spent last week fencing who may own the customer; this data shows the banks had already abandoned the retail borrower, funding their books with consumer deposits but lending the proceeds to oil, telecoms and manufacturing. Two — the gap is the entire investment case for African fintech. 505 digital lenders have been licensed since 2022; FairMoney alone disbursed >₦150bn in 2025. The banks won't serve this customer, so someone else will. Three — corporate-heavy is not safe. Rising NPLs (8.03%, up from 4% in 2022) show the “low-risk” corporate book is itself under strain from inflation, rates and FX.

Source: TechCabal / 2025 annual reports · Nigeria sector NPL ratio 8.03% (Jan 2026), above the 5% prudential threshold
TechCabal — “For every ₦1 Nigerian banks lent consumers, corporates got ₦10”

Hold this number next to Monday's lead. The CBN spent last week building a statutory ceiling on how much of the customer any one operator may own; this analysis shows that, on the dimension that matters most to a household or a small business — access to credit — the incumbent banks had quietly stepped back years ago. They take deposits from millions of ordinary Nigerians and lend the proceeds, ten-to-one, to a handful of large corporates that are cheaper to monitor and easier to collateralise. The retail customer is a funding source, not a borrower.

“The banks take the deposits of the many and lend to the few. The whole of African fintech is a bet that the gap between those two facts is an investable market.”

For an allocator this is the clearest single chart of the thesis Chronos keeps returning to. A market where only ~6% of adults borrow formally, where the largest bank lends ₦18 to companies for every ₦1 to a person, and where 505 licensed digital lenders have rushed into the vacuum, is not a story about disruption for its own sake — it is a structural mispricing of credit access at the scale of a 230-million-person economy. The caution rides in the same data: NPLs have doubled to 8% even on the conservative corporate book, and the FCCPC's 2025 penalties for predatory recovery are a reminder that lending to the underserved at scale is where reputations and licences are made or lost.

The thread to hold across editions: this is the quantified floor beneath Monday's MTN-lending item and today's M-KOPA piece. The banks have left a continent-sized gap; the contested question — the one worth underwriting — is which operators can fill it with durable unit economics rather than a debt-trap and a regulatory backlash. Watch GTCO's Quick Credit and Access's Oxygen: when an incumbent's retail-credit line starts compounding at the rate the fintechs do, the gap is closing — and the competitive map redraws.

Africa · Fintech · Credit For the portfolio
M-KOPA, the Nairobi-headquartered asset-financier, says it has now served nearly 10 million customers across Africa and deployed more than $2bn in credit. Smartphones sold on instalment remain its customer-acquisition tool, but it increasingly earns from lending, insurance and financial services built on the repayment data those devices generate. It receives 1m+ repayments and acquires 10,000+ customers daily; each repayment is a credit signal feeding an AI-driven risk model. Half its customers live on under $5.50/day and 38% are accessing formal credit for the first time. Nigeria is its fastest-growing lending market; portfolio losses run ~10–12%. M-KOPA turned its first profit in 2024 ($9.2m, on 66% revenue growth, after a $24.7m loss the year before).

M-KOPA's first loan is a phone — and every repayment is a data point that prices the next one

TechCabal · Temitayo Jaiyeola · Fri 19 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is the mechanism behind today's lead. Where the banks see a retail customer too costly to underwrite, M-KOPA manufactures a credit history from scratch: a customer who pays daily for 30 days has generated 30 signals, and the device is collateral that can be remotely disabled. Two — it is an AI-progress story in the numbers, not the marketing. The moat is a proprietary repayment dataset and loss-forecasting models — exactly the feedback loop Chronos screens for. Three — it is the same playbook as Monday's MTN. Own the customer, own the data, then sell credit off your own book; M-KOPA is the smaller, further-along proof of the thesis.

TechCabal — “Why M-KOPA is using smartphone repayments to expand digital lending”

Read directly against today's lead item, this is the gap and the gap-filler on one page. The Nigerian banks lend ₦10 to corporates for every ₦1 to a consumer because the consumer has no financial record they can price. M-KOPA's entire business is the construction of that record: it gives a customer a device they could not otherwise afford, and in doing so creates the daily repayment stream that becomes a proprietary credit profile no bank possesses. “We are not selling them a phone,” the CFO puts it; “we are selling them the ability to qualify for things they would otherwise not qualify for.”

“The bank needs a credit history to lend. M-KOPA manufactures the credit history as a by-product of the first sale. That is the whole moat — and it compounds with every repayment.”

For an allocator this is a cleaner expression of the Chronos AI-progress test than most listed names offer. The asset is a feedback loop: 1m+ repayments a day, each one improving the loss model that prices the next loan, with an increasing share of revenue coming from digital financial services rather than the device sale. The economics are turning — first profit in 2024, losses held to 10–12% on a base where half the customers live on under $5.50 a day. The caution is the same as in the lead item: this is population-scale lending to thin-file borrowers, and the difference between a financial-inclusion champion and the next predatory-lending headline is entirely in the discipline of the underwriting and the recovery practices.

The thread to hold: M-KOPA, Moniepoint (underwriting merchants off transaction data, ₦1tn disbursed in 2025) and MTN (Monday's item) are three versions of one idea — proprietary behavioural data as the credit engine the banks never built. For a portfolio with exposure to African lending, the question to carry into the next IC is concrete: whose data loop is deepest, whose losses are most stable through a cycle, and whose funding base can survive a dollar-liquidity squeeze. That is where this thesis is won or lost — not in the adoption numbers, but in the loss curve.

Africa · Mobility · Capital Hyper-local
Spiro, one of Africa's largest electric-motorcycle and battery-swapping companies, has secured an additional $55m equity cheque from Chinese early-stage investor NewTrails Capital, lifting a round disclosed earlier in June to $270m and total disclosed funding to roughly $557m. The raise lands days after Spiro named former Indofast Energy chief Anant Badjatya (who ran a 1,800-station Indian swapping network) as group CEO. Founded in 2022, Spiro says it has deployed 100,000+ electric vehicles and 2,500+ swapping stations across seven countries including Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda and Nigeria. NewTrails calls it “an infrastructure-like business”; 30% of each motorcycle's value is now produced locally, with batteries sourced from Chinese manufacturers.

Spiro takes a second cheque in three weeks — Africa's most-funded EV bet deepens its China ties to build the rails

TechCabal · Kenn Abuya · Mon 22 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — the framing is “infrastructure,” not “vehicles.” The investable asset is the battery-swapping network — the toll-road of two-wheel electric mobility — not the motorcycle. NewTrails is explicit about it, and it is the same own-the-rails logic that runs through this whole edition. Two — it is Monday's dependency sub-plot, in energy. Just as Flutterwave deepened its tie to a US stablecoin, Spiro deepens its tie to Chinese capital and battery supply to scale faster than African capital alone would allow. Three — the capital is patient and strategic. A second cheque inside three weeks, plus an operator CEO from India's swapping market, signals a build-out, not a trade.

TechCabal — “Spiro secures another $55 million three weeks after $215 million raise”

We run this beside the lending items deliberately, because it is the same structural move one sector over. Spiro is not really selling motorcycles; it is building an energy network — 2,500 swap stations and counting — and financing the riders onto it. The battery-swapping grid is to electric mobility what the repayment loop is to M-KOPA's credit business: the durable, hard-to-replicate asset that captures value long after the first sale. NewTrails naming it “infrastructure-like” is the tell — this is patient capital underwriting a toll-road, not a venture punt on unit sales.

“The motorcycle is the customer-acquisition cost. The swap network is the asset. Spiro is raising at infrastructure scale to own the rails before anyone else can lay them.”

For an allocator two things stand out. First, the dependency mirror from Monday: an African champion is deliberately deepening its reliance on a single foreign ecosystem — Chinese capital and Chinese batteries — to compress years off its build-out. That is rational and probably necessary; it is also a concentration risk to price, because the cost of capital and the supply of cells are now set partly in Shenzhen. The 30%-local-content figure is the number to watch: the further it climbs, the more of the value Spiro captures and the less exposed it is to a supply or policy shock abroad. Second, the governance signal — an operator CEO who ran 1,800 stations in India — says the next chapter is execution and density, not story.

The thread to hold across editions: Africa's energy-and-mobility transition is increasingly financed and supplied from Asia, just as its payment rails are increasingly threaded through US stablecoins. The recurring Chronos question applies unchanged — when the capital and the components come from abroad, which African operators capture the margin on the network they build, and which merely assemble someone else's hardware for a thin spread. Spiro's swap density and local-content curve are where that answer will show up.

Africa · Connectivity · Regulatory Structural
Namibia's communications regulator, Cran, said on Monday it had dismissed Starlink's appeal against the March rejection of its licence applications, reaffirming that SpaceX's satellite-internet service failed the local ownership-and-control test under section 46 of the Communications Act. Of 624 public reconsideration requests, 622 were dismissed; the two that qualified introduced no new facts. Starlink's reconsideration was also filed after the 23 April deadline. In neighbouring South Africa the path has stalled on near-identical grounds — a 30% historically-disadvantaged-ownership requirement SpaceX says it won't meet — with Icasa flagging that the minister's equity-equivalent workaround needs a law change, pushing any legal launch beyond 2027.

Namibia tells a $3-trillion SpaceX, again, that it cannot own the sky over Windhoek

TechCentral · Reuters / NewsCentral · Mon 22 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is Monday's $1.75tn IPO meeting an African ownership line. The same SpaceX that just became one of the four most valuable companies on earth cannot get a licence in Namibia or South Africa, because the binding constraint is local equity, not technology or capital. Two — the leverage runs both ways. A small state can hold the line against an immensely powerful counterparty — but only if it is willing to forgo the signal, which is a real cost to rural users. Three — it threads straight to Monday's subsea-cable item. Block the satellite and the terrestrial-and-subsea plumbing becomes the only route in — which is exactly where local value can still be captured.

TechCentral — “Namibia tells Starlink to take a hike – again”

Set this directly against Monday's SpaceX item and the picture completes. We wrote then that the counterparty on the other side of every African licence negotiation had just become immeasurably stronger — a $3-trillion entity with a cost of capital no regulator can match. Today, two small African regulators demonstrate the one lever they still hold: market access, gated on local ownership. Namibia did not dispute Starlink's technology or its capital; it dismissed the appeal on section 46 — ownership and control — and batted away 622 of 624 public objections on procedure. The wall is equity, and equity is the one thing SpaceX has said, repeatedly, it will not concede.

“The most valuable connectivity company on earth can be kept out by a clause about who owns the local subsidiary. The question is who blinks first — and what the wait costs the farmer in Kavango.”

For an allocator the read is about leverage and its price. The southern-African ownership regime is, in effect, a negotiating position: cede local equity (or a credible equity-equivalent), or stay in the grey market. It is a defensible stance — the same instinct visible across this edition, from Lagos's concentration rule to Windhoek's licence test — and it preserves the possibility of local value capture in the connectivity stack. But it carries a cost the spreadsheet should name: every month of stand-off is a month rural users go without the capacity that fibre will not reach for years, and that compounds the digital divide the rest of this edition keeps circling. South Africa's parallel impasse — an equity-equivalent fix that now needs an Act of Parliament — shows how long that cost can run.

The thread to hold across editions: pair this with Monday's Medusa cable and the Starlink map from Issue 027. The investable layer in African connectivity remains the terrestrial and subsea plumbing the signal still needs — precisely because the satellite layer is owned by a counterparty the continent cannot out-capitalise and, for now, will not licence. When the satellite is blocked, the toll-road underneath it gets more valuable, not less.

Africa · AI · Infrastructure Tests a thesis
In a TechCabal interview marking the graduation of 15 startups from eight countries through Google for Startups Accelerator Africa (60% already profitable, ~$60,000 average monthly revenue), Google's managing director for Africa, Alex Okosi, argued that African founders have embraced AI but that investment, infrastructure and compute have not kept pace. Africa holds only ~1% of global data-centre capacity, forcing builders to move data offshore to process it. The African Development Bank projects AI could add up to $1.5tn to the continent's economy by 2035 — ~40% of current GDP — if compute, connectivity (subsea cables Equiano, Umoja) and talent scale fast enough.

Africa's AI bottleneck isn't models — it's compute, capital and people, says Google's Africa head

TechCabal · Kenn Abuya · Fri 19 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is the constructive version of Monday's AI-policy failure. SA's hallucinated policy showed the institutional-capacity gap; Okosi names what closing it actually requires — data centres, cloud, connectivity and skills, not more model access. Two — the scale of the prize is real and quantified. $1.5tn / ~40% of GDP by 2035 is the AfDB's number, and it is conditional on infrastructure the continent does not yet own (1% of global data-centre capacity). Three — it sets up today's closing item. The talent that would build and run that infrastructure is the very cohort emigrating in a hurry.

TechCabal — “Google's Alex Okosi on what's holding back Africa's AI startups”

This is the item to read as a corrective to the easy AI narrative. Okosi's argument is that African startups have already crossed the adoption line — 60% of Google's latest cohort is profitable, building fraud detection, transit optimisation and payments on real models — and that the binding constraint has moved downstream to the unglamorous foundations: compute, cloud, connectivity and skills. The single most arresting figure is that Africa holds roughly 1% of global data-centre capacity, which means many African AI products are literally built by shipping data offshore to be processed and bringing the answers back. Sovereignty over AI, on those terms, is a long way off — and it is an infrastructure problem before it is a policy one.

“The model is no longer the scarce input. The scarce inputs are a data centre on the continent, the power to run it, and an engineer who hasn't yet emigrated.”

For an allocator the read is where the value actually accrues. If the AfDB's $1.5tn-by-2035 figure is even directionally right, the capture points are not the consumer-facing AI apps that attract the headlines but the picks-and-shovels Okosi lists: data-centre capacity, the subsea-and-terrestrial connectivity that feeds it (Equiano, Umoja — the same plumbing thread as Monday's Medusa cable), and the cloud regions being built in South Africa. This is the same “back the toll-road” logic that runs through Spiro's swap network and the lending items' data loops. The deployment-for-portcos angle is concrete too: the constraint Okosi describes — data leaving the continent for processing — is a live cost and a live opportunity for any Chronos company weighing where to host its AI workloads.

The thread to hold across editions: this rhymes with Issue 027's Ruto/Altman item and Monday's AI-policy collapse. The continent's AI ambition is genuine and the adoption is real; the gap is foundational capacity — compute, power, capital and people. Today's closing item is the sharpest possible illustration of the last of those: you cannot run the data centres you don't yet have with the engineers who are already filling in Australian visa forms.

Africa · Talent · Structural Chart
New enquiry data from immigration firm New World Immigration logs 437 IT, software, data and cybersecurity professionals who approached it about moving to Australia between 22 April and 22 June 2026 — and the cohort is in a hurry: ~70% want to leave “immediately” or within six months, against the usual one-to-two-year horizon. Johannesburg, not “Silicon Cape,” leads the outflow (115 leads vs Cape Town's 76), driven by the JSE-listed corporates, banks, insurers and mining houses whose IT departments employ at scale. Australia's skills-assessment rules reward formal ICT degrees, stranding self-taught engineers with years of shipped code. Twenty of the leads are cybersecurity specialists — against a global shortage the ISC2 puts at 4.8 million unfilled roles.

Joburg, not Cape Town, is the epicentre of South Africa's tech brain drain — and the enterprise floor is emptying

TechCentral · Fanie van Rooyen · Mon 22 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — this is the human side of the AI-capacity gap. Okosi says the constraint is compute, capital and people; this is the people, packing. The continent cannot run the data centres it lacks with engineers it is exporting. Two — the loss is institutional, not start-up. The exodus is concentrated in Joburg's enterprise IT — the banks, insurers and miners — so the skills leaving are exactly those that run the systems Chronos's portfolio and counterparties depend on. Three — cybersecurity is the acute edge. Twenty specialists in one firm's two-month pipeline, against a 4.8m global shortfall, thins the defence of South African banks, hospitals and state systems.

Source: New World Immigration enquiry data via TechCentral · leads with a recorded location, 22 Apr–22 Jun 2026
TechCentral — “Joburg the epicentre of South Africa's tech brain drain”

We close the edition here deliberately, because it is the binding constraint underneath every other item. Okosi's argument was that Africa's AI ceiling is compute, capital and people; this is the people, and the direction of travel is out. The detail that should give a South African allocator pause is not the headline number but its shape: the exodus is led by Johannesburg, not the “Silicon Cape” start-up scene — which means the skills leaving sit inside the JSE-listed banks, insurers and miners whose technology departments run the country's financial and industrial plumbing. This is institutional capacity walking out of the building, and roughly 70% of those who gave a timeframe want to be gone within six months.

“You cannot transform a bank with AI if the engineers who would build it are filling in Australian visa forms. The talent gap is the execution gap, made personal.”

For an allocator this connects two threads Chronos cares about. First, it is the management-and-culture question made concrete: when we ask whether a portfolio company or a target has the DNA to execute an AI-transformation thesis, the honest answer increasingly depends on whether it can retain a scarce, globally-mobile engineering bench against an Australian salary and a permanent-residency pathway. Twenty cybersecurity specialists in a single firm's two-month pipeline — against a 4.8-million-role global shortage — is a quiet operational-risk flag for every South African financial institution. Second, it sharpens the “where does value accrue” read: scarce local talent is itself an asset class, and the companies that can build a magnetic enough culture (or a remote-first model that keeps the engineer in Joburg) hold an advantage that does not show up in a multiple.

The thread to hold across editions: pair this with today's Okosi item and Monday's AI-policy collapse. Africa's AI opportunity is real and its adoption is genuine, but the constraint is foundational — compute, power, capital and people — and the most mobile of those four is voting with its feet. The government's mooted white paper to lure expatriate talent home is the right instinct; the data says the harder problem is keeping the people who are still here. For an investor, that is not a macro footnote — it is a diligence question to ask of every South African management team this year.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Force the 2×2 — make the model collapse a mess into a decision

Three of today's items are really one question wearing different clothes: who captures the value when the gap is huge but the execution is hard? Nigeria's lending split, M-KOPA's data loop, Spiro's swap network — each is a trade-off between two axes. When a situation has too many moving parts to hold in your head, the fastest way to a decision is to force it onto two. Ask the model to pick the two variables that actually drive the outcome, build a 2×2, and place every option in a quadrant. It turns a sprawling memo into a single picture you can argue with — and, more usefully, it exposes the empty quadrant nobody is occupying, which is often where the opportunity sits.

A worked example

Paste your notes and say: “Reduce this to a 2×2. First, tell me which two variables most determine success here and why those two over the others. Then draw the matrix, place each option (and each competitor) in a quadrant, and name each quadrant in plain language. Finally: which quadrant is empty or underserved, what would it take to own it, and which of my current options is mis-positioned? Don't hedge — commit to the two axes and defend them.” The value is as much in the argument over which two axes matter as in the grid itself.
