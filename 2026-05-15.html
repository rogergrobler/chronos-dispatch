Agentic workflows in production: three case studies from the past quarter

News wire compilation · Fri 15 May 2026

Three reported deployments this quarter make the agentic-workflows pattern concrete. A US insurance underwriter rebuilt claims review around an iterative agent that reads the loss report, queries the policy, drafts the determination, and routes anomalies to a human — cycle time down 70%, agreement-with-human-decision at 94%. A European law firm rebuilt contract redlining around a similar loop and reports 50% reduction in associate review hours per matter. A telco rebuilt L1 customer support and is now resolving 60% of inbound queries end-to-end without escalation.

The Batch coverage

The three cases share a structural pattern that's worth naming explicitly. Each rebuilt a workflow — not a single task — around an iterative agent loop. The agent isn't replacing the human; it's restructuring the unit of work. What used to be ten sequential human steps is now one agent execution with a single human checkpoint at the end.

The 94% agreement-with-human number on the insurance underwriter case is the one to dwell on. A 6% disagreement rate at 70% lower cycle time means the agent is producing more total correct decisions per unit time, not fewer. The economic profile of underwriting just changed.

"What used to be ten sequential human steps is now one agent execution with a single human checkpoint at the end." — observation

Issue 004 sharpened the diagnosis with Andrew Ng (agentic workflows beat better models). Today the case studies confirm the prescription. The four issues together describe the same arc: capability is abundant, deployment is the constraint, workflow-rebuild is the moat.

Net Interest: stablecoin infrastructure is the structural fintech story of 2026

Marc Rubinstein · Net Interest · This week

In a recent appearance, Marc Rubinstein named three structural forces shaping 2026 finance: why the U.S. has thousands of community banks, the durability of the 30-year mortgage product, and stablecoin infrastructure as a settlement layer. The third is the one that compounds. Stablecoins aren't winning at the consumer layer — they're winning quietly as B2B settlement rails for cross-border, particularly in EM corridors.

Read at netinterest.co

Rubinstein's specific argument: the headlines on stablecoins are about consumer adoption, but the actual volume growth is in B2B treasury, supplier payments, and cross-border settlement. Companies are using stablecoin rails not because they want crypto exposure but because the settlement is faster and cheaper than correspondent banking — particularly for emerging-market corridors where the receiving bank is small or sanctioned-adjacent.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 002 surfaced Zynta as a B2B stablecoin last-mile infra play. Issue 003 connected the "Africa's deeply local" thesis to locally-native primitives. Rubinstein's framing fits into both — the African operators building stablecoin payment rails are not consumer-crypto stories, they're settlement-infrastructure plays solving for a correspondent-banking deficit.

"Stablecoins are winning at the boring layer. The settlement layer. The layer where consumers don't see them — but volume compounds." — Marc Rubinstein, paraphrased

The piece is the kind of structural framing you've consistently up-weighted: a non-obvious thesis with implications for how to read both the existing portfolio (Crossfin's merchant rail) and adjacent opportunities (any African fintech embedding a stablecoin settlement layer).

Africa Bitcoin Corporation upgrades JSE listing to Main Board

TechCabal · This week

Africa Bitcoin Corporation's move to the JSE Main Board takes effect Friday, May 22 — a multi-exchange expansion across Africa intended to bring Bitcoin exposure to institutional investors via publicly-traded stock. The shift signals two things: South African institutional allocators are now willing to underwrite Bitcoin via a regulated equity wrapper, and African operators are positioning to be the regional channel for that exposure rather than ceding the trade to US ETFs.

Read at techcabal.com

The interesting part isn't Bitcoin. It's the regulatory and allocator behaviour. Two years ago the JSE wouldn't have accepted a Bitcoin-exposure equity for Main Board listing; today it does. The local pension funds and asset managers couldn't put a US Bitcoin ETF in their portfolios because of FX and regulatory friction; they can now buy this.

The longitudinal thread: Issue 003's "Africa's deeply local" thesis argued that the next billion-dollar African companies will be built around structurally-different primitives rather than adapted Western templates. Africa Bitcoin Corp is a different example of the same pattern — a locally-listed wrapper for global-asset exposure, structured for South African institutional constraints, that has no direct US equivalent.

"Local wrappers for global assets, structured for local constraints. The category didn't exist on the JSE a year ago." — observation

No portfolio pull-through. The piece is here because it's a credible signal that South African institutional allocators are now willing to consider new asset categories within a regulated equity wrapper. That changes what's underwriteable for any new portfolio company contemplating a JSE secondary route.

US natural gas supply heads for record summer at 117 bcf/day

Energy briefings · This week

EIA and industry forecasts converge on a US natural gas supply record of 117 billion cubic feet per day this summer — driven by LNG exports, data-centre load growth, and accelerating coal retirements that have pushed power generation toward gas as base-load alternative. The composite picture: US gas is now structural infrastructure for both the AI-compute build-out and the global LNG-export market.

Read at eia.gov

Three drivers in one number. Coal retirements have removed roughly 10 GW of US baseload in 2025-2026, with gas absorbing most of the displaced demand. LNG export capacity grew 0.9 bcf/day in April alone (Golden Pass Train 1, Corpus Christi Stage 3). And data-centre load growth — even after the 20 cancellations Heatmap covered yesterday — is still net-additive at the system level.

5-Bullet Friday — five things worth your weekend reading time

Tim Ferriss · 5-Bullet Friday · Fri 15 May 2026

Tim Ferriss's signature Friday compilation. This week's five: (i) Tyler Cowen × Ezra Klein on whether AI labs misread their enterprise customers; (ii) a long essay on why deep work is harder in 2026 than 2018 (the meeting culture, not the tooling); (iii) a Stanford paper on "agentic conviction" as a partner-selection variable in VC; (iv) a book recommendation — "The Bonfire of Modernity" — on institutional fragility; (v) a Bryan Johnson interview clip on biological-age testing as a forcing function.

Read at tim.blog

Bullet one is the one to read. Cowen and Klein argue that the "deployment is the bottleneck" frame (which has run through this week's dispatch) may be the labs' own narrative more than reality. Their alternative: enterprise buyers don't want operating-model rebuilds; they want point-solution tools that slot into existing workflows without restructure. If they're right, the agentic-workflows thesis is correct in the limit but wrong in the time horizon.

Bullet three — "agentic conviction" from a Stanford working paper — argues that the best venture partners are distinguished by an unusual willingness to act on weakly-evidenced theses early. The data is from a 30-year cohort study of US VC partner returns. The implication, if it holds, is that pattern-matching from base rates is the wrong frame; conviction-and-position-size is the right one. Worth a partner conversation.

"The best partners are not the most accurate. They are the most willing to act on weakly-evidenced theses." — Stanford working paper, paraphrased by Ferriss

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Use Claude as a sparring partner — "argue against this"

The default partner habit is to ask Claude to help build the case. Flip it. After you've drafted a thesis, a pitch, or an investment memo, paste it back to Claude and ask: "Argue against this. What's the strongest counter-argument? What evidence would make me wrong?" Claude is good at adversarial reasoning when asked explicitly. You'll catch the weaknesses you missed.

Try this on your next IC memo

"Here is my IC paper on [portco]. Argue the bear case against the investment. Find the three assumptions most likely to break. For each, tell me what evidence would invalidate the thesis. Don't be polite."
