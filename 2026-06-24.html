Africa · Capital · Exits Chart
Across 22 editions of its “Ask an Investor” column in H1 2026, TechCabal found every conversation converging on one question: can invested money come back? The structural picture behind it has shifted hard. African investors now write nearly 40% of the cheques on the continent, up from 25%, while foreign money has roughly halved from about $5bn in 2022 to $2.3bn. Development-finance institutions — still 70–75% of the ecosystem's capital — fell to just 27% of commitments in 2025, and Africa-focused fund managers raised only $107m across six final closes, an 87% drop by value year-on-year. Launch Africa's answer to the exit problem: it returned $2.5m to LPs (about 7% of paid-in capital) after 11 exits, eight of them secondaries, none below 1x, the best at 5x — and built a dedicated head-of-exits role most African firms don't have.

Twenty-two investors, one verdict: exits are now the only thing that matters — and the capital base is being rebuilt to force them

TechCabal · Ask An Investor · Muktar Oladunmade · Mon 22 Jun 2026

Three reads. One — liquidity, not valuation, is the new discipline. Ido Sum's framing (TLcom, 14 years) is that African venture isn't broken, it's early — at the stage the US was in the 1970s, where stepping-stone M&A in the tens then low hundreds of millions builds trust before any billion-dollar outcome. Two — local capital now goes first, not second. “Africa must fund Africa”: the AFC, a $19bn DFI that funds ports and cables, made its first-ever VC commitment ($40m anchoring Future Africa and LightRock). Three — debt is having its moment because equity's limits finally showed: credit recycles faster, prices currency risk explicitly, and stops the over-dilution that compounds when everything is funded with equity.

Local capital is rising as foreign and DFI money retreats · the exit, not the round, is now the scarce event
TechCabal — “What 22 investors taught us so far in 2026: exits are the only thing that matters”

This is the cleanest distillation we have seen of the question Chronos lives inside: not whether African businesses can grow, but whether the capital structure around them can convert that growth into realised returns. The column's running theme — can the money come back? — is the only question that ultimately survives a cycle. And the half-year's data says the people best placed to answer it are increasingly local: African investors at ~40% of funding, foreign money halved, DFIs retreating from the marginal cheque even as they remain the load-bearing wall.

“Every time founders raise at a higher valuation, they shrink the pool of people who can buy them. The exit is not something you wait for — it is something you design for, from day one.”

Hold this against Tuesday's Nigeria-banks chart. The retail-credit gap is real and vast; 505 digital lenders have rushed into it. But this column is the cold corrective: a licensed lender disbursing at scale is not a return. The return is a buyer — a strategic, a secondary fund, an in-continent consolidator — and the whole ecosystem is now reorganising to manufacture that buyer. Launch Africa returning capital in year five rather than year ten, and hiring a head of exits, is the operational expression of the same insight: in this market liquidity has to be worked for, not waited for.

The thread to hold forward: the rise of credit (AHL leaving a decade of equity for private debt; BFA thinking like a fixed-income investor) is the same signal read from the asset side. When sophisticated allocators move toward instruments that self-liquidate — debt that recycles, secondaries that clear — they are telling you the binding constraint is no longer access to deals but conversion of paper into cash. For a Chronos screen, the question to put to any African growth-equity opportunity is now blunt: name the three plausible buyers and the multiple they have actually paid before. If that sentence can't be finished, the gap-closing thesis is incomplete.

Capital · Structure · Power Structural
In a Next Wave essay, TechCabal's Kenn Abuya argues that the three-year founder obsession with dilution has obscured the real question. The most sophisticated capital providers are becoming less interested in owning startups and more interested in owning the ecosystems, revenue streams and commercial relationships those startups create. Google's equity-free African accelerator is the soft version — it takes no shares because every startup that scales on Google Cloud, ships on Android or buys ad inventory generates value without a seat on the cap table. The hard version is a roughly $3-trillion private-credit market whose growth, Abuya contends, has not lowered the cost of capital so much as given investors new ways to capture startup upside without ownership: revenue-based financing, royalties, venture debt with warrants. “Non-dilutive” describes where the bill lands, not whether there is one.

The myth of founder-friendly capital — equity dilution is visible the day a deal closes; everything else hides in the cash flows

TechCabal · The Next Wave · Kenn Abuya · Sun 21 Jun 2026

The argument that should sit in every term-sheet discussion. Giving up 20% of a company feels expensive because the sacrifice is immediate; committing 5% of future revenue feels modest — even when the successful company ultimately surrenders more economic value through the latter. Stack the instruments — revenue-based financing beside venture debt beside factoring beside a bank facility — and the balance sheet fills with competing claims that collide exactly when the company needs flexibility to raise again. The real question is not whether founders surrender equity, but whether they surrender future economic value through means that don't show up as dilution. “They have not disappeared,” Abuya writes of the costs. “They have become harder to see.”

TechCabal — “The Next Wave: The myth of founder-friendly capital”

This is the conceptual backbone under today's lead. Where the 22-investor column gives us the empirics of a capital base reorganising around liquidity, this essay names the mechanism: capital is unbundling ownership from economics. The two used to travel together — you bought equity, you bore the risk, you took the upside. The new architecture lets the provider keep the upside and hand the risk back to the company, through structures that read as “founder-friendly” precisely because their cost is deferred and diffuse.

“Founders who preserve every percentage point of their cap table may face a harder question: how much of the company's future did they preserve along with it?”

For an allocator this cuts two ways, and both matter to Chronos. On the buy side, it is a reminder to read the whole capital stack of any target, not just the equity register — a clean cap table sitting on a thicket of revenue-share, royalty and warrant obligations is not the asset it appears to be, and those prior claims are exactly what make a company hard to back, or to exit (the thread straight back to today's lead). On the platform side, it is a strategic prompt: the highest-return position is often not equity at all but ownership of the rail, the distribution, the data exhaust — the Google move. Tuesday, Okosi told us compute, capital and people are the bottleneck; today his own employer demonstrates that whoever supplies the rail through the bottleneck captures the value, no shares required.

The forward thread: watch how Africa's emerging private-credit and revenue-based lenders price these instruments as the asset class matures. If the “hidden” costs stay hidden, founders over-distribute their future and the next equity round stalls; if the market learns to price total economic surrender rather than headline dilution, the discipline the 22 investors are asking for arrives from the financing side too.

Africa · Mobility · Strategy Tests a thesis
In a long interview on the sidelines of the Africa CEO Forum in Kigali, Yango Group's CEO for Africa, Adeniyi Adebayo, lays out the thinking behind a company most still call a ride-hailing app. The Dubai-headquartered group says it has completed 340 million rides, runs 500,000+ drivers, operates in 35 markets and has built 70+ product lines — and on 18 May formally launched Yango Tech, a B2B/B2G arm packaging AI consulting, smart-city infrastructure, healthcare digitisation and payments for businesses and governments, off the back of $150m+ invested in proprietary mapping, routing, payments and data tools. Its market unit is the city, not the country (Abidjan, 6.3m people, sits behind ~60% of Côte d'Ivoire's GDP via its port). Its edge it calls “perception arbitrage” — received wisdom about African markets lags reality by years, so it entered Côte d'Ivoire, Ethiopia and tier-two cities like Bouaké before the consensus arrived.

Ride-hailing was just the door — Yango is selling the city's operating stack it was forced to build for itself

TechCabal · Features · Ganiu Oloruntade · Tue 23 Jun 2026

A clinic in two ideas worth stealing. The city as the unit of analysis. An Abidjan resident's consumption behaviour is closer to Dubai's than to rural Côte d'Ivoire's; Yango's playbook is to build density and profitability in the densest commercial node, then use that pool to subsidise the long tail — “if you don't build a profitable business in Lusaka, you can't build one for the Copperbelt.” Regulatory knowledge as a compounding asset. Having co-designed (with PwC and EY) a self-employed tax regime in Côte d'Ivoire where it collects ~3–4% of driver income for the state, Yango carries that template into Senegal and beyond, shortening “time to market for regulation.” Francophone Africa still draws only ~10% of venture funding (down from 15%); the Big Four take 67% — the arbitrage is structural.

TechCabal — “Ride-hailing was just the entry point. Yango had bigger plans.”

Read Yango next to M-KOPA from Tuesday and a pattern hardens into a doctrine: the most defensible products in frontier markets are the internal tools you were forced to build because nothing off the shelf existed. M-KOPA had to build a credit model to sell phones; Yango had to build mapping, routing, payments and a driver-tax rail to run cars. Both then discovered the by-product was the business. Yango Tech is that realisation productised — eight years of regulatory relationships and proprietary city-level data, sold to the governments that lack it.

“Our game is perception arbitrage. If I tell you the cafeteria is closed, you don't double-check me. People hold stories about African markets that lag reality by years.”

For Chronos two things are worth underlining. First, the city-as-unit lens is directly transferable to how we frame African market sizing — national GDP-per-capita systematically understates the addressable wallet in the one or two cities where the economy actually concentrates, and a thesis priced off the national average will misjudge both demand and willingness to pay. Second, the moat here is not capital and not technology in the abstract; it is accumulated regulatory know-how that compounds across borders — the one asset a better-funded competitor cannot simply buy. That is the same insight as today's capital essay seen from the operating side: the durable position is ownership of the relationship and the rail, not the headline product.

The caution rides alongside. Yango is a foreign-rooted operator (Dubai-HQ'd, with a data centre in Serbia) executing a roll-up of African urban infrastructure — a structure that, like Spiro's China ties on Tuesday, trades faster scale today for questions about who ultimately owns the continent's digital plumbing tomorrow. The B2G market it is entering is crowded and politically exposed. But the thesis is coherent, evidenced and unusually honest about its own trade-offs — exactly the kind of operator-logic worth pressure-testing against our own portfolio's expansion plans.

Africa · Fintech · Regulatory Hyper-local
Yellow Card, the stablecoin-infrastructure provider operating across Africa and other emerging markets, has secured an anti-money-laundering (AML) regulatory affiliation in Switzerland and is establishing a permanent base in Lugano — a city that has issued four blockchain bonds (a CHF100m / $123m issuance in 2025) and partnered with Tether under its “Plan B” initiative. The Swiss subsidiary gives institutional and corporate clients a regulated, supervised counterparty to move capital into emerging markets using stablecoins. The approval lands seven months after Yellow Card shut its retail trading app to focus entirely on B2B infrastructure, and follows enterprise partnerships with Visa, Mastercard, Western Union, Thunes and MoneyGram. Founded in 2016, the company now operates across 50+ emerging markets and holds licences including a VASP authorisation in Botswana.

An African stablecoin rail walks into a Swiss vault — Yellow Card builds the regulated on-ramp from Geneva to the frontier

TechCabal · Cryptocurrency · Opeyemi Kareem · Tue 23 Jun 2026

The corridor, reversed. The familiar story is foreign capital reaching into Africa; here an Africa-born operator acquires the developed-market supervision that lets global institutions reach the frontier through its rails. CEO Chris Maurice's framing is pure infrastructure: “Stablecoins have become critical infrastructure for global institutions, and compliant access to the rails is a requirement.” The Swiss entity, led by a French lawyer on Yellow Card's senior team, is the regulated front door to a settlement network spanning Africa, Latin America, the US and other emerging markets. The strategic tell is the retail shutdown seven months ago: Yellow Card decided the durable business is being the supervised plumbing between institutions and emerging markets, not a consumer exchange.

TechCabal — “Yellow Card expands global stablecoin network with Swiss regulatory approval”

Set this beside Tuesday's Namibia–Starlink item and the symmetry is the point. There, a small African state held the ownership line against the most valuable connectivity company on earth, insisting the rail over its territory be locally controlled. Here, an African fintech crosses the other way — into one of the world's strictest regulatory jurisdictions — not to be controlled but to acquire the badge of supervision that makes its rail usable by institutions that would never touch an unregulated emerging-market exchange. Sovereignty over infrastructure is leverage in both directions, and the African operator is now sometimes the one doing the crossing.

“Switzerland holds financial intermediaries to one of the highest regulatory standards in the world. Combined with our licensed network, this gives partners a rare combination of regulatory confidence and real operational reach.”

For Chronos this is a clean read on where stablecoin value is actually accruing — not in tokens or consumer apps but in regulated cross-border B2B settlement and treasury, exactly the layer Visa called “pretty big” for Africa last month. Yellow Card's deliberate retreat from retail to become supervised institutional plumbing is the same ownership-of-the-rail logic running through today's edition: the money is in being the compliant pipe, not the flashy product on top. The Lugano choice also signals how regulatory geography is becoming a competitive asset — a jurisdiction can manufacture a blockchain hub the way it once manufactured a banking-secrecy one.

The thread to hold: watch whether African payment and remittance operators increasingly seek developed-market licences as a moat — a credential that lets them intermediate the inbound institutional flows our portfolio's payments names (and our own LP base) ultimately ride. The reverse corridor is the story to track.

South Africa · Web3 · Sport Chart
As Bafana Bafana play out their FIFA World Cup group stage, the South African Football Association (SAFA) is running a parallel experiment off the pitch: a blockchain-based $SAFA fan token, launched in May on Socios.com and built on the Chiliz Chain, that tries to turn supporters into participants in a year-round digital economy. Holders vote in polls, unlock rewards and matchday experiences, redeem merchandise and can trade the token, whose price floats with sentiment. Early numbers, per Chiliz: 840,000 $SAFA tokens taken up, nearly 248,000 users participating in polls, 37,000+ redeeming rewards. Globally, Socios says it has returned $700m+ to sports bodies across 170+ partnerships since 2018, on a revenue-share model. Supporting the rollout is Bitexen South Africa, repositioning from crypto exchange to tokenisation-infrastructure provider.

A football federation tries to turn a crowd into an asset class — SAFA's fan token tests whether community is the balance sheet

TechCabal · Cryptocurrency · Phathisani Moyo · Tue 23 Jun 2026

The pitch is a genuinely interesting reframe of where value sits. Bitexen SA's CEO Mark Diuga: “The most valuable asset a club owns may not be its stadium, players, or broadcast rights. It may be the community that has formed around it.” SAFA is betting supporters are worth more than TV audiences and matchday tickets — that an engaged crowd can be a recurring, monetisable digital relationship rather than a once-a-season transaction. The model is unproven and contested: prices swing, critics call the tokens speculative, and Chiliz insists they are “utility” instruments, not investments. No revenue figure has been disclosed. But as a structural question — can you securitise belonging? — it is exactly the kind of experiment worth watching from a distance.

Engagement narrows fast down the funnel — the open question is whether participation, not speculation, sustains it
TechCabal — “South Africa's blockchain fan token is testing a new business model for African football”

Strip away the crypto packaging and this is a question about the unit of ownership — the same question running through today's edition. Yango wants to own the city's stack; Yellow Card wants to own the regulated rail; SAFA, more speculatively, wants to own the economic relationship with a crowd that currently expresses itself only as TV ratings and ticket stubs. The interesting claim is not the token mechanics but the assertion underneath: that a community is a strategic asset most rights-holders fail to treat as one.

“The most valuable asset a club owns may not be its stadium, players, or broadcast rights. It may be the community that has formed around it.”

We file this under watch-don't-touch. The evidence is thin — mixed global results, sharp price volatility, and a real risk that a “utility” token behaves like a speculative one and damages the very community it monetises, which would be a reputational own-goal for a national federation. There is no disclosed economics. But the structural prompt is legitimate and portable: in a continent where consumer wallets are shallow but engagement is deep, durable monetisation may come from converting recurring participation into a relationship, not from extracting more per transaction. That is the same logic our portfolio's consumer-facing names are circling.

The thread to hold: whether SAFA can convert World Cup passion into year-round, paid participation is the real test — engagement that survives the final whistle. If it does, the “community as asset” thesis graduates from sport to any business sitting on a loyal, identifiable base. If it doesn't, it joins the long list of tokenisation experiments that confused a crowd for a customer book.

South Africa · Capital Allocation · Companies For the portfolio
Prosus — the Amsterdam-listed internet group majority-owned by South Africa's Naspers, and for years valued chiefly through its early stake in Tencent — said in a trading statement ahead of annual results that, for the first time, every one of its operating ecosystems is profitable. For the year to 31 March 2026 it generated $7.3bn in revenue and $1.1bn in ecosystem adjusted EBITDA, with core headline EPS expected up 19–28%. Management says it has “completed our transformation from a traditional holding company into an active operator of AI-driven lifestyle ecosystems” across Latin America, Europe and India — food delivery (iFood), payments (PayU), classifieds and e-commerce. Free cash flow excluding Tencent continues to grow. The caveat is intact: earnings still flatter on Tencent, and FX losses on euro bonds bit.

Prosus says it is no longer just a Tencent story — every operating ecosystem is finally profitable, and the discount thesis gets a test

TechCabal · Companies · Phathisani Moyo · Fri 19 Jun 2026

For any South African allocator this is a bellwether, not a footnote. Prosus/Naspers has been the canonical “holding-company discount” case — a portfolio of growth-for-growth's-sake operating businesses whose value the market refused to credit because Tencent did all the heavy lifting. The claim that all ecosystems are now cash-generative is the inflection the bulls have waited a decade for. Nitrogen Fund Managers' Rowan Williams: “This is probably the first time all of the ecosystem assets are cash-flow positive and generating a profit — that should help Prosus become increasingly independent and less reliant on Tencent's cash flows.” The repositioning from “holding company” to “active operator of AI-driven lifestyle ecosystems” is also a deliberate narrative move to close the discount.

TechCabal — “Prosus says it is no longer just a Tencent story”

This closes today's loop neatly. The edition opened on a capital base reorganising to force liquidity and an essay on capital unbundling ownership from economics; it ends with the continent's most-watched listed investor demonstrating the operator's side of the same trade — that the value the market wouldn't pay for was always in whether the operating businesses could stand on their own cash flows rather than on a single moonshot stake. “Every ecosystem profitable” is the operating analogue of “name the buyer”: it is the moment a portfolio stops being a story about one asset and becomes a book that earns.

“This is probably the first time all of the ecosystem assets are cash-flow positive and generating a profit. That should help Prosus become increasingly independent of Tencent.”

For Chronos the relevance is double. First, as a read on the holding-company discount that shadows any diversified investment vehicle — including the structures we and our LPs operate within: the market pays for demonstrated, independent cash generation, not for narrative breadth, and the path to re-rating runs through proving each underlying ecosystem earns on its own. Second, as a live AI-execution test of exactly the kind our Spock framework is built to score — Prosus is explicitly recasting itself as an operator of “AI-driven” ecosystems, and the next few results will show whether that is genuine margin and feedback-loop improvement or a relabelling exercise. The honest caveat in the piece — earnings still partly flattered by Tencent, FX losses on euro bonds — is the right note of caution to carry.

The thread to hold forward: track whether the Prosus discount actually narrows on this. If a listed operator with full disclosure can convert “all ecosystems profitable” into a re-rating, it strengthens the case that disciplined, cash-generative operating performance — not multiple expansion — is what closes value gaps in this cycle. If the discount persists despite the numbers, that itself is information about how much the market now trusts the “active operator” story.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Save your best prompts — build a reusable template library, stop re-typing the same instructions

Today's lead asked you to interrogate a capital structure; another item asked you to size a market by city rather than country. If you found a prompt that produced a genuinely useful answer — the framing, the constraints, the output shape — the worst thing you can do is type it once and lose it. The partners who get the most out of these models treat strong prompts the way an analyst treats a good Excel template: save it, name it, reuse it. A “name the three plausible buyers and the multiple each has actually paid” prompt, or a “read this whole capital stack, not just the equity register” prompt, becomes a repeatable instrument the moment you keep it somewhere. Over a few weeks you accumulate a private library that turns one good result into a hundred.

A worked example

When a prompt works, immediately ask the model: “Turn the instructions I just gave you into a reusable template. Replace the specifics with clearly labelled placeholders in [BRACKETS], keep the structure and the constraints that made the answer good, and add a one-line note on when to use it.” Save the result in a single note or doc — “Deal screen: name-the-buyer,” “Capital-stack read,” “City-level market sizing.” Next time, paste the template, fill the brackets, and you start from your own best thinking instead of a blank box. The compounding is the point: your prompt library is the most underrated asset you'll build this year.
