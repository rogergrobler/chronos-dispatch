South Africa · Banking · AI Deployment Analysis Chart of the day
Standard Bank, Africa's largest lender by assets, used its H1 2026 results (published Thursday) to make an explicit claim on the continent's payment flows — and disclosed unusually specific numbers on the machinery behind it. Domestic payment values rose 11% and cross-border values 7%; the bank holds a 30% share of South Africa's cross-border payments and 19% across Africa, making it the continent's largest transactional franchise by payment value. Africa Regions contributed R10.4bn ($644.4m), or 40% of group headline earnings. Underneath: 72% of employees were active generative-AI users by June, 87 AI use cases approved group-wide, AI-enabled recommendation systems drove 10m+ personalised client interactions in six months, and 78% of computing infrastructure has moved to cloud. Standard Bank was ranked the leading bank in Africa in the inaugural Evident AI Index for Banks — Middle East & Africa (June). Group headline earnings rose 10% to R26.1bn ($1.6bn), ROE improved to 19.8%, on 19.5m active clients. It put fresh capital into Standard Bank Tanzania in July and plans to lift its stake in Standard Bank Angola in H2. A Mastercard-commissioned Genesis Analytics report projects Africa's payments market reaching $1.5trn by 2030.

Africa's biggest bank rebuilt its own plumbing before going after the payment flows — 72% of staff on gen-AI, 78% of compute in the cloud

TechCabal · Banking · Phathisani Moyo · Thu 13 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a solid interim result from a large bank. The one that matters is an incumbent doing the expensive, unglamorous internal rebuild first, and only then reaching for the flows — cloud migration, an approved AI use-case register, and firm-wide tool adoption are the sort of disclosures banks usually keep for investor days, and putting them in a results release is a statement of where management thinks the moat now sits. It threads directly from Issue 064's Interpol item, where the gap was institutions unable to use the tool: here is the institution that can. The honest caveat is that the internal bars are still running ahead of the revenue ones.

The capability is built and the flows are growing — but the internal bars are still running well ahead of the revenue ones
TechCabal — “Standard Bank wants to own more of Africa's digital payment flows”

The structural read is that Standard Bank has answered the build-or-rent question by building, and has chosen to say so in a results release rather than a conference keynote. Almost every large African financial institution now says it is “AI-enabled”; very few will put a number on what fraction of their people actually open the tool on a Tuesday. Seventy-two per cent adoption alongside eighty-seven governed use cases is a materially different claim from a pilot programme — it says the bank has solved the two problems that kill most enterprise AI efforts, which are distribution to ordinary staff and a governance path that lets a use case reach production. The 78% cloud migration is the precondition that makes the rest possible, and it is the part that took years and cost real money. Read alongside the payments position — a 30% grip on South African cross-border value, 19% Africa-wide — the strategy resolves into something coherent: own the rail, then use the capability layer to widen the take from every transaction that crosses it, through merchant acquiring, cross-border fee income and value-added services rather than net interest margin alone.

Interpol's picture on Friday was of institutions unable to pick the tool up. Here is the institution that did — and the disclosure gap between them is now the single clearest read on which African incumbents will still be compounding in 2030.

The threads: to Issue 064's item 3 (Interpol's AI-crime gap) — that piece measured the defenders' incapacity at 8% of analysts; this one measures a defender's capacity at 72% of staff, and the two together say the African AI gap is not continental but institutional, running between well-capitalised private balance sheets and under-funded public ones. To Issue 064's item 4 (KCB) and to today's item 2 (E-finance) — three financial franchises all finding growth one layer out from the home market, whether by geography, by adjacency or by acquisition. To Issue 063's item 3 (SARB takes the payment rail) — the regulator drew the perimeter around the rail a week ago, and this is the largest private operator inside that perimeter arguing it can still capture the economics; how those two claims coexist is the live question for every South African payments business. To today's item 3 — note that Standard Bank appears on the other side of the funding table too, co-backing Bridgement's $20.3m round, which is what a mature balance sheet looks like when it decides to buy optionality on AI-native lending rather than build it. To the Chronos read on incumbency and AI transformation — this is the closest thing to a control case the continent offers: a legacy institution with the capital to do the rebuild properly, and now enough disclosure to be marked against it. The sceptic's ledger: adoption is not productivity, and “active user” is a soft metric that a mandated tool rollout can inflate without a rand of cost taken out; 87 approved use cases says nothing about how many are material, and none of the disclosed numbers connect AI spend to the 19.8% ROE; the payments growth of 11% and 7% is respectable but hardly the signature of a franchise being transformed, and could as easily be nominal-GDP drift; the 40% Africa Regions earnings contribution carries the FX and country risk that a rand-reported figure smooths away; and the Evident ranking is a relative measure against a field that is not setting a high bar. The forward marker: the FY 2026 print — whether cost-to-income actually falls, because that is the only place an AI programme this size can legitimately show up, and if it does not, the internal bars stay a very expensive dashboard.

Egypt · Fintech · M&A Briefing Structural design
E-finance, the Egyptian-listed fintech that builds and runs much of Egypt's digital government and payment infrastructure, is acquiring 100% of Tamweely Financial Services in a deal valued at up to EGP 4.8bn ($96m). Tamweely, launched in 2017, is a significant player in Egypt's non-banking financial services sector, specialising in microfinance and microinsurance for small, medium and micro enterprises, with over 230 branches and 183,000 active borrowers — implying roughly $522 per active customer. The logic is vertical integration: E-finance already processes the payments and tax filings of the same small businesses, and can now extend credit directly to them, capturing the value chain from the moment a business pays its taxes to the moment it needs working capital to expand.

Egypt's payment-infrastructure incumbent pays $96m to stop being a utility — buying 183,000 borrowers to own the credit layer above its own rails

TechCabal Daily · Emmanuel Nwosu & Zia Yusuf · Fri 14 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a mid-size North African acquisition. The one that matters is an infrastructure operator concluding that owning the pipes is no longer where the margin lives — as digital payments commoditise, the economics migrate up the stack into credit and insurance, and the fastest route there is to buy a distribution network rather than originate one. It is the same conclusion Standard Bank reaches in item 1 by a different route: build the capability, then reach for the adjacent layer. The tell is the price per borrower — $522 for a customer already visible on your own payment rails is a bet that the data you hold makes them cheaper to underwrite than they were for Tamweely.

TechCabal Daily — “E-finance makes a $96 million bet on Egypt's micro-lenders”

The structural read is that this is a commoditisation trade dressed as an expansion. Payment processing in a maturing market converges on a thin, regulated, volume-driven margin — the utility outcome. The escape is either to become the only utility, or to move to the layer where risk is priced, because risk-pricing is where the spread is and where proprietary data actually confers an advantage. E-finance is choosing the second, and the reason it can pay $522 a borrower is that it plausibly already knows more about those businesses' cash flows than the lender it is buying does: it sees the tax filings and the receipts. That is a genuine underwriting edge if the data can legally be joined, and a slide if it cannot. The 230-branch network is the other half of the purchase — microfinance in Egypt is still a feet-on-the-street business, and the branches are what E-finance could not have built quickly at any reasonable price.

Buying the lender is the honest admission that the rail alone was never going to earn enough. The interesting question is not whether the credit layer pays better — it does — but whether a company built to process transactions can learn to price default.

The threads: to today's item 1 (Standard Bank) — the same strategic conclusion reached from opposite starting points: a bank building the technology layer to defend its flows, an infrastructure company buying the balance-sheet layer to escape thin ones, both converging on the view that neither rails nor licences alone are a business any more. To Issue 064's item 4 (KCB) — a third variation on the theme, growing earnings by moving into adjacent markets rather than deeper into the home one. To Issue 063's item 5 and Issue 062's rail thesis — “value migrates to whoever controls the rail” gets its first serious counter-example here, because E-finance controls a rail and has just concluded that it needs to own something else as well. To Issue 059 (Moment) and Issue 057 (Vodacom's fintech pivot) — the pattern of payment operators reaching for financial-services revenue is now visible in North, East and Southern Africa in the space of eight editions. To the Chronos read on vertical integration and adjacency — the diligence question on any payments asset is now explicitly “what is the second revenue layer, and can this team execute it?” The sceptic's ledger: transaction processors have a long history of buying lenders and discovering that credit risk is a different discipline with a different cost of failure, and E-finance has no demonstrated underwriting track record; EGP 4.8bn is an “up to” figure, which usually means earn-outs and therefore an unresolved argument about value; microfinance books in emerging markets look excellent until the cycle turns and 183,000 small borrowers reprice at once; Egyptian macro and currency volatility sit directly under this asset; and the data-joining advantage that justifies the price may run into exactly the localisation and privacy rules tightening across the continent. The forward marker: the first post-acquisition disclosure of Tamweely's non-performing loan ratio and cost of risk — that single number will tell you whether E-finance bought a distribution network or a credit problem.

Pan-Africa · Capital Markets · Data Analysis Chart of the day
African startups raised $224m across 37 tracked deals in July 2026 (28 disclosed), down from June's $334m, taking the year to $1.66bn after a $1.44bn first half. The composition is the story: debt accounted for 75.2% ($168.55m), equity 24.8% ($55.51m), and grants under 0.1% ($95,000). The largest cheques went to physical infrastructure — Gotion Power Morocco took a $114m African Development Bank loan to build Africa's first lithium-iron-phosphate battery gigafactory; M-Kopa took $30m in debt from Dutch development bank FMO for EV and smartphone asset finance; South Africa's Bridgement, an AI-powered SME lender, raised $20.3m backed by RMB and Standard Bank. Others: BioLite $10.7m, CrossBoundary Energy $10m from the IFC, South African AI customer-service platform Cue $5m from Knife Capital, Peach Cars $4m, Fuzu $3.86m. Consolidation continued with 13 M&A deals in July, after 63 in H1 — including Pepkor's $1.29bn (R21.3bn) Flash/Shop2Shop merger and Vodacom's $2.1bn purchase of 20% of Safaricom.

Three-quarters of July's African capital came as debt — founders are borrowing to build gigafactories rather than selling equity to promise them

TC Insights · Joseph Oloyede · Mon 10 Aug 2026 (updated Fri 14 Aug)

Two lines of read: the surface story is a soft funding month. The one that matters is a structural change in what African capital is for — a 75/25 debt-to-equity split is not a venture ecosystem having a bad month, it is a different asset class arriving. Debt requires collateral and cash flow rather than a narrative, which is why the money is going into batteries, fleets and solar rather than software. It is Issue 064's proof-over-promise thesis expressed as a capital structure, and it resolves Issue 062's funding-squeeze question in an unexpected way: the money did not leave, it changed instrument and changed destination.

Three-quarters of a month's African capital arrived as debt — the ownership question has already been settled quietly
TC Insights — “$224 million raised in July 2026, Electric Vehicles dominate”

The structural read is that two separate things are being measured under one heading, and the aggregate number hides both. There is a venture market, which is small, shrinking in deal count and concentrating on fewer names; and there is a project-finance market for African physical infrastructure, which is growing, is largely funded by development finance institutions, and has almost nothing to do with venture capital except that the recipients happen to be young companies. Gotion's $114m from the AfDB is a gigafactory loan wearing a startup label. Once separated, July's equity number is roughly $55m across the continent — a genuinely thin month — while the infrastructure line is healthy and structurally durable, because a battery plant and a solar portfolio produce the contracted cash flows that service debt and an app usually does not. The second-order point matters more for allocators: debt does not dilute, but it does impose a fixed charge on businesses operating in currencies that move against the loan. A wave of hard-currency development-bank debt into local-revenue African businesses is a bet on FX stability that nobody is explicitly underwriting.

A 75/25 debt split is not a venture market having an off month. It is project finance arriving in a category that still reports itself as startup funding — and the two should stop being counted together.

The threads: to Issue 064's item 1 (Jumia's IFC-led equity round) — the same development-finance institutions appear on both sides of this ledger, writing equity where a path to profit is demonstrable and debt where an asset can be pledged, which is a more disciplined posture than the venture market showed for a decade. To Issue 062's funding-squeeze piece — that thesis is now answered: capital did not withdraw, it repriced and moved instrument, so “African funding is down” is the wrong sentence and “African equity risk appetite is down while asset-backed lending is up” is the right one. To Issue 061 (Spiro's Rwanda-versus-Nigeria fleet gap) — that piece argued policy and infrastructure beat population; the July numbers show capital agreeing, funding Morocco's gigafactory and Kenyan asset finance rather than the largest addressable market. To today's item 1 — Standard Bank sits inside this table as a backer of Bridgement, which is a bank buying exposure to AI-native SME lending rather than building it, and worth reading against its own internal-build story. To the Chronos read on capital structure and entry points — the debt-heavy mix is precisely the condition in which patient equity is scarce and therefore best priced. The sceptic's ledger: single-month funding data is noisy and one $114m loan swings the entire mix, so the 75/25 split may not survive August; “tracked deals” systematically under-counts undisclosed rounds, and 9 of 37 July deals withheld numbers; classifying a sovereign-adjacent AfDB gigafactory loan as startup funding inflates the headline in a way that flatters the ecosystem; and debt-funded infrastructure in weak-currency markets carries a servicing risk that the celebratory framing skips entirely. The forward marker: whether the equity line recovers above $100m a month in Q4 — if it does not, the African “startup” category has quietly become an infrastructure asset class, and should be diligenced as one.

South Africa · Sovereign Capability · Deeptech Briefing Wouldn't have seen it
The University of KwaZulu-Natal's Aerospace Systems Research Institute (ASRI) is targeting a suborbital, space-capable rocket in 2028 and South Africa's first orbital satellite launch from home soil in 2030, using its own Saffire liquid-fuel engine (South African First Integrated Rocket Engine, designed for 27.6kN of thrust) and a locally developed two-stage commercial launch vehicle sized comparably to Rocket Lab's Electron. The target payload is a 200kg satellite to roughly 500km — the class used for Earth observation, weather, mapping, agriculture and communications. This is not vapour: an ablatively cooled, pressure-fed demonstrator called Able, producing 18kN on liquid oxygen and Jet A-1, has been hot-fired across three campaigns, and ASRI's Phoenix sounding rockets have flown repeatedly from the Denel Overberg Test Range on the Western Cape coast, where a permanent launch gantry was commissioned in December 2024. The gap is arithmetic: the full programme is costed at about R3bn ($186m) against a current government allocation of R100m ($6.2m) for 2026 and 2027, and the core engineering team must grow from roughly 20 to more than 120.

South Africa has hot-fired the engine and built the gantry — it is R2.9bn and a hundred engineers short of the orbit

TechCentral · via TechCabal Daily · Fri 14 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is another national space announcement. The one that matters is a country deciding whether sovereign capability is something you negotiate for or something you build — and discovering that the hard part is neither the physics nor the politics but the financing and the headcount. South Africa already designs and builds satellites; what it has never done is put one up on its own rocket, which is the difference between being a supplier in someone else's chain and holding an option nobody can withdraw. Read against Issue 064's India–SACU pact, it is the same question with the opposite answer: trade for the capability, or fund it.

TechCentral — “South Africa targets 2030 for its first satellite launch from home soil”

The structural read is that the technical risk here is unusually low and the institutional risk unusually high. ASRI is not proposing to invent a liquid rocket engine; it has hot-fired one three times, from a range that already has a permanent gantry, in a country with a real aerospace supplier base around the Western Cape and a functioning satellite-manufacturing industry. What it is proposing is far harder in a South African context: to convert a university research group into a repeat-launch commercial operation, sustain a thirty-fold funding increase across multiple budget cycles, navigate export-control regimes designed to make exactly this difficult, and grow a specialist team sixfold in a country that exports engineers. The R100m-against-R3bn gap is not a rounding error to be closed by enthusiasm; at the current run rate the programme is funded to roughly 3% of its requirement, which means the 2030 date is a statement of intent rather than a schedule. The interesting commercial question is whether the 200kg-to-500km slot is genuinely under-served for African customers, or whether rideshare launch has already made sovereign access a prestige purchase rather than an economic one.

The engine has been fired, the gantry is built, the range exists. What is missing is the least glamorous input of all — a funded budget line that survives three election cycles and a hundred engineers who do not emigrate.

The threads: to Issue 064's item 5 (India–SACU) — the two are the same strategic question answered opposite ways: there, Southern Africa proposed trading critical minerals for access to manufacturing it does not have; here, it proposes building a capability outright. The negotiating table is faster and cheaper; the launch pad cannot be revoked when the relationship sours. To Issue 062's data-sovereignty items and Issue 063's SARB piece — the same sovereignty instinct now reaching orbit, and the same unresolved question of whether the state will fund what it declares strategic. To today's item 3 — a useful contrast in how hard infrastructure gets financed: Morocco's battery gigafactory drew $114m of development-bank debt against contracted offtake, while a launch programme with no revenue line gets R100m of grant money, which tells you precisely what bankability buys. To the Chronos read on Western Cape deeptech and on optionality — the supplier network, test range and skills base clustering around the Overberg and Stellenbosch corridor is the durable asset here regardless of whether the 2030 date holds, and it is where the investable exposure sits. The sceptic's ledger: national launch programmes are the most over-promised category in aerospace, and 2030 for a first orbital flight from a 20-person university institute would be fast even in a well-funded jurisdiction; the funding gap is not 20% but 97%, and no credible private co-financing has been named; the small-satellite launch market is already crowded, with rideshare making dedicated launch economically hard to justify for a 200kg payload; export controls and ITAR-adjacent restrictions have quietly killed comparable programmes elsewhere; and growing to 120 specialists competes directly with a global market that pays in dollars. The forward marker: the 2028 suborbital flight — and, before it, whether the 2027 budget carries a materially larger allocation, because the gap between R100m and R3bn will not be closed by another announcement.

Nigeria · Tax & Regulation · Platform Risk Analysis Structural design
On 15 July, Douglas Kendyson, founder and CEO of creator-economy startup Selar, publicly accused the Lagos State Internal Revenue Service (LIRS) of “hounding” his company over a backdated 5% royalty withholding on all sales processed through the platform. LIRS's position, set out to TechCabal, is that when someone buys an ebook or a course they are paying for the right to use a creator's copyrighted work — making the payment a royalty, subject to 5% withholding tax. Crucially, the duty to withhold falls on whoever facilitates the payment: the platforms. The rationale is administrative and explicit — 400,000 creators are hard to reach; one platform is not. LIRS confirmed it is running compliance verifications across digital-economy platforms and has taken statutory action against Mainstack under s.67 of the NTAA 2025; Mainstack filed a formal objection on 27 July. Selar paid over ₦18bn ($13.2m) to 400,000 creators last year. The precedent is unhelpful for the platforms: Bolt made the identical “we are only an intermediary” argument on VAT, lost at the Tax Appeal Tribunal in May 2023, lost again at the Federal High Court with ₦1m in costs, and its Court of Appeal challenge filed in September 2025 remains undecided.

Lagos decides the cheapest way to tax 400,000 creators is to conscript the three platforms that pay them

TechCabal · Features · Muktar Oladunmade · Fri 14 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a tax dispute over a definition. The one that matters is the state systematically deputising private platforms as its collection infrastructure wherever the taxpayer base is too fragmented to reach directly — the same move made against Bolt on VAT, now extended from rides to royalties. It threads straight from Issue 064's NIMC item: first the state makes the person legible, then it makes the income legible, and in both cases the compliance burden lands on the rail rather than the regulator. The sting is in the arithmetic — the design collects most aggressively from the creators who owe least.

TechCabal — “The Selar row shows how Nigeria plans to tax its creator economy”

The structural read is that this is a platform-liability question far more than a tax question, and it generalises well beyond Nigerian ebooks. Any business that sits between a large, fragmented supply base and its customers — marketplaces, gig platforms, payment processors, creator tools — is an irresistibly efficient collection point for a revenue authority with limited enforcement capacity. Once one authority establishes the principle, the classification argument that follows is almost decorative: LIRS says the payment is a royalty because the terms of service grant a licence and a sub-licence, Selar says the commercial substance is software-as-a-service on a commission, and a tax tribunal will resolve it by looking at substance, as tribunals do. The mechanism deserves the closer look. Withholding tax is an advance on income tax, not an additional charge, so a high-earning creator is inconvenienced and made whole at year-end by the credit note. But under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025 the first ₦800,000 of annual income is exempt, with up to ₦500,000 more deductible for rent, and the 5% is deducted from gross sales. A creator earning ₦1m therefore has ₦50,000 withheld against a liability of roughly ₦30,000 — or, with the rent relief, nothing at all. She is not paying early; she is lending the state money and must produce a tax identification number, a filed return and a credit note to get it back. Neither the agency nor the article identifies a small-creator threshold or a refund path, and that is the real design flaw.

Withholding is an advance for the creator who owes tax. For the majority who owe nothing, it is an interest-free loan to the state that most of them will never file the paperwork to recover.

The threads: to Issue 064's item 2 (NIMC as Nigeria's Root CA) — the identity layer and the income layer being built in the same period by the same instinct: make the citizen legible, then make the cash flow legible, and deputise the private infrastructure in between. To Issue 062's item on Nigeria's digital tax net and Issue 058's crypto-tax perimeter — this is the third instalment of a coherent Nigerian programme to bring digital income inside the fiscal boundary, and reading them in sequence makes the direction unmistakable. To today's item 2 — an interesting inversion: E-finance is buying its way further up the stack precisely because it already sits between the state and the small business, while Selar is discovering that the same position is a liability rather than an asset when the state comes calling. To the Chronos read on regulatory and structural risk — any portfolio company operating a payout rail to a long tail of individuals now carries a contingent withholding-agent liability that may be backdated, and that belongs in diligence rather than in a footnote. The sceptic's ledger: LIRS has issued no formal assessment, calls the classification preliminary, and there are objection and appeal rights before anything becomes enforceable, so the “hounding” framing came from one side of an open reconciliation; the tax itself is not new money but timing, and for the large sellers who earn most of the ₦18bn it is a genuine non-event; Nigeria's fiscal case for pulling a fast-growing informal sector into the net is entirely legitimate and the platforms' preferred alternative is that nobody collects at all; and the Bolt precedent, while adverse, concerned VAT on a different statutory footing and is still under appeal. The forward marker: whether LIRS issues a formal assessment against Selar or Mainstack, and whether any small-creator exemption threshold appears — the presence or absence of that threshold is the whole difference between a collection mechanism and a regressive one.

Global · AI & Media · Cost Structure Analysis Wouldn't have seen it
Writing in his weekly Crossed Wires column, Steven Boykey Sidley — professor of practice at the University of Johannesburg and a partner at Bridge Capital — reports on “The Cully Hill Boys”, a 110-minute feature film produced by Higgsfield in the San Francisco area. The screenplay is by Timothy Flanagan, a working independent-film writer who had been shopping the script for years without success; the two lead directors are Aitore Zholdaskali and Adilet Abish, filmmakers from Kazakhstan. It is acted by AI-generated “lookalikes and imaginaries” but written and directed by real human talent, and made for a fraction of the millions a comparable studio production would cost. Sidley's argument is that this is categorically different from the other big AI-film story of 2026 — the Iranian-themed Dreams of Violets, admitted to the Tribeca Film Festival — because the significance here is not the novelty of AI assistance but the economics: a full-length feature at a cost that removes the studio from the equation entirely.

A writer nobody would fund and two directors from Kazakhstan just made a 110-minute feature for a rounding error — the gatekeeper was the cost, not the talent

Daily Maverick · Crossed Wires · Steven Boykey Sidley · Sun 16 Aug 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is another AI-and-Hollywood provocation. The one that matters is what happens to an industry whose moat was never taste but capital intensity — if a feature can be made for a fraction of a studio budget, then the studio's real function, which was rationing access to expensive production capacity, stops being a function at all. The detail that carries the argument is who made it: a screenwriter who could not get funded and two directors from Kazakhstan, i.e. precisely the talent the old cost structure filtered out. For a firm that underwrites moats, this is a live case of one dissolving from underneath.

Daily Maverick — “Crossed Wires: Hollywood — dying voices on the wind”

The structural read is that studios were never primarily creative institutions; they were capital allocators and distribution monopolists whose leverage came from controlling access to a production apparatus that cost tens of millions to run. Every part of that apparatus — crews, sets, cameras, post — was a fixed cost that only a large balance sheet could absorb, and the right to say yes to a script was simply the visible expression of who could absorb it. Remove the cost and you have not made the studio worse at its job; you have removed the job. What remains genuinely scarce is the part the article is careful to preserve: the script that works, the directorial judgement, the taste to know which of ten thousand generated options is the right one, and above all the distribution and attention that gets a finished film watched. That last one is the durable moat, and it is the one worth watching, because it is not obvious that it belongs to the studios either — it may already sit with the platforms. The transferable question for any business is the uncomfortable one: how much of what you charge for is judgement, and how much is the fact that the thing you do is expensive to do?

A screenwriter who spent years being told no, and two directors from Kazakhstan. The old system was not selecting for talent — it was selecting for who could afford the apparatus. Remove the apparatus and you discover what the gate was actually filtering.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Make Claude force the mess into a 2×2 — the axes it picks tell you more than the boxes it fills

When a situation has too many moving parts to hold in your head — six portfolio companies, four candidate strategies, a market with a dozen players — the instinct is to ask for a summary, and a summary is exactly the wrong output because it preserves the mess in shorter form. The better instruction is to force a structure: give Claude the material and require a 2×2, naming the two axes that best separate the cases and placing everything on it. The value is rarely in the finished grid. It is in the axes — because to choose two dimensions, the model has to decide which of the fifteen differences between your options actually matter and which are noise, and that judgement is visible and arguable in a way a paragraph of prose is not. If the axes it picks feel wrong, you have learned something specific about your own framing and can say so. If they feel right but the placements surprise you, you have found the case that does not fit your mental model. Then ask for a second, deliberately different pair of axes on the same material: two good 2×2s of the same situation is usually a sharper diagnostic than any amount of narrative.

A worked example

Instead of “Summarise where each of these portfolio companies stands”, try: “Here are notes on eleven portfolio companies. Force them onto a single 2×2. Choose the two axes that genuinely separate them — not obvious ones like size or sector — and justify the choice in two sentences before you place anything. Then flag the companies that sit awkwardly on your own grid and say why. Finally, give me a second 2×2 using a completely different pair of axes, and tell me which of the two is more decision-useful.”
