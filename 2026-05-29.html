Africa & EM · SARB Monetary Policy 25bp hike landed
The decision Issue 011 flagged 18 hours ago landed: SARB hiked the repo rate by 25bp to 7%, effective today. The MPC split 4-2 — the first hike since 2023 and the first material defence of the new 3% inflation target. Headline inflation is now forecast at 4.4% this year and 3.7% in 2027, returning to target only in 2028. The hawks won the credibility argument: even a Middle East oil-shock one-off pulse cannot be tolerated when expectations re-anchor.

SARB hikes 25bp to 7% — Kganyago pays for the 3% target

Stella Mapenzauswa · Business Day · Thu 28 May 2026, 15:21 SAST
SARB MPC statement, 28 May 2026 · Stats SA · Spock

Kganyago framed the decision as defensive against second-round effects from the Middle East oil shock — Brent fluctuating around $100/bbl, food prices rising on diesel and fertiliser pressure. Four members voted hike, two voted hold at 6.75%. The headline inflation track is now 4.4% (2026), 3.7% (2027), with the 3% target reached only in 2028. Kganyago noted Moody's positive outlook on the sovereign rating and called the macro "demonstrating resilience". Prime moves to 11.50% from 11.25% — adding roughly R165/month to a R1m bond, R495/month to R3m. Capitec, FirstRand and Standard Bank now operate with a 25bp NIM tailwind on the funded book, against a 25bp pressure on credit-extension growth and default risk for the marginal consumer borrower.

Business Day — Stella Mapenzauswa

The 4-2 split is the read, not the 25bp. A unanimous hike would have signalled an MPC unified by the data. A split signals an MPC where two members concluded the data didn't justify making households pay for an exogenous oil shock. That dissent is now publicly on the record — it will frame every subsequent decision and gives Kganyago a credible bridge if the next print softens and he chooses to hold. The hawks have won the round; the doves have positioned themselves for the next one.

The 2028 return-to-target is the more important number than the 7% repo. SARB has now told the market that the new 3% midpoint is a three-year, not a one-year, journey. The credibility cost of the new target is being explicitly amortised. For LPs assessing SA macro discipline, this is the cleanest answer Kganyago could have given: a hike (defends the target), a split (signals proportionality), and a three-year glidepath (signals discipline without panic).

"These projections entail some second-round effects, as the shock broadens out into wages and inflation expectations. Given the forecasts, we see upside risks to inflation." — Lesetja Kganyago, MPC statement, 28 May 2026

The longitudinal thread: Issue 008's Howard Marks "calibrated uncertainty" framing warned about central banks losing the discipline to defend new targets under political pressure. Issue 009's SARB stablecoin sandbox showed credibility extension on one perimeter. Issue 011 flagged the live decision. Today's outcome — hike, split, three-year glidepath — is the credibility-defence move that completes the arc. The Kganyago doctrine: target defence at the cost of growth, communicated through wording rather than magnitude.

For the portfolio: rain pricing its mid-tier 5G plan into a consumer with R165–R495/month less disposable income; Optasia's airtime-credit book absorbing 25bp of marginal-default deterioration; TymeBank earning the NIM but slowing funded growth; Crossfin's terminal-acquired merchant base seeing volume softness in the next 60 days. MGT's premium-bearing assets are the relative winner. Each board pack between now and the September MPC needs an updated stress on a hold-or-hike-again decision path.

Anthropic vaults to $965bn — Willison's April PMF call now has the capital signature

Hayden Field & Kif Leswing · CNBC · Thu 28 May 2026

The financing nearly triples Anthropic's $380bn February valuation. Revenue acceleration is the headline: $10bn → $30bn → $47bn ARR in roughly 15 months. The funding round was over-subscribed; Altimeter, Dragoneer, Greenoaks and Sequoia anchored. Anthropic's enterprise-deployment moves over the past six weeks compound the signal: the $200m Gates Foundation partnership, the KPMG 276,000-seat strategic alliance, the Microsoft 365 deep integration, the Moody's data partnership, and the Wall Street-focused agent suite. OpenAI's last round (March, $122bn raise) put it at $852bn. The two companies now sit on either side of a trillion-dollar threshold with different go-to-market shapes — Anthropic enterprise-first, OpenAI consumer-first — and the public capital markets are pricing both as scarcity assets.

CNBC — Anthropic nears $1tn valuation

The valuation gap to OpenAI is the news. Anthropic is now valued at roughly 20.5× ARR; OpenAI at the equivalent multiple is closer to 22× on its disclosed run-rate. The market is paying not for revenue today but for the rate of acceleration. $10bn → $47bn ARR in 15 months is the kind of trajectory that, if maintained for two more years, gets you to a $200bn-plus revenue line — which the $965bn valuation is roughly capitalising at 5× forward. The bet is duration of the acceleration. If Anthropic runs the next 15 months at the same shape, the multiple compresses naturally. If the acceleration breaks, the multiple is exposed.

The strategic read: the enterprise-stack-lock-in thesis is now the priced thesis. KPMG, PwC, Moody's, Microsoft 365 integration — each one of these is a structural wedge that compounds month-on-month inside a regulated client's working artefacts. The cost of switching out grows with every audit working paper produced through Claude. The model-performance war is becoming the floor (any frontier model that can keep up will), and the enterprise-deployment war is becoming the differentiator. Anthropic is winning round one of the enterprise-deployment war. OpenAI has not yet shown an equivalent moat outside the consumer surface.

Africa & EM · SA Governance Structural
Busisiwe Mavuso, CEO of Business Leadership South Africa, told a Daily Maverick business breakfast in Nelson Mandela Bay yesterday that organised business has made "a fundamental mistake of having created this dependency from government". The line lands at the moment Volkswagen Group Africa — the metro's largest employer — discloses it has spent R900,000 on fleet repair due to pothole damage on top of millions on its own electricity supply. The structural argument: every act of corporate substitution for the state lengthens the runway of state dysfunction.

Mavuso: SA business is making "a fundamental mistake" by adopting the state's job

Riaan Marais · Daily Maverick · Thu 28 May 2026

Mavuso, speaking alongside VWGA chair Martina Biene, named NMB mayor Babalwa Lobishe directly as one of the leadership failures responsible for the metro's decline — Gqeberha has had 18 city managers since 2020. Power generation is no longer the binding constraint: SA now generates more electricity than it needs. The constraint is distribution — substations, powerlines, the maintenance layer between generation and consumer. Water shows the same pattern: 60%+ of NMB's water is lost to leaks, framed by Van Huyssteen as "not a drought, a management crisis". Mavuso's framing — "capital is like water, it follows the path of least resistance" — is the hinge. The foreign-direct-investment thesis breaks down once friction exceeds a threshold, regardless of how much patriotic-business adoption picks up the operational slack.

Daily Maverick — Riaan Marais

The Mavuso framing is uncomfortable because it is correct in both directions. If business stops repairing roads, the immediate operational consequence is real. If business continues repairing roads, the medium-term political consequence is that the state is allowed to abandon its obligations. There is no clean answer — only a question of where the line is drawn and how the cost of substitution is recognised. The intellectually honest position is that the substitution cannot be permanent, and the public-political pressure has to be maintained at the same time, by the same business community, with the same energy.

For Chronos: every SA portfolio company is paying a substitution tax somewhere on the P&L. rain operates a tower network in an environment where municipal power supply is unreliable enough that battery + solar redundancy is not optional. TymeBank operates a branch and kiosk network where physical-infrastructure failure feeds back into customer experience. Crossfin's POS-acquired merchants live and die on payment-rail uptime, which depends on telco infrastructure, which depends on municipal power. MGT writes premiums against a built environment whose risk-of-loss is rising on management-failure grounds, not on actuarial-noise grounds. The substitution cost is in the operating expense line of every one of those companies. None of it shows up as a separate disclosure.

"Capital is like water. It will always follow the path of least resistance. They are not going to perpetually understand the mess and the managing around the problem that is actually happening here." — Busisiwe Mavuso, BLSA

The longitudinal thread: Issue 011's Standard Bank R517bn structural reorder showed the pan-African banking hierarchy responding to a CMD commitment to operating-model discipline. Today's Mavuso piece is the same insight at the country level: the institutions that win are the ones that publicly commit to a doctrine and execute against it. The institutions that lose are the ones that allow others to substitute their obligations. The board-level read for portcos: publish the doctrine, defend the doctrine, do not let the operating environment quietly subsidise weakness elsewhere in the value chain.

Paga Engine processed roughly $12bn across 100 million transactions in 2025 — implied revenue at a 0.1–0.3% take rate of $12m–$36m from the infrastructure business alone. Clients include Meta and Amazon. The Africa-specific "Stripe moment" is now visible.

Paga packages 20 years of payment rails as an engine — $12bn flowed through it in 2025

Temitayo Jaiyeola · TechCabal · Wed 27 May 2026

Nigerian digital payment volumes hit ₦1.07 quadrillion ($774.9bn) in 2024 (NIBSS) and ₦284.99 trillion ($206.4bn) in Q1 2025 alone. Building a regulated payment stack in Nigeria costs ₦100m+ ($72,422) just for licensing, and Stripe estimates $150k-$250k for a minimum-viable payment gateway, before maintenance, compliance and ongoing engineering. Paga Engine offers that capability as a hosted service: payment gateways, wallet infrastructure, transfers, embedded payment experiences, and agent distribution. Operational tooling — compliance, fraud monitoring, transaction visibility, enterprise support — is layered on top. Competition is real (Flutterwave, Paystack, Onafriq), but Paga's bet is on operational depth, not feature parity. The infrastructure-versus-consumer split mirrors what Stripe did to PayPal: the same flows, with steadier economics underneath.

TechCabal — Temitayo Jaiyeola

The Paga Engine pivot is the African enactment of a structural rule the rest of the world learned eight years ago: in payments, the consumer wallet is a customer-acquisition cost story; the infrastructure-as-a-service business sits underneath those flows with much steadier unit economics and a much harder moat. The moat is not technology — it is licensing, regulatory relationships, operational tooling, fraud-monitoring, settlement experience, and the kind of debugging knowledge you only acquire by clearing 100 million transactions. Each of those is a multi-year compound. The competitive set in Africa — Flutterwave, Paystack, Onafriq — converges on the same insight at different speeds.

For Crossfin specifically, this is the closest direct read in the portfolio. Crossfin owns the merchant terminal layer in South Africa, which is the same structural position with a different geographic license. The valuation implication: Crossfin's revenue mix should be analysed on the same "infrastructure share" lens, with the share-of-flow metric rather than the share-of-customer metric. If Crossfin can disclose flow-share and the implied take-rate compounding over the next four quarters, the listed-comparable framing becomes Paga, Flutterwave and PagSeguro — not the Nigerian fintech consumer-wallet pack. That is a different multiple regime.

"When you look at payments, money will move back and forth. That is where the Paga Engine makes money. Within that flow of money, we charge a very small fee that allows us to keep the service running." — Ramon Bello, GM, Paga Engine

The longitudinal thread: Issue 007's "AT1 raise" piece showed SA banks accessing the capital markets to compete with the same infrastructure layer at scale. Issue 009's stablecoin-sandbox piece showed the regulatory perimeter being extended toward the same infrastructure layer. Today's Paga piece shows an operator productising twenty years of internal payment technology into the same layer. The convergence is consistent: the most valuable position in African financial services for the rest of the decade is the infrastructure layer below the consumer surface. The question for each portco is not "do we play here" but "what is our specific infrastructure-share metric and is it disclosable to LPs at the next quarterly".

Africa & EM · AI Regulation
South Africa's national AI policy has been delayed to January 2027 after the government withdrew its first draft when reports surfaced that several academic references in the document were fabricated or misattributed — almost certainly generative-AI hallucinations passed through without verification. Two officials suspended. A 12-person independent review panel chaired by Prof Benjamin Rosman of Wits has been appointed to rebuild the framework. The country wanting to be the continental leader in AI policy just demonstrated the basic governance problem AI creates.

SA delays national AI policy to 2027 after Cabinet draft cited fabricated academic references

Phathisani Moyo · TechCabal · Tue 26 May 2026

Cabinet approved the original draft in March; it was gazetted in April for public comment. Minister Solly Malatsi confirmed to MPs on Tuesday that the department "had not picked up that there were issues with the references in the draft policy document before the events were exposed in news reports". A draft built with generative-AI assistance was published as a state document without basic citation verification. The new panel includes Prof Vukosi Marivate, Prof Alison Gillwald, Bowman's partner Heather Irvine, Dr Tshepo Feela, Jabu Mtsweni and cyber lawyer Lufuno Tshikalange. Revised framework opens for public comment January 2027 — meaning South Africa enters an 8-month window of formal AI policy vacuum while businesses and government institutions deploy AI systems anyway.

TechCabal — Phathisani Moyo

The negative-case read on AI sovereignty narratives is now active in South Africa. Issue 011's Rest of World piece celebrated the $60bn African AI Sovereignty ambition. Today's piece shows what the execution layer looks like when institutional capacity hasn't caught up with declarative ambition. The two stories belong together — and any LP conversation about "African AI sovereignty as a thesis" now has to acknowledge that the supply side of the thesis is the governance institutions, not the model availability, and that supply side is materially behind the demand side.

For the Chronos portfolio operating in South Africa, the eight-month policy vacuum is the operational read. There is no formal national policy on AI use in regulated industries until January 2027 at the earliest — and the public-comment-to-Cabinet-to-implementation timeline pushes practical effect into late 2027 or 2028. Boards must self-govern. That means written, board-approved policies on: model selection, vendor risk, citation/source verification (this is exactly the failure mode that happened), data residency, customer disclosure, audit-trail retention, and the prudential treatment of AI-generated work product. Spock's recommendation: every portco board pack in Q3 2026 includes a one-page "AI governance status" disclosure.

"The department had not picked up that there were issues with the references in the draft policy document before the events were exposed in news reports." — Solly Malatsi, Minister of Communications and Digital Technologies, briefing Parliament

The longitudinal thread: Issue 009's stablecoin sandbox showed SARB extending the regulatory perimeter on credibility. Issue 011's AI sovereignty piece showed the ambition. Today's piece shows the governance gap. The implicit Chronos play: investments that depend on regulated AI deployment in South Africa face an 18-month additional moat-from-regulatory-uncertainty period. Investments that depend on operator-driven self-governance (private companies setting their own standards) move faster than the market expects, because the absence of regulation means the operators who set the de facto standard inherit the moat.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Draft then edit, never edit then draft

The single highest-leverage habit-change for using Claude well in a partner workflow is to run the model on a draft-then-edit cycle, not an edit-then-draft cycle. The default temptation is to refine a request before sending it — to fix the framing, add context, tighten the question. That habit comes from email. It is wrong here. Claude's marginal cost of producing a first draft is near-zero. Use that. Send the rough thought as the prompt, get the first draft back, then edit the model on what is wrong with the draft. Two or three edit cycles produce a sharper artefact than the polished initial prompt would have done — because the model has surfaced what you actually wanted versus what you said you wanted. The cycle is the work; the prompt is just the starter pistol.

A three-cycle worked example

Cycle 1 prompt: "Quick read on whether SARB's 25bp hike yesterday changes the rain board pricing decision." Cycle 1 output: a generic six-paragraph treatment. Cycle 2 prompt: "Cut the macro section. Focus only on the through-line: hike → consumer disposable income compression → 5G plan price elasticity → revenue forecast. Give me three numbers." Cycle 2 output: a sharper three-number version. Cycle 3 prompt: "Now write the one paragraph I would put into the board pack." Cycle 3 output: a board-ready paragraph. The first prompt did 20% of the work. The last two cycles did 80%. That is the model — and it is the opposite of how most partners use the tool today.
