Nigeria · Banking · Regulation Longitudinal
Nigeria’s Central Bank has revoked the operating licences of 46 microfinance banks, effective 1 July 2026, after supervisory reviews found breaches of licensing conditions — insufficient assets to cover liabilities, operating without approval, prolonged inactivity, failure to commence operations, or failure to maintain minimum capital. Governor Olayemi Cardoso approved the revocations under Sections 12 and 13 of BOFIA 2020; the largest concentrations are in Kano, Lagos, Abuja and Kebbi. It extends a wider clean-up: in 2024 the CBN raised minimum capital for commercial, merchant and non-interest banks, giving them until 2026 to recapitalise or merge, and it has been steadily withdrawing licences from microfinance operators that miss prudential standards. Nigeria had more than 900 licensed microfinance banks at end-2024 (NDIC) — one of Africa’s largest such markets — so the shift is explicitly from expanding the number of operators to improving the quality and stability of the ones that remain.

Nigeria cancels 46 microfinance-bank licences in a day — the same week its fintechs were racing to become microfinance banks

Ecofin Agency (Cynthia Ebot Takang) · Finance · Fri 3 Jul 2026

This is the counterweight to the story we ran on Wednesday, when Nigeria’s biggest fintechs were trading payments licences for microfinance-bank licences to unlock a deposit base. Today the regulator shows what that licence actually costs to keep: 46 holders of it just lost it for failing prudential standards. The CBN is doing to microfinance what it is already doing to the commercial tier — culling under-capitalised operators and forcing the sector to consolidate around fewer, sounder balance sheets. For anyone underwriting Nigerian financial-inclusion exposure, the message is that a microfinance-bank licence is not a growth hack; it is a supervised banking permission with capital, governance and reporting obligations the CBN is now willing to enforce by revocation.

Ecofin Agency — “Nigeria Revokes 46 Microfinance Bank Licenses Over Regulatory Breaches”

Wednesday’s thread was that Nigeria’s fintechs are becoming banks on the regulator’s terms — inheriting a rulebook that dictates how deposits are lent and how income is earned. Today closes the loop: the regulator is simultaneously thinning the existing field of banks that could not carry those terms. Read together, the two stories describe a single motion. The prize (a licensed deposit base) and the price (CBN capital and governance rules, enforced) are two halves of the same transaction, and Cardoso is willing to complete the transaction by cancelling licences at scale.

“The action forms part of the CBN’s broader supervisory programme to protect depositors and ensure that only financially sound institutions remain in Nigeria’s banking system.”

For Chronos this matters on three levels, and it is directly relevant to Optasia’s Nigerian footprint. First, it is a live read on regulatory direction: the CBN is consolidating, not liberalising, and any thesis that relied on a permissive microfinance regime is now weaker. Second, it reframes the “fintech becomes a bank” trade — the licence that looked like an unlock on Wednesday is, on Friday’s evidence, a standing obligation that a well-capitalised, well-governed operator can turn into a moat and a thin one cannot survive. That asymmetry favours scaled players with real balance sheets over sub-scale licence-holders, which is a tailwind for the larger, better-capitalised lenders in our line of sight and a warning on the long tail. Third, the depositor-protection framing is the one that travels: as African regulators from Lagos to Kinshasa reassert control over who may hold retail money, the value in financial inclusion shifts from having the licence to being the counterparty regulators trust to keep it. The forward question: as the 900-plus field compresses, which survivors inherit the customers — and is the acquirer of those relationships a bank, a telco wallet, or a fintech that just became both.

DR Congo · Payments · Monetary Sovereignty Wouldn't have seen otherwise
Ecofin frames the DRC as the live proving ground for dollar-settled African mobile money. Onafriq — the network whose APIs connect M-Pesa, Airtel Money and Orange Money wallets in the country — is moving cross-border settlement onto USDC stablecoin rails via US platform Conduit, promising same-day payouts where correspondent banking through SWIFT takes days. The backdrop is stark: the World Bank puts sub-Saharan remittance costs near 8% (the world’s most expensive corridor); Chainalysis measured $205bn of on-chain value received across the region in the year to June 2025, up 52%, with stablecoins at 43% of volume; Conduit’s African customer base grew 80% in one quarter. Against that, the Central Bank of the Congo’s new governor, André Wameso, is fighting to restore a franc in a country where ~80% of transactions are in foreign currency — and Washington’s GENIUS Act (July 2025) has given regulated dollar stablecoins a formal tailwind, hardwiring their growth into demand for US Treasuries.

DR Congo becomes Africa’s live test for dollar-settled mobile money — digital dollars re-plumb the wallets just as the central bank fights to rebuild the franc

Ecofin Agency (Idriss Linge) · Finance · Thu 2 Jul 2026

This is the payments version of Thursday’s gold story: a contest over who sets the terms on which African value is captured and settled. Stablecoin rails are a genuine gift to the trader in Kinshasa paying a supplier in Nairobi — minutes instead of days, single digits instead of 8% — and Africans are adopting them ahead of any permission. But every wallet that settles in USDC replaces the physical dollar the BCC is trying to squeeze out with a faster, borderless, harder-to-see digital one, and quietly turns a Congolese saver into a lender to the US Treasury rather than a depositor in a local bank. The winners (traders, remittance families, fintechs on cheaper rails) are easy to photograph; the losers (central banks watching deposits migrate beyond reach, local banks cut out of the chain) are not.

Ecofin Agency — “DR Congo Becomes Africa’s Live Test For Dollar-Settled Mobile Money”

We have been building a monetary-sovereignty thread across editions — the yuan hub, the gold-pricing push, the DRC’s strong-franc project. This is the same argument at street level, and it is the sharpest version yet because the two forces are colliding inside a single product: the mobile-money wallet. Wameso is trying to rebuild confidence in the franc; the rails beneath the wallet are being re-laid in dollars. Both cannot win, and the outcome will be decided less by monetary policy than by which settlement layer merchants actually choose.

“When a Kinshasa merchant holds savings in USDC, she is, in effect, lending to the US Treasury rather than depositing in a Congolese bank — with consequences for local credit creation the central banks never voted on.”

For Chronos this is a thread to hold, and it is directly load-bearing for the fintech and payments assets we touch. Three calibrations. First, the GENIUS Act changes the risk profile of the instrument: a regulated USDC is far less likely to break its peg than the last cycle’s experiments, which means stablecoin settlement is graduating from grey-zone workaround to durable infrastructure — and any African payments business now has to have a view on whether it settles on those rails or gets undercut by one that does. Second, it is a regulatory-risk map: the losers here are central banks and local banks, and their response (the BCEAO is weighing an e-FCFA; Kinshasa must decide whether to license, tax or channel the flows) is exactly the kind of intervention that reprices a payments licence overnight. Third, the deeper point rhymes with the whole week: owning the wallet is worth little if the settlement layer beneath it is someone else’s dollar rail. The forward question for our portfolio: where a company’s edge is its settlement relationships, does dollar-stablecoin plumbing strengthen the moat (cheaper, faster, sticky) or erode it (the rail becomes a commodity anyone can rent) — and which of our operators has actually chosen a side.

Kenya · Mobile Money · M&A Thesis test
Vodacom completed a $2.1bn purchase on 30 June that lifted its effective stake in Safaricom to 55% from 35%, handing the South African group majority control of East Africa’s largest telecom operator after Kenya’s Court of Appeal cleared a months-long legal suspension on 26 June. The deal folds Safaricom and M-Pesa — which generates ~44% of Safaricom’s Kenyan revenue — into Vodacom’s accounts. It lands as M-Pesa’s home dominance shows its first cracks: its share of Kenyan mobile-money subscriptions slipped below 90% for the first time (89.7% late 2025) as Airtel Money climbed to 10.3% on lower tariffs, wallet interoperability and central-bank fee pressure. Vodacom counts 103 million financial-service customers and $525.6bn in annual transaction value group-wide. The contrast with rivals is the story: MTN is carving out MoMo to attract outside capital (Mastercard holds a stake valued at $5.2bn), and Airtel is weighing an Airtel Money IPO bankers value at up to $10bn — which would set the first public price for an African mobile-money business.

Vodacom pays $2.1bn to stop renting M-Pesa and own it outright — just as its two-decade lead starts to crack

Ecofin Agency (Idriss Linge) · Digital · Wed 1 Jul 2026

This is a clean test of the mobile-money moat thesis, and the three carriers are running three different experiments on it at once. Vodacom is consolidating — buying full control of the crown asset at the moment competition is heating up, on the view that owning M-Pesa outright is worth more than the price of a contested lead. MTN is doing the opposite, separating MoMo to let outside capital mark it. Airtel is going furthest, planning to float Airtel Money and let the public price it. Between them they will, within a year or two, tell us what an African mobile-money business is actually worth — and whether the moat is the network, the licence, or simply the two-decade head start that Airtel is now eroding one tariff cut at a time.

Ecofin Agency — “Vodacom Cements Safaricom Grip Amid Battle for Africa’s Mobile Money Market”

The timing is the tell. Vodacom is not buying control of Safaricom because M-Pesa is unassailable; it is buying because M-Pesa has, for the first time in nearly two decades, slipped below 90% share at home. Consolidating the crown asset while its lead is still large but no longer growing is the same move a state champion made on Thursday when it locked up a cobalt deposit early and cheap — secure the strategic asset before the market reprices the fight for it.

“M-Pesa’s share of Kenyan mobile-money subscriptions slipped below 90% for the first time… Vodacom is responding with a super-app built around M-Pesa, hosting 221 mini apps and 9.4 million active financial-service users, up 40%.”

For Chronos the value is the natural experiment the three carriers are staging on a question we underwrite constantly: what is a payments network’s moat actually worth? Three reads. First, the divergence is investable intelligence — Airtel Money’s mooted $10bn IPO would set the first public comparable for an African mobile-money asset, and MTN’s MoMo carve-out (Mastercard’s stake implying ~$17bn for the whole) gives a second private mark; together they will discipline every valuation conversation we have about payment rails on the continent. Second, the competitive dynamic validates a caution we have carried: interoperability and central-bank fee caps are structural headwinds to incumbency, and even a two-decade monopoly like M-Pesa is not immune — the moat erodes from the tariff sheet up. Third, Vodacom’s answer — a super-app wrapping payments, lending and 221 mini-apps around the wallet — is the same “own the surface where the work happens” logic that ran through the week, applied to consumer finance. The forward question: as MTN pushes into direct lending against Nigeria’s $236bn SME credit gap and Vodacom deepens its super-app, the mobile-money winners are moving from transaction fees toward balance-sheet risk — and that shift is exactly where a growth-equity investor should be asking who has the underwriting discipline to survive it.

Nigeria · Fintech Infrastructure · Tokenisation Longitudinal
Paga — one of Africa’s oldest fintechs — is partnering with New York-based blockchain-infrastructure startup TBook to let consumers and businesses invest in tokenised real-world assets (RWAs), from fixed income to tokenised private assets, built on the Sui blockchain. The strategic point is not the crypto: it is that Paga Engine, its payments-infrastructure arm which processed ~$12bn in transaction value in 2025, can now distribute investment products through the 300-plus businesses building on it — positioning Paga to power the financial products other companies sell, not just its own. TBook supplies the plug-and-play rails so fintechs can embed yield-bearing tokenised assets without building the blockchain themselves; it runs the same model with OmniPay in the Philippines. Context on the category: tokenised RWAs have grown to ~$10bn–$31bn on-chain (tenfold since early 2024); Mordor sizes the broader tokenisation industry at $2.08tn in 2025. Paga distributes only through entities regulated in the markets where it operates, and holds a CBN microfinance-banking licence alongside its mobile-money licence.

Paga bets on owning the rail, not the asset — turning idle balances into yield and letting 300 businesses embed tokenised investments

TechCabal (Emmanuel Nwosu) · Cryptocurrency · Mon 30 Jun 2026

This is the counterpoint to every “own the hard asset” story in the edition — and it revisits the Paga thread Roger flagged in Issue 012, where the moat was “a form of geographical moat” built on licences, regulatory relationships and settlement experience. Here Paga extends exactly that moat upward: rather than owning any single financial product, it wants to be the regulated pipe through which other businesses distribute savings and investment products to millions of Africans. TBook’s pitch names the prize plainly — every consumer fintech sits on idle balances that earn nothing for the customer and little for the business, and whoever turns those balances into yield first captures a new revenue layer beyond transaction fees. The risk is equally plain: tokenisation does not remove the underlying asset’s dependence on real custody, valuation and jurisdictional regulation.

TechCabal — “Paga turns to tokenised investments in latest infrastructure push”

Issue 012’s Paga item drew Roger’s sharpest note of that edition: the moat is not technology but licensing, regulatory relationships, operational tooling and the debugging knowledge you only get by clearing a hundred million transactions — a geographical moat. This move is the logical next chapter of that thesis. Paga is not chasing the shiny end of crypto; it is using $12bn of cleared volume and a CBN microfinance licence to become the distribution layer for regulated, yield-bearing products that 300-plus businesses can embed. The infrastructure is the product; the tokenised asset is just the first thing it carries.

“Every consumer fintech is sitting on idle balances that earn nothing for the customer and little for the business — especially in emerging markets. This is what financial inclusion looks like at scale.”

For Chronos there are three things to hold. First, it sharpens the “own the rail” playbook we keep circling: in a market where owning any single hard asset is contested and cheap to acquire, the durable position is the regulated pipe others must run through — Paga is trying to be to African wealth products what a payments switch is to transactions. Second, it is a direct read on where fintech margin migrates next: as transaction fees compress under regulatory pressure (see today’s Vodacom and Nigeria items), monetising idle customer balances via embedded yield is the obvious next revenue layer, and the winners will be whoever pairs distribution reach with a regulatory permission to hold and invest — a combination Paga has deliberately assembled. Third, the caution is real and worth underwriting explicitly: tokenised RWAs inherit every risk of the underlying — custody, valuation, and the regulator’s posture in each jurisdiction — and the category’s $2tn projections are exactly the kind of number that invites both genuine inclusion and genuine mis-selling. The forward question for any African fintech in our line of sight: is it building to own a product, or to own the rail the product rides on — and does it have the licence stack to make the second one defensible.

South Africa · Downstream Energy · M&A Longitudinal
Shell is in advanced talks to sell its entire 600-station South African forecourt network to ADNOC Distribution — the retail arm of the UAE’s national oil company — in a deal valued at about $1bn (Bloomberg, 30 June; unconfirmed by either party). It would hand ADNOC roughly 10% of the fuel-retail market in the continent’s largest economy and mark Shell’s exit after more than 120 years in the country (Shell entered in 1902; the network runs through Shell Downstream South Africa, in which black-owned Thebe Investment Corporation holds 28%). ADNOC emerged as preferred buyer after talks with Gunvor failed; Puma Energy, Sasol and PetroSA are no longer bidding. It fits a clear consolidation pattern: Glencore bought Chevron/Caltex’s network in 2018; Vitol’s Vivo Energy acquired Engen in 2025; Shell already sold its Durban refinery to the SA government in 2024. For ADNOC it is one more step in a plan to deploy $150bn of acquisitions worldwide between 2026 and 2030.

A Gulf oil champion nears a $1bn deal for Shell’s 600 SA forecourts — foreign capital keeps buying the piece of South Africa it can price

Ecofin Agency (Abdel-Latif Boureima) · Industry · Thu 2 Jul 2026

Read against Thursday’s Alcoa–Hillside deal, this completes a picture of how foreign capital is repricing South African assets: it pays up for the parts it can underwrite and walks from the parts it cannot. Where Alcoa bet on a bankable power price, ADNOC is buying a distribution network with predictable retail cash flows and ~10% market share — a defensible, regulated slice of the continent’s largest fuel market — as the century-old oil majors rotate out. The through-line to the whole edition: as international incumbents exit, the SA base does not empty; it consolidates into the hands of state champions and trading houses (Glencore, Vitol, now ADNOC) that value security of supply and scale over the majors’ portfolio discipline.

Ecofin Agency — “Shell Nears $1 Billion Sale of South African Service Stations to ADNOC Distribution”

Thursday we said the Alcoa deal was a precise map of where foreign confidence in South Africa is and isn’t — it pays for the bankable asset and walks from the un-powerable one. The Shell–ADNOC talks extend the same map into downstream energy. A Gulf state champion paying $1bn for 600 forecourts and 10% of the market is a real vote of confidence in South African retail demand and cash generation — but it is also the fourth international major in seven years to hand its SA network to a trader or a state player, which says as much about who is leaving as about who is arriving.

“Shell would become the latest international oil major to leave South Africa’s fuel-retail market, ending more than 120 years of operations since entering the country in 1902.”

For Chronos this is thematic rather than a live deal, and it lands on three points. First, it is a clean read on the type of SA asset that still attracts a premium bidder: not the commodity-exposed or power-hungry, but the regulated, cash-generative distribution network with a defensible market share — the same “own the rail” quality that recurs through the edition, here in physical form as a retail fuel footprint. Second, the buyer profile is the recurring feature of this whole week — a state-backed champion (ADNOC) executing an industrial-policy mandate with a $150bn acquisition budget, willing to pay for strategic reach in ways a return-seeking financial investor cannot, exactly as we noted with the Chinese cobalt and gold moves. That has a direct implication: in the assets these champions want, a financial investor is often the seller, not the buyer. Third, the consolidation itself (Glencore, Vivo/Vitol, now ADNOC) is quietly reshaping SA downstream into fewer, larger, better-capitalised hands — a more concentrated market with pricing power, and one worth watching for how the Thebe 28% BEE stake is treated, since empowerment continuity is where SA regulatory approval will actually turn. The forward question: as the majors complete their exit, does South African downstream energy end up more efficient and better-capitalised, or simply more concentrated and more exposed to the priorities of foreign state owners?

Africa · Labour · Data Chart
TechCabal Insights compiled every publicly reported African tech layoff between January 2023 and March 2026: at least 56 events and 4,948 disclosed job losses. The dataset surfaces patterns invisible in single announcements. Funding fell first — from a record $4.65bn across 941 deals in 2022 to $2.92bn (554 deals) in 2023 and $2.24bn in 2024, less than half the peak — and the layoffs were the hangover from over-hiring in the boom. Fintech recorded the most events (20 of 56, 35%) but only 416 job losses (~20 per cut); e-commerce recorded just 12 events but 2,872 losses, because warehouses and last-mile logistics employ far more people per dollar. Growth-stage companies bore 88% of disclosed losses; two of the five largest events (Copia Global, KOKO Networks) ended in outright shutdown. The finding that matters most: across all 56 events, AI is cited as a direct cause exactly once (Zap Africa, Feb 2026) — against ~55,000 AI-attributed US layoffs in 2025 alone.

Three years of African tech layoffs, mapped — and AI is named as the reason exactly once

TechCabal Insights (Margaret Awojide) · TC Insights · Mon 29 Jun 2026

This is the primary-data read that disciplines a lazy narrative — and it threads straight back to the “Labour Shift” item Roger up-voted in Issue 017. The story African tech tells itself is about restructuring and cost-cutting; the story global tech tells is about AI. The same underlying force may be at work in both, but African companies almost never say so: 55 of 56 events are attributed to funding, macro or “efficiency,” and the one explicit “AI-driven” layoff cut eight people. The more useful structural insight is the divergence between where the events cluster (fintech, many small cuts) and where the jobs actually went (e-commerce and logistics, few but massive) — a reminder that headcount risk in a portfolio scales with physical operations, not with sector headlines.

Fintech has the most layoff events but the fewest jobs lost; e-commerce and logistics, with warehouses and last-mile staff, is where the headcount actually went
TechCabal Insights — “Inside Africa’s tech layoffs: What the data says (2023–2026)”

Issue 017’s “Labour Shift” item flagged African tech layoffs crossing 1,000 and turning explicitly AI-driven. This three-year dataset lets us pressure-test that claim, and it partly disconfirms it: the layoffs are real and large, but the AI attribution is almost entirely absent from what companies actually say. Only one of 56 events names AI as the cause. That gap — near-total silence in Africa versus ~55,000 AI-attributed cuts in the US in 2025 — is the single most interesting line in the report, precisely because we cannot yet tell whether it reflects genuinely lower AI adoption, a difference in what firms will admit publicly, or simply a lag before the same logic arrives.

“It is worth asking whether AI is quietly embedded inside some of the ‘restructuring’ and ‘cost-cutting’ language in this dataset, even where it is not named.”

For Chronos there are three takeaways. First, it is a discipline check on our own pattern-matching, the same lesson as Thursday’s funding data: the headline (“AI is coming for the jobs”) is not what the primary data supports on this continent yet, and a portfolio thesis that over-indexes on African AI-driven displacement is, on this evidence, ahead of the record. Second, the events-versus-jobs divergence is genuinely useful for how we think about headcount risk in the businesses we back: fintech generates lots of small cuts, but the catastrophic, business-ending losses sit in e-commerce and physical logistics — Copia and KOKO both shut entirely, and both were capital-intensive, last-mile, low-margin models. That maps neatly onto Thursday’s point that the capital which flows now flows toward collateral, and it argues for caution on operationally heavy, thin-margin models regardless of how good the growth story reads. Third — and this is where it rhymes with today’s Claude-in-Slack thread — the fact that AI is not yet named does not mean it is not already reshaping the org quietly, inside the “restructuring” language; the honest read is that the AI colleague is arriving before the headcount admits it left. The forward question: when we press a portfolio company on productivity, the useful probe is not “have you cut heads to AI” but “where has AI let you grow revenue without growing headcount” — the same efficiency, stated as leverage rather than loss.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Force the 2×2 — make the model collapse a messy decision onto two axes

When a decision has too many moving parts to hold in your head — a deal, a portfolio choice, a hiring call — the instinct is to write a long list of pros and cons and drown in it. A sharper move is to ask the model to force the decision onto a two-by-two: name the two variables that actually matter most, plot the options against them, and tell you which quadrant each one lands in. The discipline is in the constraint. Reducing a decision to two axes forces you (and the model) to decide what genuinely drives the outcome and what is noise — and it usually exposes that two options you were agonising over sit in the same quadrant, while the real choice is one you had not framed.

A worked example

On a stack of opportunities, prompt: “Take these five deals. Choose the two variables that most determine whether each one succeeds for a growth-equity investor — not the obvious ones, the ones that actually decide it. Draw a 2×2 with those axes, place each deal in a quadrant, and name the quadrant that is a trap even though it looks attractive. Then tell me which single variable, if I could get certainty on it, would most change the map. UK English, no preamble.” The value is not the grid itself — it is watching what the model chooses as the two axes. If its axes are wrong, you have just learned that your team is optimising for the wrong two things; if they are right, you have a one-glance map you can put in front of the IC. Then flip it: ask it to redraw the 2×2 with the two axes you think matter, and argue which framing is better.
