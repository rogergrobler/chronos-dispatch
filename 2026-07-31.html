Africa · Venture capital · Concentration Analysis Chart of the day
TechCabal's "Follow the Money" column, drawing on TC Insights' State of Tech in Africa data, reports that African startups raised $1.44bn in H1 2026 — a shade above the $1.42bn of H1 2025 — but across only 146 disclosed deals, down from 252 a year earlier. The total held up largely because of a handful of very large rounds (Spiro, the pan-African electric-motorbike maker, alone took $215m plus a further $55m in June). Debt did heavy lifting: of the six-month total, roughly $818m was equity, $614m debt and $9m grants, with capital tilting toward asset-backed businesses — EVs, solar — that can borrow against physical collateral. M&A hit a record 63 deals (versus 33 a year earlier), and the ecosystem tracked 1,000+ layoffs (up from 698), with companies increasingly naming AI as the reason.

Africa's venture money didn't shrink — it concentrated: the same capital, backing 40% fewer founders

TechCabal · Follow the Money · Temitayo Jaiyeola · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the cheerful headline is that funding is flat-to-up in a hard global year, which reads as resilience. The uncomfortable one is underneath it — the number of companies sharing that money fell by more than a third in twelve months, and the mix shifted from equity toward debt and from many small bets toward a few large, asset-heavy ones. This is not a funding winter so much as a funding narrowing: capital is still here, but it is backing fewer, later, safer names — and the base of first cheques that feeds the whole pipeline is quietly hollowing out.

Flat dollars, a third fewer cheques — the story of 2026 is concentration, not contraction
TechCabal — “African venture capital is backing fewer founders than ever”

The structural point is that an ecosystem's health is not measured only by dollars raised but by the width of the funnel — how many first cheques get written, because those are what become the Series A and the exit three years out. A market can post a flat headline and still be quietly narrowing if the same money is landing in fewer, bigger, later rounds. That is what the 252-to-146 collapse says: the mega-deal (Spiro at $270m for the half) flatters the total, debt is doing the work equity used to, and the long tail of small, risky, first-time founders — the part of the base that yesterday's "Diversity Dividend" card showed thinning unevenly — is where the deals are disappearing.

A flat funding total can hide a shrinking funnel. The number that matters is not the dollars raised — it is how many founders got a first cheque, and that fell by a third in a year.

The threads: to today's item 2 — the record 63 M&A deals are the other side of this coin, because when fresh equity is scarce, selling to an incumbent replaces raising the next round, and South Africa's banks are stepping into exactly that gap. To today's item 5 — GoLemon is what the narrowing looks like from the floor: real demand, a profitable order, and still no bridge cheque. To Issue 055's "Diversity Dividend" card — the thinning is not neutral; it cuts the least-connected founders first. The sceptic's ledger: "disclosed deals" undercount the quiet, undisclosed pre-seed layer, so the funnel may be wider than 146 suggests; debt rising over equity is arguably a sign of maturity, not distress, for asset-backed models; and one $270m outlier distorts any half-year read. The forward marker: whether H2 broadens the deal count back out or whether 2026 sets a new, lower baseline for how many African founders get funded at all — the single number worth watching more than the dollar total.

South Africa · Fintech M&A · Venture exits Analysis Wouldn't have seen otherwise
New research from the SA SME Fund, Endeavor South Africa and industry body SAVCA finds that domestic M&A — led by the banks — has become a major exit route for venture-backed South African tech, alongside the international deals that once dominated. The named deals: Nedbank bought payments fintech iKhokha (R1.65bn); Capitec acquired WalletDoc; TymeBank took SME lender Retail Capital; Lesaka Technologies absorbed payments group Adumo. An analysis of 226 realised exits reported by local fund managers from 2009–2026 found capital-weighted returns of 2.01–2.45x invested capital — in line with mature US, UK and European venture — on roughly R16bn invested across 1,100+ companies, with ~60% of exited investments making money. A companion study of 18 hand-picked winners showed a median 54% gross IRR and 3.5x. Optasia's R23.5bn JSE listing and Mastercard's pending ~R30bn purchase of BVNK show the public-market and international routes remain open.

South Africa's banks have become the exit ramp for its fintechs — and the return data finally clears 2x

TechCentral · Duncan McLeod · Mon 27 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the near-term story is that South African founders no longer have to wait for a foreign buyer — the local banks, which for a decade tried to build everything in-house, are now the ones knocking, because AI-native challengers make in-house too slow and buying is cheaper than building. The deeper story, aimed squarely at the pension funds this research is written to persuade, is that SA venture is at last producing the evidence it was always accused of lacking: realised, capital-weighted returns above 2x across 226 exits, and a Regulation 28 allowance to put 15% into private capital that sits almost entirely unused.

TechCentral — “South Africa's banks have become the exit ramp for its fintechs”

The structural read is that an exit market is infrastructure for a venture ecosystem the same way a ratings agency is for a debt market — without a credible way out, no amount of early capital compounds. For most of the past decade South Africa had exactly one exit door, and it was offshore. What the SA SME Fund / Endeavor / SAVCA work documents is a second door opening at home, and it is the incumbents walking through it: Retail Capital → TymeBank, WalletDoc → Capitec, iKhokha → Nedbank, Adumo → Lesaka. Karl Westvig, who built Retail Capital and sold it, puts the mechanism plainly — the banks used to buy tiny stakes "to learn the model and did it in-house anyway," and that is breaking down because what they build is now easier to clone by a start-up with no legacy stack, so they "partner, participate or buy." That is the moat question of the whole page, answered from the incumbent's side.

For a decade a South African founder had one exit — a foreign buyer. Now the bank down the road is the buyer, and the return data finally reads like a real asset class.

The threads: to today's item 1 — the record M&A wave is the direct consequence of the funding narrowing, because a scarce next round makes a trade sale the rational exit, and this is where those 63 deals are landing. To Chronos directly — Optasia's R23.5bn JSE listing is cited as the proof that African fintech can exit at scale on a public market, and the piece names the very portfolio-adjacent dynamics we live inside; TymeBank appears as an acquirer, not a target. The sceptic's ledger: the eye-catching return figures are gross — the researchers admitted at the briefing they had not netted out fees and carry, and the 18-company 54% IRR study is an explicitly hand-picked winners' sample, not the asset class; the whole exercise has a fundraising purpose (a new R2bn fund), so the numbers are marketing as much as measurement. The forward marker: whether Regulation 28's unused 15% actually starts flowing to private capital now that there is a returns story to point at — the single unlock that would change the size of the South African venture base.

Kenya · AI governance · Regulation Briefing Tests a thesis
Kenya's ICT ministry has published a draft Kenya Artificial Intelligence and Other Emerging Technologies Policy (226 pages) that would require organisations to tell people when they are interacting directly with an AI system rather than a human, and to disclose when an automated system "makes or materially shapes" a decision affecting someone's rights, services or legal interests, or when public content has been AI-generated or substantially altered. If adopted, it would touch banks using AI in credit decisions, insurers processing claims, telcos running chatbots, employers screening applicants and hospitals using clinical decision support. The draft also insists AI be "explainable, auditable, and subject to human oversight," that humans retain "meaningful control" over high-risk systems, and proposes a national AI council and AI office. It mirrors the EU AI Act's transparency duties and forms part of Kenya's bid to become a regional AI-governance hub — it backed May's Africa Forward Declaration and won the right to host the 2027 REAIM summit.

Kenya moves to make companies say when an algorithm — not a person — is deciding for you

TechCabal · Artificial Intelligence · Adonijah Ndege · Wed 29 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the surface story is a consumer-protection rule — a right to be told when you are talking to a bot or when a model has shaped a decision about you. The more consequential story for anyone deploying AI in African financial services is the compliance surface it creates: "explainable, auditable, human-in-the-loop" is easy to write into a policy and expensive to retrofit into a credit engine or a claims model that was built for speed, not for showing its work. Kenya is trying to move from promoting AI adoption to governing it — and to set the continent's template while doing so.

TechCabal — “Kenyan companies must disclose when people interact with AI under new policy”

The structural read is that transparency obligations are a tax on opacity, and they land hardest on exactly the AI deployments that are currently cheapest — the silent chatbot, the black-box credit score, the automated claims triage that no one is told about. A disclosure regime does not ban any of that; it forces it into the light, and "auditable and explainable" quietly reprices the build. For a bank or insurer, the winning architecture stops being the most accurate model and becomes the most defensible one — the system that can show its reasoning, log its decisions and route the hard cases to a human. That is a real design constraint, and it is the one the market has been able to ignore while AI stayed a back-office efficiency play.

"Individuals are entitled to know when an AI system is acting on them." The cheapest AI deployment is the silent one — and Kenya is proposing to make silence illegal.

The threads: to today's item 4 — Luno is cutting staff because automation now runs the exchange, which is precisely the kind of decision-making Kenya wants surfaced; the efficiency and the disclosure duty are two ends of the same shift. To Issue 055's Nairobi-EV card (the AI that repossesses a car on a missed payment) — that is the archetypal "automated system that materially affects your rights," and under this draft the driver would have to be told an algorithm, not a person, flipped the switch. To Issue 055's Madagascar and ATLAS Umoja "sovereignty" cards — this is the governance version of the same posture: own the rulebook, not just the compute. The sceptic's ledger: it is a draft policy, not law, with no penalties, no threshold for when a chatbot triggers notice, and no definition of "materially shaped," so the teeth are all in implementing regulations that do not yet exist; disclosure regimes are also easy to satisfy with a boilerplate banner that changes nothing. The forward marker: whether the final version carries real enforcement and a workable materiality test, or lands as a label everyone clicks past — and whether other African regulators copy Kenya's text, which is the outcome Nairobi is actually playing for.

Pan-Africa · Crypto · AI & automation Analysis Longitudinal thread
Luno — the crypto exchange founded in South Africa in 2013, now owned by US-based Digital Currency Group — is cutting 20% of its global headcount and reorganising into three units, CEO James Lanigan said on 28 July. It will not disclose numbers; South African staff are affected and are being consulted under s189 of the Labour Relations Act. Luno attributed the cuts to two things: a cyclical downturn in retail crypto trading, and its own investment in automation, saying it is "integrating and developing tools that are rapidly changing the resource model required to run the business." It is the second deep cut in three-and-a-half years (35% in January 2023). The new structure: (1) a retail exchange plus a "crypto-as-a-service" API that lets banks and fintechs offer crypto under their own brand while Luno supplies liquidity, custody and compliance (template: Discovery Bank); (2) stablecoins and wallet infrastructure, built around Zaru, its rand-backed stablecoin, with partners including EasyEquities, Lesaka, Sanlam and Standard Bank; (3) global cross-border settlements. Luno is also exiting non-core markets by 1 September 2026.

Luno cuts a fifth of its staff, names automation as a reason — and turns itself into a rail banks can rent

TechCentral · Cryptocurrencies · Fanie van Rooyen · Tue 28 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the headline is a crypto-cycle retrenchment, the second in three years, and easy to file as "crypto winter, again." The more important fact is what Luno is becoming as it shrinks — not a smaller retail app but a three-part platform, deliberately turning its custody, liquidity, compliance and a regulated rand stablecoin into infrastructure that banks and fintechs rent under their own brands. The layoffs and the pivot are the same move: automation lowers the cost of running the rails, and the strategy shifts from owning the customer to owning the plumbing everyone else's customers cross.

Fewer people, more surface — the exchange is quietly becoming the infrastructure other brands run on
TechCentral — “Luno cutting a fifth of its workforce — South Africa not spared”

The structural read is that Luno is doing openly what a lot of financial-services businesses are about to do quietly: using automation to break the link between headcount and scale, and then re-pointing the freed capacity at infrastructure rather than retail. The three-unit design is the tell. Crypto-as-a-service, a regulated rand stablecoin and a cross-border settlement network are all "own the rail" products — Luno keeps the custody, liquidity and compliance and lets Discovery Bank, EasyEquities or Standard Bank put their brand on the front. It is the same instinct that ran through the whole of last week's page (own the rating, own the payment flow), now applied to crypto plumbing, and the automation story is what makes the economics work: you cannot run a low-margin infrastructure business on a retail-sized cost base.

The layoffs and the pivot are one decision. Automation lets the exchange run on fewer people; the platform strategy lets it sell those rails to everyone else's bank.

The threads: to today's item 3 — Luno is the live example of the automation Kenya wants disclosed; the tools "changing the resource model" are the same class of system a customer would, under Nairobi's draft, be entitled to know about. To Issue 054's Absa Kenya card (71% of processes automated after a $31m spend, ten points off cost-to-income) — there the automation dividend paid out as a better ratio; here it pays out as a smaller workforce, the same force with the human cost on the other side of the ledger. To Issue 055's Nairobi-EV automation card — efficiency as the through-line of the fortnight. The sceptic's ledger: Luno has cried "cycle" before (35% in 2023) and retail crypto is genuinely volatile, so "automation" may be as much a palatable framing as a cause; a rand stablecoin and a crypto-as-a-service API are crowded, capital-intensive bets with no guarantee the bank partners materialise; and DCG ownership means the strategy is set offshore. The forward marker: whether the platform units actually sign the bank partners the pitch implies — a live read on whether African incumbents choose to rent crypto rails rather than build or avoid them.

Nigeria · Q-commerce · Unit economics Briefing Longitudinal thread
GoLemon, the Lagos grocery-delivery startup founded in 2024 by four former senior Paystack employees, is shutting down after failing to raise the funding needed for its next phase. It stopped taking orders in July and closes support channels on Sunday, 2 August. Over 28 months it delivered tens of thousands of orders (average basket ~₦43,700 / ~$32) and moved more than ₦2bn of groceries. Its own account of why is unusually precise: individual orders were profitable after direct costs, but it never reached the order density needed for those contributions to cover the fixed costs of a vertically integrated supply chain — warehouses, staff, power, its own app and delivery. Food inflation and a weaker naira raised input costs against a low-price promise; a December 2025 fulfilment tie-up with Chowdeck widened reach but did not close the gap. Jumia and Bolt had both exited African grocery before GoLemon launched, citing the same weak unit economics.

GoLemon shuts down — profitable per order, fatal at the fixed-cost line, and out of runway in a thinning base

TechCabal · News · Muktar Oladunmade · Wed 29 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: the obvious story is another African e-grocery casualty in a category that has already swallowed Jumia and Bolt. The more useful one is the diagnosis GoLemon itself offers — the order was profitable; the business was not, because vertical integration front-loads fixed costs that only very high, very dense order volumes can absorb, and the capital to buy time to reach that density evaporated. It is a clean case study in the difference between unit economics and business economics, and a direct read-through to what the funding narrowing (item 1) does to capital-hungry consumer models.

TechCabal — “Grocery delivery startup GoLemon shuts down after failing to raise fresh capital”

The structural read is that GoLemon chose the hardest possible shape — own the farms-to-kitchen chain end to end (bulk buying, warehouses, quality control, its own app, its own drivers) in order to control price and quality — and that choice is precisely what killed it. Owning every step converts other people's variable costs into your fixed costs, which is a magnificent moat if you reach scale and a millstone if you do not. Management's own post-mortem is refreshingly honest: none of the in-house builds was individually wrong, but doing them all "too fast," before order density arrived, left a cost base that a profitable-per-order business still could not cover. In a category where two far larger players had already proved the unit economics brutal, the margin for financing error was near zero — and the financing error is what happened.

The order made money. The business did not. Vertical integration turns everyone else's variable costs into your fixed ones — a moat at scale, a millstone before it.

The threads: to today's item 1 — GoLemon is the funnel narrowing made concrete. Its founders had strong reputations and there was investor interest, but "the timelines required to complete an investment did not align with the runway we had left" — exactly the fate of capital-hungry consumer bets when deal counts halve and money moves to asset-backed, later-stage names. To today's item 2 (inverted) — where SA fintechs found a bank to buy them, GoLemon held talks on "strategic alternatives" and none closed; the exit ramp that is opening for infrastructure fintech is not open for a cash-burning grocery operation. The sceptic's ledger: GoLemon declined to disclose how much it raised, sought, or spent on power and payroll, so the "profitable order" claim cannot be independently checked; a December pivot to fulfilling through Chowdeck suggests the owned-delivery thesis was already wobbling; and "no single reason investors passed" can be founder-framing for a model the market had simply judged. The forward marker: whether Nigerian grocery migrates decisively to the asset-light marketplace model (list, don't own) that GoLemon's failure argues for — and whether any investor funds the heavy version again after a third proof it does not work.

Nigeria · Telecoms · Privatisation & M&A Briefing Wouldn't have seen otherwise
The Asset Management Corporation of Nigeria (AMCON), which took full management control of ntel — the successor to former state monopoly NITEL — in 2024, announced on Monday it has begun divesting its 55% stake. A buyer would inherit one of West Africa's largest portfolios of telecom spectrum (valuable 900MHz and 1800MHz bands), fibre, access to the SAT-3 submarine cable and extensive legacy real estate — plus the capital-intensive job of turning it into a modern digital-infrastructure company. Over two years AMCON restructured ntel away from the crowded retail mobile market into three units: Beam (enterprise connectivity), Titan (towers and colocation) and Eden (monetising real estate). The process is early — no bidders yet, and AMCON is still securing regulatory approvals. Under new NCC/CAC rules introduced on 18 June 2026, any acquisition of 10%+ of a licensed telco needs prior NCC approval, with review of spectrum fees, levies and competition/spectrum-concentration effects.

Nigeria puts its old telecom monopoly up for sale — a bet on infrastructure, not subscribers, and a test of investor appetite

TechCabal · Analysis / News · Frank Eleanya · Wed 29 Jul 2026

Two lines of read: on the surface this is a routine state divestment — a bad-bank agency selling a distressed asset it was never meant to hold long term. Underneath, it is a genuine test of whether investors will fund a telecom turnaround built on spectrum, fibre and towers rather than on winning mobile subscribers from MTN, Airtel and Glo. AMCON has done the unglamorous work — stabilise, restructure debt, protect the spectrum, split the business into three infrastructure verticals — and now needs a long-term investor with deep pockets, into a tougher acquisition regime that gives the regulator a veto over who gets the keys.

TechCabal — “Nigeria tests investor appetite with sale of former telecom monopoly ntel”

The structural read is that ntel's value is not a customer base — it barely has one — but a stock of scarce, expensive-to-replicate infrastructure: prime spectrum, fibre, a submarine-cable landing and real estate. AMCON's two-year restructuring reframed the asset around that reality, splitting it into Beam (enterprise), Titan (towers) and Eden (real estate), which is really an argument that the business should be valued as three infrastructure annuities rather than one failed mobile operator. CEO Soji Maurice-Diya's pitch — "a smarter, savvy investor will simply double down" on the three-pillar strategy, and 55% "more than gives" operational control — is the classic distressed-infrastructure story: the hard part (stabilisation) is done, the upside needs capital. For a PE reader it is a recognisable shape: buy the bones, fund the rebuild, monetise the underused assets.

Nobody is buying ntel for its subscribers. They would be buying spectrum, fibre and a cable landing — the scarce infrastructure a rival would find hard and expensive to replicate.

The threads: to today's item 2 — this is the same "control changes hands to someone who will actually invest" logic as the SA bank-buys-fintech wave, but at the heavy-infrastructure end and with the state, not a startup, as the seller. To Issue 055's S&P–Agusto card — both are about buying the scarce layer everything else depends on (there the credit verdict, here the spectrum and fibre), the "own the rails" thesis in its most literal form. To Issue 054's Airtel-to-London and Angola-lists-Unitel items — another African telecom asset testing where and how it can be financed, this time onshore. The sceptic's ledger: there are no bidders yet and AMCON is "still going through the regulatory stage," so this is an intention, not a deal; the new NCC 10% rule means any buyer faces a regulatory review that could add months and deter incumbents (the obvious deep-pocketed bidders) on competition grounds; and AMCON is selling only its 55%, so a buyer gets operational control without clean ownership, with minority holders who have not agreed to sell. The forward marker: whether a credible strategic investor with real working capital actually emerges — because, as Maurice-Diya concedes, changing the ownership structure means nothing unless whoever buys it can fund the build.

Today's Tip · AI craft for the partners

Stop typing — talk it out, then let Claude turn the ramble into structure

You think faster than you type, and you certainly think faster in the car than at a keyboard. The highest-leverage habit is to stop treating writing as something you do sitting down, and start treating your own voice as the raw input. Dictate — into your phone, a voice note, a transcription app — for two or three unedited minutes: everything you know about the situation, in whatever order it falls out, with the tangents and the second thoughts left in. Then hand the transcript to Claude with a single instruction: "This is me thinking out loud. Pull out the actual argument, drop the repetition, and give me a tight structure — don't add anything I didn't say." The model is extraordinarily good at finding the spine inside a messy monologue, which is the one thing that is genuinely hard to do to your own talking. You keep the thinking; it does the sorting. For a sprinter, this collapses the slowest part of the job — getting a first shape onto the page — into the time it takes to walk to the next meeting.

A worked example

Before a board call, record yourself for three minutes: "Okay, on the portfolio company — the number I care about is the deal count, not the total, and here's why, and also there's this thing about the exit ramp, and I keep coming back to the fixed-cost point…" Then: "Turn that into three crisp bullets for the board, in my order of priority, and flag the one place I contradicted myself." What comes back is your agenda — found, not written. You spoke it in three minutes; editing it takes two.
